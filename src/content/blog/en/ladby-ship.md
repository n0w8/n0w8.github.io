A Viking ship usually leaves at least some wood behind, provided the ground cooperates - at Oseberg and Gokstad, wet blue clay kept entire hulls intact for over a thousand years. At Ladby, on the Danish island of Funen, that didn't happen. The soil near Kerteminde broke down the oak almost completely, and what archaeologists found in 1935 wasn't a ship anymore, but its ghost: a precise imprint in the earth, traced by roughly 2,000 rusted iron rivets that once held the planks together. The **Ladby ship** remains, to this day, the only Viking Age ship burial discovered within the borders of modern Denmark - and precisely because so little of it survives in the conventional sense, it tells a very different story about archaeology, loss, and what soil preserves across the centuries.

## A Ship Made of Rust, Not Wood

What sets the Ladby ship apart from its more famous Norwegian relatives is exactly this absence of substance. Where Oseberg and Gokstad yielded planks, frames, and carved ornamentation, excavators at Ladby found mainly the iron fittings, sitting exactly where the surrounding wood had once rotted away. Each rivet marked the spot where a plank edge had once been - and from the pattern of thousands of these tiny metal points scattered through the sand, archaeologists were able to reconstruct the entire shape of the hull.

This method of reconstruction was unusually demanding for the excavation techniques of the time. Instead of lifting a ship plank by plank, archaeologists essentially had to read a three-dimensional puzzle of rust traces in the sand, without the original timber to guide them. The result is nevertheless remarkably precise: the ship measured roughly 21.5 to 22 meters long and about 3 meters wide, with room for 16 pairs of oars, meaning 32 rowers, and a draft of just 50 centimeters. With a length-to-beam ratio of about 7 to 1 it was a fast, agile warship, not merely a ceremonial showpiece.

Ladby isn't entirely alone in this: four years later, excavators at Sutton Hoo in England uncovered another ship that survived only as an imprint with rows of rivets. One detail does set Ladby apart, though: the dragon's mane at the bow. The carved head is gone, but a row of delicate iron spirals survives and still shows where it once sat. Those puzzling "iron spikes" are what set the whole discovery in motion.

## Discovery at Kerteminde in 1935

It started with a farmer, not a scholar. **Erik Eriksen** of Nymarksgård farm turned up a skull in his field at Ladby in 1933, then in autumn 1934 hit large stones and strange iron spikes in the mound, which ploughing had already flattened. He called in pharmacist and amateur archaeologist **Poul Helweg Mikkelsen**, who began digging in northeastern Funen and soon reached the first rivets. The **National Museum of Denmark** stepped in, and the drawings by Mikkelsen and conservator **Gustav Rosenberg** remain the main source on the find. When rivets emerged in long rows in early 1935, the conclusion was clear: this was a ship, the first of its kind found in Denmark. The excavation closed in 1936.

The dating of the grave rests largely on stylistic features of the surviving metal grave goods, in particular a gilded bronze link from a dog harness, decorated with animal ornament in the so-called Jelling style. That places the burial in the first half of the 10th century, between 900 and 950 AD - making the Ladby ship clearly younger than the Oseberg ship, whose burial is dated to 834, and a little younger than the [Gokstad ship](/en/blog/gokstad-ship/).

Responsibility for the site stayed with the National Museum, which still owns the mound and ship, until 1994. Day-to-day operation then passed to the Museums of Eastern Funen.

Geographically, the location is no accident: Kerteminde sits on a fjord with direct access to the Great Belt, one of the most important waterways in Viking Age Denmark, connecting Funen and Zealand. A man who could afford a ship burial on this scale was, in all likelihood, someone whose wealth and status were closely tied to control over exactly such sea routes - even though his name and precise role are lost to us for good.

## Eleven Horses, Dogs, and a Vanished Man

Despite the missing wood, the Ladby grave is anything but empty. Inside the ship, archaeologists found the bones of **eleven horses**, built like modern Nordic ponies at about 1.25 meters at the withers, and at least **four dogs**, apparently buried as companions for the final journey. The gilded bronze piece from a dog harness lay up in the bow and ranks among the most elaborate single finds. The burial chamber itself was built of timber and stood between the stern and the mast.

In the bow of the ship lay an **iron stock anchor** that originally weighed about 20 kilograms, still attached to its chain, and both are still visible on site today.

![Iron anchor with a coiled chain in the bow of the Ladby ship](/images/ladby-ship-3.jpg)
*The stock anchor and iron chain in the bow of the Ladby ship. Photo: Malene Thyssen, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

Outside the gunwale lay 45 arrowheads with traces of their shafts. Near the burial chamber, excavators found some 600 fragments that speak of a lavish burial: a belt buckle, a silver plate, a bronze bowl, a gaming board, a wooden bucket. There were also remains of a gold-threaded Byzantine silk tunic, plus fur and feathers, possibly from the dead man's bedding. Apart from a shield boss, weapons are missing, which is unusual and probably goes back to the later break-in.

The anchor is more than just another accessory. Our reading: an anchor left in its place in the bow with its chain intact, rather than stowed elsewhere as a separate grave good, fits well with the idea of a ship that was, in a sense, ready to set sail - ready to genuinely carry its owner into the afterlife, not just to represent him there symbolically.

> Who exactly was buried in the Ladby ship can no longer be said - the grave was opened and plundered so thoroughly, already in the Viking Age, that only a handful of small bone fragments remain of the man himself.

This near-total absence of human remains has produced two competing explanations among archaeologists. One theory holds that the body was deliberately removed after the original burial and reinterred elsewhere - possibly in consecrated Christian ground - a practice known as "translation." The other, more politically charged theory links the plundering to the power struggles around **Harald Bluetooth** and his son **Sweyn Forkbeard**: the body was removed and the grave goods hacked into hundreds of pieces, which may have been a show of force aimed at the prestige of the dead man's heirs. A clue that this happened not long after the burial is the shield boss, already rusted enough to be snapped in two. Neither theory is proven, but both fit the picture of a man of considerable, if unknown, rank, perhaps a local lord.

## The Ladby Ship Compared: What Actually Survived?

A direct comparison with the two great Norwegian ship burials shows just how unusual the preservation conditions at Ladby really were:

| Ship Burial | Discovered | Length | Soil Conditions | What Survived |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ladby | 1935, Kerteminde (DK) | c. 21.5 to 22 m | permeable soil, no clay seal | iron rivets and imprint, practically no wood |
| Oseberg | 1904, Tønsberg (NO) | 21.58 m | airtight blue clay | nearly complete wooden hull, rich carvings |
| Gokstad | 1880, Sandefjord (NO) | 23.24 m | airtight blue clay | largely preserved wooden hull |

The difference isn't really about the ship itself, which was comparable in size to the ships found at [Oseberg](/en/blog/oseberg-ship/) and Gokstad, but mostly about the ground it spent a thousand years in. Blue clay keeps oxygen out and preserves organic material; in permeable soil, wood can rot away, leaving mainly the metal fittings and their imprint behind. That makes Ladby something of a textbook case for how much the survival of an archaeological find depends on geological chance rather than on the find's original importance.

## The Museum Built Over the Grave

Today, the **Viking Museum Ladby** (Vikingemuseet Ladby) stands at the original grave site, with the ship under the burial mound beneath a concrete vault from 1937. It allows visitors to view the ship's imprint and iron rivets, the anchor and its chain, and the remains of the animal burials exactly where they were excavated. That is partly down to tight budgets: the National Museum decided against bringing the find to Copenhagen because of the cost, and Mikkelsen offered to pay for the vault himself. So the grave can be visited exactly where it was found. Since 2007 an extension has shown original finds and a reconstruction of the burial, and since 2011 the museum has been building a full-size replica, the "Ladbydragen".

![Wooden replica of the Ladby ship on the slipway at the Viking Museum Ladby](/images/ladby-ship-4.jpg)
*The replica under construction at the Viking Museum Ladby. Photo: Toxophilus, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

This solution - a structure built directly over the find instead of a transfer to a distant national museum - sets Ladby apart from Oseberg and Gokstad, and it makes a visit feel unusually immediate: you are standing inside the burial mound itself, not in front of a model of it. Readers curious about the broader range of Viking Age burial customs can find more context in our article on [Viking ship burials](/en/blog/viking-burial-ship/).

For visitors, that means something concrete: instead of a restored wooden hull like the ones in Oslo, Ladby shows the imprint in the earth with exposed iron rivets left in their original positions, along with the animal bones on the floor of the ship. It's a far quieter, more understated kind of presentation than Oseberg or Gokstad offer - but that very restraint communicates, very directly, just how much a find like this depends on soil, time, and chance, rather than on the material a ship happened to be built from.

## Why the Ladby Ship Still Matters

In its own understated way, the Ladby ship proves something the grander Norwegian finds cannot: even when little remains of a ship burial but rust and an imprint in the earth, it can still yield an enormous amount of knowledge - about shipbuilding, burial ritual, animal sacrifice, and perhaps even the political power struggles of a society in the process of converting to Christianity. The very emptiness of the burial chamber, from which the actual occupant vanished without a trace, is what makes Ladby one of Denmark's most puzzling and, at the same time, most instructive Viking Age finds.

Our take: where Oseberg and Gokstad show what a Viking ship actually was, Ladby shows what's left of a Viking ship once time and soil have run their own, unforgiving archaeology on it - and that is exactly what makes this find one of the most striking examples of how much history can still be read from something that has, in every material sense, already disappeared.
