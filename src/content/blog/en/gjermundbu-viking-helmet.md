The **Gjermundbu helmet** is the only near-complete Viking helmet ever found in Scandinavia: a rounded iron cap with a spectacle-shaped guard for the eyes and nose, recovered in 1943 from a 10th-century warrior's grave at Gjermundbu farm in Ringerike, Norway. Picture a Viking, and the helmet in that image almost certainly traces back to this one battered, corroded piece of iron, which somehow ended up defining the look of an entire era. It was found, of all times, in the middle of the Second World War, by a farmer's son levelling ground on his family's land. How this one helmet survived, and why hundreds of thousands of other warriors left us nothing at all, is one of the great detective stories of Viking archaeology.

## Where the Gjermundbu Helmet Was Found: A Wartime Spring

On 29 March 1943, **Gunnar Gjermundbo**, son of the farmer Lars Gjermundbo, was levelling ground on the family farm of Gjermundbu, near Haugsbygd in the Ringerike district northwest of Oslo, when he struck old iron in a large earthen mound. Word soon reached the University of Oslo's collection of antiquities. Norway was under German occupation at the time, and large research campaigns were out of the question. Even so, archaeologists **Sverre Marstrander** and **Charlotte Blindheim** travelled to the site that same spring to excavate it properly.

What they uncovered exceeded every expectation. The mound, first measured at about 25 meters and later at about 29 meters long, held two Viking Age burials. The richer of the two, known today simply as Grave I, turned out to be one of the most significant warrior graves ever discovered in Norway. It was a cremation burial: the dead man had been burned together with his equipment, and the remains were then laid in the mound. The grave is dated to the second half of the 10th century, somewhere between roughly 950 and 1000 AD.

## What Lay in the Grave at Gjermundbu

The contents of the grave leave no doubt that the man buried here held serious rank and wealth. Alongside the famous helmet, the archaeologists found a **mail shirt**, weapons including a sword, spears and axes, several shield bosses, spurs and five or six bridle bits, plus gaming pieces and dice - a combination the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo interprets as the grave of a mounted warrior. The riding equipment is the telling detail: this was not an ordinary foot soldier, but a member of the military elite of his day.

The mail shirt deserves almost as much attention as the helmet itself. Ring mail was staggeringly expensive in the Viking Age; making a single shirt meant riveting thousands of individual iron rings over many weeks of work. Almost no Scandinavian examples survive - the Gjermundbu byrnie is by far the most important mail find of the Norwegian Viking Age, and it is displayed today right beside the helmet.

## What Did Viking Helmets Look Like? The Gjermundbu Design

The helmet did not come out of the ground as a gleaming showpiece. It emerged in nine fragments, which conservators painstakingly reassembled into the shape everyone now recognizes: a rounded iron cap built from four plates, held together by riveted iron bands. A sturdy ridge runs over the crown and ends in a small spike - a detail missing from most modern replicas.

Its most striking feature, though, is the so-called **spectacle guard**: a curved piece of iron that wraps around the wearer's eyes and nose and gives the helmet its unmistakable, almost mask-like face. The design stands in a long Nordic tradition - the lavish helmets of the Vendel period, the centuries immediately before the Viking Age, already used comparable face protection. Next to those gilded predecessors, the Gjermundbu helmet looks almost austere: no gold foil, no figural friezes, no display. It is a working piece of war gear.

Whether a curtain of mail once hung from the lower rim to protect the neck is debated; the surviving material does not settle the question. What is certain is that whoever wore this helmet spent more on protecting his head than most of his contemporaries could have afforded in a lifetime.

## Why Is There Only One Viking Helmet From Scandinavia?

Here lies the real riddle of the find. The Viking Age lasted some 250 years, its armies numbered in the tens of thousands, and yet the Scandinavian world has produced exactly one near-complete helmet. Researchers debate two main explanations, and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first: iron helmets were simply rare. Iron was costly, forging a helmet was slow, skilled work, and most fighters probably made do with leather caps or no head protection at all. The second explanation looks at what happened after a helmet was made: iron rots in the ground, and a helmet was far too valuable to throw into a grave lightly. Helmets were inherited, repaired, reworked and passed on until they were quite literally used up. That the lord of Gjermundbu was allowed to take his helmet onto the pyre was an extraordinary exception - and that exception is the only reason we have a complete example to look at today.

The grave still has surprises. According to a re-examination of the find by the archaeologists Frans-Arne Stylegar and Ragnar Løken Børsheim, reported by ScienceNorway in 2022, the helmet was deliberately destroyed in connection with the funeral, pierced with the spears that lay in the grave. Stylegar also suggested that the dead man may have served a ruler of Kievan Rus in the east, because the helmet, the mail and a sledge in the grave recall elite burials there. The helmets in those eastern graves are of different types than the one from Gjermundbu, though, so the idea remains a hypothesis.

> One helmet now has to stand in for hundreds of thousands of warriors - few objects in archaeology carry so much weight on so little iron.

Our editorial take: that is also the trap. Because the Gjermundbu helmet has no rival, it gets promoted to the universal standard, the template for every Viking head ever drawn. Strictly speaking, it is just one thing: the helmet of a single, exceptionally wealthy mounted warrior from Ringerike. What an average fighter wore on his head is something we genuinely do not know - and honest archaeology should name that gap instead of papering over it with a single find.

### Viking Age Helmet Finds Compared

A look at the handful of other known helmet remains shows just how thin the evidence really is:

| Find | Location | Condition | Dating |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gjermundbu | Ringerike, Norway | near-complete, reconstructed from fragments | late 10th century |
| Yarm | Yorkshire, England | largely complete, Viking Age attribution debated since 2020 | ca. 10th century |
| Tjele | Jutland, Denmark | fragment of an eye and nose guard | 10th century |
| Lokrume | Gotland, Sweden | fragment of a decorated brow band | 10th century |

The Yarm helmet, found by workmen digging pipe trenches in Yarm in the 1950s, sat largely ignored for decades. A study led by Chris Caple of Durham University, published in *Medieval Archaeology* in 2020, assessed it as probably an Anglo-Scandinavian piece of the 10th century. If that interpretation holds, the Gjermundbu helmet would at least gain a distant cousin - though its status as the only near-complete helmet from the Scandinavian heartland would remain untouched.

## Myth and Fact: About Those Horns

No article on Viking helmets can dodge popular culture's favorite mistake, so here it is in brief:

- **Myth:** Vikings wore horned helmets into battle.
- **Fact:** Not a single Viking Age helmet find, Gjermundbu included, carries horns or any fittings for them. The image largely comes from the 19th century, when costume designers like Carl Emil Doepler put the Romantic era's Norse fantasies on stage for Wagner's Ring cycle in 1876.
- **Fact:** Horned figures do appear in Scandinavian imagery, most likely in ritual contexts - just never as the combat equipment of historical Vikings.

In a shield wall, horns would also have been a liability: they give enemy weapons something to catch, guiding blows toward the skull instead of letting them glance off. ## Where the Helmet Stands Today

The Gjermundbu helmet belongs to the **Museum of Cultural History** (Kulturhistorisk Museum) of the University of Oslo and is on show at the university's Historical Museum in central Oslo, together with the mail shirt and other finds from the grave, dramatically lit in a case of its own. It is expected to move to the university's new Museum of the Viking Age at Bygdøy once that opens - a remarkable career for an object that arrived in 1943 as a heap of rusted fragments from a field in Ringerike.

## FAQ

### Is the Gjermundbu helmet the only Viking helmet ever found?

It is the only near-complete Viking Age helmet from Scandinavia. The only other relatively complete example is the Yarm helmet from northern England, dated to the 10th century in a 2020 study. Everything else consists of fragments, such as the pieces from Tjele in Denmark and Lokrume on Gotland.

### When and where was the Gjermundbu helmet found?

In spring 1943, on the farm Gjermundbu near Haugsbygd in Ringerike, Norway. Gunnar Gjermundbo struck iron while levelling ground, and the archaeologists Sverre Marstrander and Charlotte Blindheim then excavated the mound. The helmet lay in nine fragments in a cremation grave from the second half of the 10th century.

### What did Viking helmets look like?

Judging from Gjermundbu, a rounded iron cap made of plates riveted to iron bands, with a small spike on top and a spectacle-shaped guard around the eyes and nose. Simpler caps with a nose guard were probably more common, and many warriors had no metal helmet at all. None of the surviving helmets has horns.

### Where can you see the Gjermundbu helmet?

It belongs to the Museum of Cultural History of the University of Oslo and is displayed at the Historical Museum in central Oslo, next to the mail shirt from the same grave. It is due to move to the new Museum of the Viking Age at Bygdøy when that museum opens.

## A Piece of Iron as the Face of an Era

The Gjermundbu helmet is a textbook case of how a single find can shape our entire picture of history. Every serious reconstruction, every museum diorama and every halfway-researched film reaches for this one object, because there is simply nothing else like it. That a nameless mounted warrior from Ringerike, cremated and forgotten under a mound, ended up becoming the face of the whole Viking Age is one of archaeology's quiet ironies. His name is lost. His helmet is immortal.
