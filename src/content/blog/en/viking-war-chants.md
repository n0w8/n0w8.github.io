The best-documented **Viking war chant** is a poem, not a roar: according to Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla, the Icelandic skald Þormóðr Kolbrúnarskáld recited the old Bjarkamál at dawn before the Battle of Stiklestad in 1030, loudly enough to wake the entire army. No Old Norse source describes a thousand-voice choir, a hum swelling behind the shields or a "huh" timed to clapping hands. What the texts record instead are shouted watchwords, horn blasts, berserkers bellowing in a poem from around 900, and a spear thrown over the enemy to give them all to Odin.

Picture the morning the way Snorri tells it. A field in Trøndelag in high summer, men who have slept under their shields, a king who prayed for hours and woke at first light. Too early to rouse the army, he decides. So he asks where his poet is.

## Did Vikings Sing Before Battle? The Short Answer

The honest answer comes in three layers:

- **Attested in early poetry:** noise. Hrafnsmál, a poem from around 900, has berserkers bellowing and wolf-coated warriors howling. That is a din, not a song.
- **Recorded late, but specific:** a single skald reciting an old heroic poem, and battle cries such as "Fram, fram!" that worked mainly as recognition signals. Both come from thirteenth-century sagas.
- **Modern inventions:** the Viking clap and its "huh", "skål" as a skull-drinking ritual, and the image of an entire army chanting in unison.

Everyday Norse music, from lyres and pipes to horns, is covered in our guide to [Viking music](/en/blog/viking-music/). This article is only about the moment before the first spear flies.

## Stiklestad, 1030: The Poem That Woke an Army

Olaf Haraldsson, later Saint Olaf, had been forced out of Norway in 1028 and went into exile in Kievan Rus. In 1030 he came back through Sweden. At Stiklestad, about 80 kilometres north of Trondheim, he met an army of farmers led by men such as Kálfr Árnason and Þórir hundr. Olaf lost the battle and his life.

In Óláfs saga helga, the longest section of Heimskringla, Snorri describes that morning closely. The king calls for Þormóðr: *"Tel þú oss kvæði nökkut"*, recite us a poem. Þormóðr sits up and delivers it *hátt mjök*, very loudly, so the whole army can hear. His choice is **Bjarkamál hin fornu**, the Old Lay of Bjarki. Snorri quotes the opening:

> *Dagr er upp kominn, dynja hana fjaðrar* - Day has come up, the cock's feathers are rustling. (our translation)

The passage Snorri quotes ends, roughly: I do not wake you for wine or for whispers with women, but for the hard game of Hild. Hild is a valkyrie, and her game is battle.

The army wakes. The men thank the skald, say the poem suited the moment, and give it a name of their own: **húskarlahvöt**, the housecarls' whetting, a spur for the king's retainers. Olaf rewards Þormóðr with a gold ring weighing half a mark.

### Why This Poem of All Poems?

Bjarkamál tells of the fall of the legendary Danish king Hrólfr kraki in his hall at Lejre on Zealand, framed as an exchange between two of his champions, Bǫðvarr Bjarki and Hjalti, on the night of their last fight. The message is blunt: loyal men die beside their lord. Only fragments survive in Old Norse; the fullest version is a free Latin reworking in Book 2 of Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum, around 1200. The poem itself is usually dated to about the year 1000, though nobody can be sure.

A poem about a king whose men fall with him, on that of all mornings: great literature or grim reality, probably both. Þormóðr outlived the battle by a matter of hours. Snorri has him walk, wounded, into an outbuilding where a woman is dressing the injured. He gives her the ring King Olaf gave him that morning. Then he pulls the arrow out of his own chest with a pair of tongs, sees fibres of his heart on the barbs, some red and some white, and says *"Vel hefir konungrinn alit oss"*: the king has fed us well. He sinks back and dies.

### Sung or Spoken?

Snorri's verb is *kveða*, which covers speaking, reciting and singing. Nobody knows how skaldic verse sounded, because no Viking Age melodies survive; the earliest written secular music from Denmark is in the Codex Runicus of around 1300. Anyone setting a Viking war chant to music today is inventing the tune, however old the words.

The same saga shows what skalds were really for in battle. While drawing up the army, Olaf placed Þormóðr, Gizurr gullbrá and Þorfinnr munnr inside the shield-fort, the ring of picked fighters around the king, so they could witness events and compose about them afterwards. Skalds were witnesses and future historians, not cheerleaders. There is more on these poets in our article on [Viking skalds](/en/blog/skalds-viking-poets/).

## "Fram, Fram!": The Viking Battle Cry at Stiklestad

If one Viking battle cry survives word for word, it is this one. According to Snorri, Olaf had a white cross painted on his men's helmets and shields and gave the whole army a single watchword:

> *Fram, fram, kristsmenn, krossmenn, konungsmenn!* - Forward, forward, Christ's men, cross men, king's men!

The farmers had their own: *"Fram, fram, búandmenn!"*, forward, farmers. As the armies closed, Þórir hundr shouted it in front of the banner. Both sides raised the **heróp**, the war-whoop, a wordless battle yell, and only then did the king's men call out their watchword, as they had been drilled.

Snorri reports the sequel without comment. Farmers on the wings heard the king's cry and repeated it. Their own comrades took them for king's men, turned on them, and many died before anyone realised. A battle cry was above all an identification signal in a world without uniforms.

In fairness, Snorri was writing around 1230, two centuries later, about a king who had long since become Norway's national saint, and the "cross men" carry an obvious Christian colouring. A convincing scene does not prove every word was spoken.

### What About a Pagan Battle Cry?

No saga preserves an explicitly pagan battle cry, but several preserve a pagan gesture. In Völuspá, stanza 24, Odin hurls, most readers assume, his spear into the enemy host, and the first war in the world begins. In Eyrbyggja saga, chapter 44, Steinþórr throws a spear over his opponents *at fornum sið*, by ancient custom. And in Styrbjarnar þáttr, before the battle of Fýrisvellir, King Eiríkr of Sweden throws a reed over the enemy army, which turns into a spear in mid-air, with the words *"Óðinn á yðr alla"*, Odin owns you all. The manuscript, Flateyjarbók, dates only from around 1390. It is a dedication, not a song.

## Howling Berserkers: What the Sources Actually Say

No figure is linked to battle howls more than the berserker, yet the most famous description is silent. In chapter 6 of Ynglinga saga, Snorri describes Odin's warriors going without mail, mad as dogs or wolves, biting their shields and strong as bears or bulls. There is not a word about chanting or howling.

The noise comes from an older source. The skald Þorbjörn hornklofi composed Hrafnsmál about Harald Fairhair late in the ninth century, and Snorri quotes it in Heimskringla, in the chapter on the sea battle at Hafrsfjord:

> *Grenjuðu berserkir ... emjuðu úlfheðnar ok ísarn glumdu* - The berserkers bellowed, the wolf-coats howled and the iron clanged. (our translation, shortened)

That is the oldest evidence for Norse warriors making noise in battle, and it is unambiguous: bellowing, howling, ringing metal. No rhythm, no words, no tune. Our article on [Viking berserkers](/en/blog/viking-berserkers/) looks at the men behind those lines.

![Walrus-ivory warder from the Lewis chessmen, baring his teeth as he bites the top edge of his shield](/images/viking-war-chants-3.jpg)

Four of the Lewis chessmen, found in the Hebrides in 1831, gnaw their shields like this. They show how firmly the image had lodged in people's minds, not what a battlefield sounded like.

## War Horns: Signals, Not Songs

If any instrument sounded before a Norse battle, it was a horn. The most famous one is mythical. In Völuspá, stanza 46, Heimdallr sounds the Gjallarhorn: *hátt blæss Heimdallr, horn er á lofti*, loud blows Heimdall, the horn is raised. It signals the last battle of the gods.

The sagas know the same thing on a human scale. Before Stiklestad, the farmers were told to fall into line as soon as the *lúðrar* sounded and the *herblástr*, the war blast, went up. In the same saga, the Swedish king Önundr has the war blast sounded when Cnut the Great's fleet comes into view. Horns woke men, gathered them and gave the signal to attack. No source says they accompanied singing.

The archaeology is thin. The Oseberg ship, buried in 834, held a wooden tube just over a metre long that most researchers read as a lur. The bronze lurs from Danish bogs are well over a thousand years older and belong to the Bronze Age.

## Tacitus and the Barditus: Germanic, Not Viking

Many online descriptions of a "Viking war chant" sound suspiciously like a text with no Vikings in it. In chapter 3 of his Germania, written in AD 98, Tacitus says the Germans sang of a hero he calls Hercules, usually taken to be Donar, as they went into battle. They called the battle song *barditus* and read the outcome of the fight from its sound. They aimed for a harsh tone, *obiectis ad os scutis*, with shields held up to their mouths, so the voice would swell fuller and deeper as it echoed back.

Wonderful source, wrong era. Nearly 700 years separate Tacitus from the raid on Lindisfarne in 793, and his Germans lived along the Rhine and the Elbe, not in Norway. Our suspicion is that the modern image of a war chant rumbling behind the shield wall is, to a large extent, Tacitus in a horned helmet. And the horned helmet, as we [show elsewhere](/en/blog/viking-horned-helmet-myth/), is a nineteenth-century invention.

## The Viking Clap: Where the "Huh" Really Comes From

The sound most people now associate with a Viking battle cry went global in 2016. Iceland, population around 330,000, beat England 2-1 in Nice at the European Championship on 27 June and only went out to hosts France in the quarter-final on 3 July. After matches, players and fans raised their arms, clapped slowly overhead and answered each clap with a deep "huh", faster and faster.

Its origin is well documented and entirely un-Viking. In July 2014, Icelandic club Stjarnan played Scotland's Motherwell in a Europa League qualifier. At the 2-2 draw at Fir Park, travelling Stjarnan fans heard the home supporters' slow clapping chant, took it home and passed it on to national team fans. Kristinn Hallur Jónsson, treasurer of the supporters' group Tólfan, told the Telegraph, as FIFA later reported, that it came from Scottish fans and owed more to the Spartans in the film "300" than to the Vikings.

![Icelandic fans in blue shirts in the stands with their arms raised, clapping above their heads](/images/viking-war-chants-4.jpg)
*Iceland fans doing the "huh" at the 2018 World Cup match against Croatia. Photo: Светлана Бекетова (Svetlana Beketova), CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

It has been called the Viking clap or thunderclap ever since, and Minnesota Vikings fans have chanted "Skol" instead of "huh" since 2016. Genuine Icelandic pop culture, but not a thousand-year-old war chant.

Modern war chants live in music now: "Scar" by [Eldruna](https://eldruna.com/) is one present-day battle hymn, newly written and explicitly not a reconstruction.

## Skål: A Toast, Not a Skull

No word gets bellowed more in Viking videos than "skål", and none has less to do with battle. Old Norse *skál* simply means bowl. Saga toasts go by other names: in the saga of Hákon the Good, the first cup at a sacrificial feast, Odin's *full*, is drunk for the king's victory and power, then those of Njörðr and Freyr for good harvests and peace. Toasts to distinguished kinsmen were called *minni*, memorials.

The skull legend goes back to a translation error. Krákumál, a twelfth-century poem, speaks of beer drunk *ór bjúgviðum hausa*, from the curved trees of skulls: a kenning for drinking horns, which do grow out of heads. The Danish scholar Ole Worm rendered it in the seventeenth century as if the heroes drank from the skulls of the slain. "Skål" itself was a well-established toast by then; English borrowed "skoal" from Danish around 1600. It only became a battle cry in the stands.

The pattern is hard to miss: the louder and more collective the idea, the younger it turns out to be.

## FAQ

### Did Vikings sing before battle?

There is no evidence of a whole army singing together. The best-attested case is one skald: according to Heimskringla, Þormóðr Kolbrúnarskáld recited the old Bjarkamál before Stiklestad in 1030 to wake the army. Whether he spoke or sang it is unknown.

### What was the Viking battle cry?

The sagas record several. The best known is the watchword of Olaf's army at Stiklestad, "Fram, fram, kristsmenn, krossmenn, konungsmenn!", answered by the farmers' "Fram, fram, búandmenn!", plus the *heróp*, a general war-whoop. Snorri's account shows that these cries mainly helped men tell friend from foe.

### Where does the Viking clap come from?

Iceland's version came from Scottish football. Fans of the Icelandic club Stjarnan picked up the slow clapping chant from Motherwell supporters in July 2014, and it went global at Euro 2016. Even a spokesman for Iceland's supporters' group Tólfan linked it to the film "300" rather than to the Vikings.

### Did berserkers howl in battle?

In Hrafnsmál, a poem from around 900, the berserkers bellow and the wolf-coats howl, the oldest evidence there is. The famous description in Ynglinga saga mentions only shield-biting and frenzy. Neither text describes anything like a song.

### What does skål mean?

Skål literally means bowl, and it is the everyday toast across Scandinavia today. In the sagas, toasts were called *full* or *minni*. The story of Vikings drinking from enemy skulls rests on a misread kenning for drinking horns.

## Our Take: One Voice at Dawn

The Viking war chant the sources support is quieter than the internet's. Not an army stamping in time, but one man in the half-light reciting an old poem about retainers who die with their king. The noise came afterwards: the horn, the *heróp*, the watchword.

What stays with us most is the ring. Olaf gave it to Þormóðr in the morning for the Bjarkamál, and before the day was out Þormóðr had handed it to a healer and died. The poem had promised that loyal men fall with their lord. It kept its word.
