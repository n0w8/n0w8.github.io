The **Elder Futhark** is the oldest runic alphabet: a row of 24 runes that Germanic peoples carved from about AD 150 to 800, arranged in three groups of eight called ættir and named after its first six letters, F-U-Þ-A-R-K. It is older than the Viking Age, it was a working script rather than an oracle, and much of what circulates online about single runes comes from far later sources. This guide sticks to what the evidence supports, from the full table to the oldest finds.

Runologists count only some 350 inscriptions in the Elder Futhark, about 270 from Scandinavia and about 80 from today's Germany, Austria and Switzerland, many of them a single name or word on a brooch, a weapon or a comb.

## All 24 Elder Futhark Runes in One Table

One caveat first: **the names of the Elder Futhark runes are not recorded anywhere.** No inscription from the period spells them out. They are scholarly reconstructions in Proto-Germanic, worked back from sources written down centuries later. Where the evidence is thin, the table says so.

The order itself was not quite fixed: the Kylver Stone puts p before ï and d before o, the Vadstena bracteate has the reverse, and modern charts simply combine the two.

## Where the Rune Names Really Come From

To reconstruct the names, scholars compare later traditions that each inherited them in their own language. The most important is the **Old English Rune Poem**, which covers counterparts of all 24 old runes; its only manuscript burned in 1731 and survives through a copy George Hickes printed in 1705. Then there are the **Norwegian and Icelandic rune poems** on the 16 runes of the Younger Futhark, known from a 17th-century copy of a lost 13th-century manuscript and from Icelandic manuscripts of around 1500, plus the 9th-century **Abecedarium Nordmannicum** and the **Gothic letter names**.

Where these traditions agree, the reconstruction is solid: Old English *feoh* and Old Norse *fé* both mean "cattle, wealth", so fehu is safe. Where they disagree, a question mark is needed. The k-rune is "ulcer" in the Norse poems but "torch" in the Old English one, which is why some books call it kenaz, and the þ-rune is "giant" in Norse but "thorn" in Old English. The p-rune's only attested name, Old English *peorð*, has no known meaning. The z-rune's original name is lost altogether: "algiz" ("elk") is a modern guess that goes back to Wilhelm Grimm in 1821, and the popular meaning "protection" is a gloss from divination books.

Keep in mind that all of these poems were written in Christian times, centuries after the Elder Futhark had fallen out of use. They show what medieval writers thought the names meant, not what a carver in the year 300 had in mind.

## The Three Ættir: Freyr's, Hagal's and Týr's Ætt

The 24 runes fall into three groups of eight. Each group is called an *ætt* (plural *ættir*), Old Norse for "family" or "lineage".

| Ætt | Runes | Sounds |
|---|---|---|
| Freyr's ætt | ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚨ ᚱ ᚲ ᚷ ᚹ | f u þ a r k g w |
| Hagal's ætt | ᚺ ᚾ ᛁ ᛃ ᛇ ᛈ ᛉ ᛊ | h n i j ï p z s |
| Týr's ætt | ᛏ ᛒ ᛖ ᛗ ᛚ ᛜ ᛞ ᛟ | t b e m l ŋ d o |

The grouping is old. The Vadstena bracteate, a gold pendant from around 500, splits the row with dots into exactly these three blocks, and later cipher runes encode each rune by the number of its group and its position in it. The names are not old: calling the groups after their first runes, Freyr's, Hagal's and Týr's ætt, is only attested from the 17th century.

## Where Runes Came From and Why They Look So Angular

Most runologists derive the runes from one of the Old Italic alphabets of the Alps and northern Italy, or from Latin itself, and place their invention around the 1st century AD. The angular shapes are practical: runes were made for carving, and on a wooden stick a horizontal line runs along the grain, where it is hard to read and likely to split the wood. The word itself goes back to Proto-Germanic *rūnō*, "secret, mystery", and lives on in Gothic *rūna* and Old Norse *rún*.

## The Oldest Finds: From Meldorf to Eggja

| Find | Where | Date | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meldorf fibula | Schleswig-Holstein, Germany | 1st century AD | runes or Latin letters? Disputed |
| Svingerud stone | Hole, Norway (found 2021) | 50 BC to AD 275 | oldest datable runestone |
| Vimose comb | Funen, Denmark | c. AD 160 | *harja*, oldest securely dated inscription |
| Tune stone | Østfold, Norway | c. 200 to 450 | early memorial, inheritance |
| Kylver Stone | Gotland, Sweden | c. 400 | first complete futhark row |
| Golden horns of Gallehus | Southern Jutland, Denmark | early 5th century | maker's inscription |
| Kragehul spear shaft | Funen, Denmark | probably 5th century | *ek erilaz*, perhaps "I, the rune master" |
| Vadstena bracteate | Östergötland, Sweden | c. 500 | row in three groups of eight |
| Eggja stone | Sogn, Norway | c. 650 to 700 | longest Elder Futhark inscription |

The **Meldorf fibula** carries four signs that some read as runes and others as Latin letters, so it proves nothing on its own; the first inscription that is both clearly runic and securely dated is *harja* on the Vimose comb, perhaps a name, perhaps "warrior" or simply "comb". The **Kylver Stone**, found in 1903, covered a grave with the complete row carved on its underside, followed by a tree-like sign and the unexplained palindrome *sueus*. It is now in the Swedish History Museum in Stockholm. And the shorter **golden horn of Gallehus** carried the most famous Elder Futhark sentence, *ek hlewagastiz holtijaz horna tawido*, "I, Hlewagastiz Holtijaz, made the horn". Both horns were stolen and melted down in 1802; museums today show replicas.

## From 24 to 16 Runes: The Younger Futhark of the Vikings

In the 7th and 8th centuries something odd happened. Spoken Proto-Norse was gaining new sounds, yet the alphabet shrank. Transitional stones in Blekinge in southern Sweden, such as Björketorp, show mixed forms, and by the late 8th century the **Younger Futhark** had only 16 runes. Carvers no longer marked long against short vowels or voiced against voiceless consonants, so one rune had to do several jobs: ᚢ for u, o, ø, y and w, ᛁ for i and e, ᚴ for k, g and ŋ, ᛏ for t and d, ᛒ for b and p. Readers worked out the sound from context. Dotted runes that told some of these sounds apart again only came in from the late 10th century.

There were two main variants, long-branch runes in Denmark and short-twig runes in Sweden and Norway. This is the script of the roughly 3,000 runestones in Scandinavia, most of them raised between 950 and 1100, mainly in Sweden. It ranges from the royal [Jelling Stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/) to inscriptions Scandinavians left far from home, like the one on the [Piraeus Lion](/en/blog/piraeus-lion-viking-runes/) in Venice. So "Viking runes" in the strict sense means the Younger Futhark. The Elder Futhark was already out of use when raiders reached Lindisfarne in 793.

## What Runes Were Actually Used For

**Names and ownership.** The earliest inscriptions often name the maker or owner of an object, and the habit lasted for a thousand years. About 670 medieval runic inscriptions found since 1955 at Bryggen in Bergen include name tags like *Eysteinn á mik*, "Eysteinn owns me", and notes like *Gyða segir at þú gakk heim*, "Gyda tells you to go home".

**Memorials.** Inscribed stones appear in Norway and Sweden from the 4th and 5th centuries and became the signature use of runes in the Viking Age, recording family, inheritance, journeys and later Christian prayers.

**Charms.** The word *alu*, literally "ale", appears on a whole series of objects from about the 3rd to the 8th century, from gold bracteates to the Elgesem stone in Norway. The Björketorp stone threatens anyone who damages it with misfortune and an evil death. Scholars agree this reflects some form of runic magic, though what *alu* was meant to do is still debated.

**Myth.** In stanzas 138 and 139 of the *Hávamál*, [Odin](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/) hangs for nine nights on a windswept tree before he takes up the runes. The poem survives in the Codex Regius from the 1270s, written in Christian Iceland long after the Elder Futhark had vanished.

**Calendars.** Runic calendars, or rune staffs, marked weekdays and moon phases with runes. The oldest known one, from Nyköping in Sweden, is thought to date from the 13th century, and they stayed in use into the 19th century.

## Runes and Modern Esotericism: List, Blum and the Rune Oracle

For runic fortune-telling there is almost no evidence. Tacitus' *Germania* (AD 98) describes lots cut from a fruit-bearing tree, marked with signs and thrown onto a white cloth, but he does not say what the signs were, and he wrote before the earliest securely dated runic inscription.

The modern rune oracle has two traceable roots. **Guido von List** (1848-1919), an Austrian occultist, claimed that the "secret" of the runes was revealed to him while he was blind for about eleven months after a cataract operation in 1902. His invented **Armanen Futhark** of 18 runes, loosely based on the Younger Futhark and tied to the 18 charms listed in the Hávamál, appeared as a book in 1908 under the title *Das Geheimnis der Runen*. Through the völkisch movement it fed into Nazi symbolism such as the SS sig runes, and neo-Nazi groups still misuse runes like othala today.

**Ralph Blum**'s *The Book of Runes* from 1982 came with a bag of rune tiles, drew heavily on popular books about the I Ching and added a blank rune that exists in no historical rune row. Many one-line "rune meanings" online, such as "Algiz means protection", go back to this tradition. Using runes as a modern spiritual practice is fine, but it is modern, just like the [Vegvísir](/en/blog/vegvisir-viking-compass/), a 19th-century Icelandic magical stave that is neither a rune nor Viking.

## How to Write Your Name in Elder Futhark Runes

You are transliterating a modern name into a script made for a language spoken some 1,500 years ago. There is no official key, and online "rune translators" that swap letter for letter usually get it wrong. Four rules get you much closer:

1. **Write the sound, not the spelling.** Say your name aloud and split it into sounds before you look at the table.
2. **Pick the nearest rune.** A hard C becomes ᚲ, a soft C ᛊ, PH ᚠ, X ᚲᛊ, the "th" in "thing" ᚦ and the "ng" in "sing" ᛜ.
3. **Write doubled sounds once**, as rune carvers often did, so Emma needs only one ᛗ.
4. **Accept the gaps.** The "j" in James, "ch" or "sh" have no rune of their own, and every approximation is a compromise.

| Name | Sounds | Elder Futhark |
|---|---|---|
| Emma | e-m-a | ᛖᛗᚨ |
| Sophie | s-o-f-i | ᛊᛟᚠᛁ |
| Christopher | k-r-i-s-t-o-f-e-r | ᚲᚱᛁᛊᛏᛟᚠᛖᚱ |
| Ethan | i-th-a-n | ᛁᚦᚨᚾ |
| Mike | m-a-i-k | ᛗᚨᛁᚲ |

Ethan is spelled with an E but starts with an "ee" sound, so it begins with ᛁ. For a name as a Viking would have carved it, you would need the Younger Futhark or a real Old Norse name from the runestones; our guide to [Viking names and their meanings](/en/blog/viking-names-meanings/) is a good place to start.

## FAQ

### How many runes are in the Elder Futhark?

The Elder Futhark has 24 runes in three groups of eight called ættir. The Viking Age Younger Futhark cut the row to 16, while the Anglo-Saxon futhorc expanded it to as many as 33.

### Is the Elder Futhark Viking?

No. The Elder Futhark was used from about AD 150 to 800, so it is older than the Viking Age, which began around 793. The Vikings wrote in the Younger Futhark with 16 runes.

### What do the runes mean?

Each rune stands for a sound and has a name that is also an ordinary word, such as fehu "cattle" or sōwilō "sun". The names are reconstructed from medieval rune poems, and for a few runes the meaning is unknown. Readings such as "protection" come from 20th-century esoteric books, not from historical sources.

### How old are runes?

The oldest securely dated runic inscription is on the Vimose comb from Denmark, from around AD 160. The Svingerud stone from Norway may be older, and the Meldorf fibula from the 1st century AD is disputed. Scholars generally place the invention of runes around the 1st century AD.

### Were runes used for fortune-telling?

There is no clear evidence for it. Charm words like *alu* show that runes played a part in magic, but fortune-telling with rune sets goes back mainly to Ralph Blum's book of 1982.

### What is the difference between Elder and Younger Futhark?

The Elder Futhark (about 150 to 800) has 24 runes, roughly one per sound. The Younger Futhark (from about 800) has only 16, so one rune often covers several sounds, such as ᛏ for both t and d.
