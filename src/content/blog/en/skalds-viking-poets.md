A **skald** was a Viking Age poet who composed praise, mockery and memorial verse for kings and jarls: more than 300 are known by name from roughly 800 to 1200, and the earliest whose lines survive is the Norwegian Bragi Boddason, active in the first half of the ninth century. **Skaldic poetry** is some of the most intricate verse medieval Europe produced, with six syllables to the line, alliteration, internal rhyme and riddling phrases that turn a ship into a "wave's horse" and gold into "Sif's hair".

Egil's saga shows what a good stanza was worth. After a battle in England, Egill Skallagrímsson sits glowering in King Æthelstan's hall, sword across his knees, refusing the drinking horn. The king slides a heavy gold ring onto the point of his sword and holds it out across the fire. Egill takes it on his own blade, puts it on, drinks, and recites a verse in the king's honour. A ring for a poem. That was the deal.

## What Is a Skald? The Short Answer

Forget the harp-strumming bard of Romantic imagination. A skald was a poet who was often also a warrior, a farmer or an envoy, and his verse had a job to do: praising rulers, insulting enemies, recording battles, mourning the dead. Where the word *skáld* comes from is uncertain; one common suggestion links it to English *scold*.

The modern collected edition, *Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages*, counts 5,797 stanzas and fragments by 447 poets in 718 manuscripts, many of them contemporary with the events they describe. Snorri Sturluson explains in the prologue to Heimskringla why he trusts them: a skald recited his poem to the ruler himself, and nobody would dare credit a king with invented deeds in front of everyone who knew better. That, Snorri says, would be mockery, not praise. A surprisingly modern argument about sources, if a convenient one for Snorri.

## What Skalds Did: Praise, Mockery and Memorial Verse

Skaldic poetry had fixed genres, and the pecking order mattered:

- **Drápa:** the full praise poem with a refrain (*stef*), the most prestigious form.
- **Flokkr:** a poem without a refrain, shorter and less formal.
- **Lausavísa:** a single stanza, often presented as improvised on the spot.
- **Níð:** insult verse aimed at an enemy.
- **Erfidrápa:** a memorial poem for a dead ruler.

Snorri shows how seriously the difference was taken. The Icelander Þórarinn loftunga had composed a mere flokkr for Cnut the Great, and Cnut ordered a drápa by the time he sat down to eat, or Þórarinn would hang for the impudence of so short a poem. Þórarinn added a refrain and a few stanzas. The result, named *Höfuðlausn* or "Head-Ransom" like Egill's more famous poem, earned him fifty marks of silver.

Payment came in things you could wear and show off. At Yule, King Olaf Haraldsson gave his skald Sigvatr a sword with a gold-wound hilt, which caused some envy at court. Einarr Helgason got a set of scales from Jarl Hákon and was known ever after as *skálaglamm*, "scale-tinkle". Egill's first fee, earned at three for verses to his grandfather: three sea-snail shells and a duck's egg.

The flip side was mockery. Níð counted as an assault on honour, and the Icelandic law code Grágás has a chapter on poetry that threatens outlawry for libellous verse and even for love poems about a woman. Þorleifr jarlsskáld is remembered for a verse curse on his former patron, Jarl Hákon.

The memorial poems were the most solemn of all. **Eiríksmál**, made around 954 for the fallen Eirik Bloodaxe, and **Hákonarmál**, Eyvindr skáldaspillir's poem for Hákon the Good, describe the dead king's reception in Valhalla, and Bragi appears in both. ## Famous Skalds: Bragi, Egill, Sigvatr and Þormóðr

**Bragi Boddason**, called "the Old", is the earliest skald with surviving verse. His *Ragnarsdrápa* is a shield poem, describing the pictures on a splendid shield he had been given: Gefjon ploughing, Thor fishing for the World Serpent, the endless battle of the Hjaðningar. Whether the god of poetry, also named Bragi, was once this poet is debated. Many scholars think it possible; Jan de Vries disagreed.

**Egill Skallagrímsson**, a tenth-century Icelander, farmer, warrior and killer, is the most famous of them all. In York he came face to face with his mortal enemy, Eirik Bloodaxe, and his friend Arinbjörn advised him to give himself up and compose a praise poem overnight. A swallow, the saga says, twittered at the window all night until Arinbjörn sat there himself. In the morning Egill delivered the *Höfuðlausn*, about twenty stanzas in end rhyme, and Eirik granted him his head. Whether Egill really composed it in this form is disputed; it is missing from some versions of the saga.

**Sonatorrek**, "The Loss of Sons", is another matter. Egill's son Böðvarr drowned, shortly after another son, Gunnarr, had died of fever. Egill shut himself away to starve until his daughter Þorgerðr tricked him into drinking and asked him to compose a poem for his sons first. Across 25 stanzas he rages at Odin, then concedes that the god gave him poetry in compensation. It is often called the first truly personal lyric of the North.

![Bronze statue of Egill lifting a horse's head above his own head, on a stone plinth with an inscription in a park](/images/skalds-viking-poets-3.jpg)
*"Egil Skallagrimsson reiser nidstang": Gustav Vigeland's statue in Mandal shows Egill raising his scorn-pole against Eirik and Gunnhild. Photo: Bjornfje, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons*

**Sigvatr Þórðarson** (c. 995 to c. 1045) was court poet and marshal to Olaf the Saint. His *Austrfararvísur* turn a gruelling diplomatic trip to Sweden in 1019 into comedy; when Olaf fell at Stiklestad in 1030, Sigvatr was on pilgrimage in Rome. Around 1038, in the *Bersöglisvísur* or "plain-speaking verses", he urged Olaf's son Magnus to stop taking revenge on his father's enemies. Magnus listened and went down in history as "the Good". Sigvatr had also chosen his name, after Charlemagne.

**Þormóðr Kolbrúnarskáld** died with his king at Stiklestad. At dawn before the battle, at Olaf's request, he performed the old *Bjarkamál* so loudly that the whole army woke, a scene we unpack in our piece on [Viking war chants](/en/blog/viking-war-chants/). Mortally wounded, he later pulled the arrowhead from his side with tongs, saw fibres of his heart on the barb and said, roughly: the king has fed us well, I am still fat around the heart-roots.

Note where these men came from. After Eyvindr skáldaspillir, the last major Norwegian skald, the known court poets came almost entirely from Iceland or Orkney. There were women too. In two surviving stanzas, **Steinunn Refsdóttir** credits Thor with wrecking the missionary Þangbrandr's ship, adding that Christ had been no help at all.

## Dróttkvætt: The Court Metre of Skaldic Poetry

The skalds' signature metre is **dróttkvætt**, named after the *drótt*, the king's household troop. The rules are punishing:

- A stanza has eight lines, split into two halves (*helmingar*).
- Each line almost always has six syllables and three stresses, and ends on a long-short cadence.
- In every odd line two stressed syllables alliterate, and the first stress of the next line picks up the sound.
- Odd lines carry a half rhyme (*skothending*), even lines a full rhyme (*aðalhending*), both inside the line.

The result is syntax in which two or three sentences run interlaced. The king's saga *Fagrskinna* gives an example from Harald Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge in 1066. Harald supposedly made a stanza in a plainer eddic metre, dismissed it as badly made, and then produced this:

> *Krjúpum vér fyr vápna / (valteigs) brǫkun eigi / (svá bauð Hildr) at hjaldri / (haldorð) í bug skjaldar.* Our rendering: In battle we do not creep into the curve of the shield before the crash of weapons; so commanded the word-true Hildr of the hawk-land, meaning a noblewoman.

Watch the machinery: "valteigs" belongs with "Hildr" two lines later, *v*ér, *v*ápna and *v*alteigs alliterate, and "h*ald*orð" rhymes with "skj*ald*ar". The anecdote may well be invented, but a saga writer picturing a king who prefers dróttkvætt to plain alliterative verse on the day of battle tells you how much prestige the form carried.

## Kenning Examples: 9 Real Kennings and Their Sources

A **kenning** is a figurative phrase of at least two parts that stands in for a plain noun. Alongside it sits the **heiti**, a simple poetic synonym: where prose says *hestr* for horse, poetry might use *fákr* or *marr*. Every example below comes from surviving verse or from Snorri's explanations:

| Kenning | Literal meaning | Refers to | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| *báru fákr* | wave's horse | ship | Þorbjörn hornklofi, Glymdrápa 3 |
| *Ymis hauss* | Ymir's skull | sky | Arnórr jarlaskáld, Magnúsdrápa 19, quoted in Skáldskaparmál |
| *Kvasis dreyri* | Kvasir's blood | poetry | Einarr skálaglamm, Vellekla 1, quoted in Skáldskaparmál |
| *íss rauðra randa* | ice of red shields | sword | Einarr Skúlason, Øxarflokkr 9 |
| *grennir gunnmás* | feeder of the war-gull | warrior | Þorbjörn hornklofi, Glymdrápa 6 |
| *sárgymir* | wound-sea | blood | Eyvindr skáldaspillir, Hákonarmál 7 |
| *haddr Sifjar* | Sif's hair | gold | Snorri, Skáldskaparmál |
| *Fróða mjöl* | Fróði's flour | gold | Snorri, Skáldskaparmál, after Grottasöngr |
| *mögfellandi mellu* | slayer of the giantess's sons | Thor | Steinunn Refsdóttir, Lausavísa 2 |

Many kennings only work if you know the myths. The sky is Ymir's skull because, in Snorri's Gylfaginning, the gods built it from the primeval giant's skull. Gold is Sif's hair because dwarves forged the goddess golden hair after Loki cut off her own. The war-gull is the raven, and whoever feeds it is supplying corpses.

Kennings could be stacked, too. One nested inside another made the figure *tvíkennt*, "doubly determined", and more than three elements made it *rekit*, "extended". Chains of up to seven elements survive, though Snorri regarded five as the limit of good taste.

## Snorri's Skáldskaparmál: The Skalds' Textbook

That we can decode any of this is largely thanks to **Snorri Sturluson** (1179 to 1241). His Prose Edda, written around 1220, has four parts: the Prologue, Gylfaginning, Skáldskaparmál and Háttatal. **Skáldskaparmál**, "the language of poetry", opens as a conversation at a feast in which Bragi explains the origins of poetry to the sea giant Ægir. It soon becomes a thesaurus of poetic expressions, backed by hundreds of quotations from older skalds, many surviving nowhere else.

Snorri writes explicitly for young poets, *ungum skáldum*, while warning that Christians should not believe in the heathen gods. **Háttatal** demonstrates the metres in 102 stanzas Snorri composed for King Hákon Hákonarson and Jarl Skúli. He was prescribing rather than describing, and the older poets did not always follow his rules.

## The Mead of Poetry: Where Skalds Got Their Gift

Where does poetry come from? Snorri answers with one of the strangest myths in the North. The dwarves Fjalarr and Galarr murdered the wise being **Kvasir** and mixed his blood with honey in the vessels Óðrerir, Boðn and Són: whoever drinks it becomes a poet. The mead passed to the giant **Suttungr**, whose daughter **Gunnlöð** guarded it inside a mountain. Odin got in as a snake, spent three nights with Gunnlöð, drained all three vessels in three swallows and fled to Asgard as an eagle, where he spat the mead into waiting containers.

In his haste some of it went out the back. Anyone could have that portion, and Snorri drily calls it *skáldfífla hlutr*, the rhymesters' share. The good mead went to the gods and to people who know how to compose. The full story is in our portrait of [Odin, the Allfather and god of wisdom](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/).

![Icelandic manuscript illustration: an eagle spits mead into a bowl while a second eagle's head pursues it, with more vessels below](/images/skalds-viking-poets-4.jpg)
*Odin in eagle form spits the mead of poetry into the vessels, with Suttungr behind him. Illustration by Jakob Sigurðsson in the Icelandic manuscript SÁM 66 (1765/66), public domain, via Wikimedia Commons*

Odin's own version, in Hávamál stanzas 104 to 110, is less heroic: without Gunnlöð, he admits, he would never have made it back. For the skalds the myth was a store of imagery. Snorri lists names for poetry such as Kvasir's blood, the dwarves' ship, Suttungr's mead, or Odin's catch and gift. Every poet was, figuratively, pouring Odin's drink.

## Was Skaldic Poetry Sung or Recited?

The honest answer is that nobody knows. There is no notation, no description of a melody and no source that shows a skald with an instrument. Some reference works state flatly that the poems were spoken. Scholars are more cautious, and in 2022 the question got a whole volume of its own, *Old Norse Poetry in Performance*.

The trouble starts with the vocabulary. The verb for performing a poem is *kveða*, which the Cleasby-Vigfússon dictionary glosses as "to say", "to make a verse" and also "to sing". In later Icelandic it describes the half-recited, half-sung delivery of rímur, explicitly not a full melody. Even translators split: when Þormóðr performs before Stiklestad, Samuel Laing has him "sing", Lee M. Hollander has him "recite".

Rímur, rhymed narrative poems going back to the fourteenth century, inherited their kennings and heiti from the skalds, and their chanted delivery survives in Iceland today. Whether it reaches back to the skalds is an attractive guess, nothing more. Our view: the most plausible picture is a heightened, rhythmic chant loud enough to fill a hall, because verse this dense would be almost impossible to follow under a tune. The harp that nineteenth-century painters gave the god Bragi belongs to Romanticism, not to the sources. ## Eddic vs Skaldic Poetry: The Difference

| | Eddic poetry | Skaldic poetry |
|---|---|---|
| Authors | anonymous | usually named |
| Subjects | gods and legendary heroes | contemporaries: kings, jarls, battles, the poet's own life |
| Metres | fornyrðislag, ljóðaháttr, málaháttr | mainly dróttkvætt and its variants |
| Style | comparatively plain | dense kennings, interlaced syntax |
| Survival | mainly the Codex Regius (GKS 2365 4to, c. 1270) | as quotations in kings' sagas, sagas of Icelanders and Snorri's Edda |

The boundary leaks. Sonatorrek is in *kviðuháttr*, a variant of the eddic fornyrðislag, and Eyvindr's Hákonarmál in *ljóðaháttr*, yet both are by known skalds. Poet and occasion make a poem skaldic more than metre alone.

## FAQ

### What is a skald?

A skald was a poet of the Viking Age and the Norse Middle Ages who composed praise, insult and memorial verse, mostly for rulers. More than 300 are known from roughly 800 to 1200, the earliest with surviving verse being Bragi Boddason. Later, most court skalds came from Iceland.

### What is a kenning in Old Norse poetry?

A kenning is a figurative phrase of at least two parts that replaces a plain noun. Attested examples include "wave's horse" for a ship, "Ymir's skull" for the sky, "Sif's hair" for gold and "Kvasir's blood" for poetry. Many only make sense if you know the myths.

### Did skalds sing or recite their poems?

Nobody knows. No source describes a melody or an instrument, and the verb *kveða* can mean to say, to compose and to sing. Our best guess is a heightened, rhythmic chant, but it cannot be proven.

### What is the difference between eddic and skaldic poetry?

Eddic poems are anonymous, tell of gods and heroes and survive mainly in the Codex Regius. Skaldic poems are by named poets, deal with contemporary people and events, and usually use the elaborate dróttkvætt metre packed with kennings.

### Who was the most famous skald?

The best known is Egill Skallagrímsson, hero of Egil's saga, to whom Höfuðlausn and Sonatorrek are attributed. Politically the most influential was probably Sigvatr Þórðarson, court poet to Olaf the Saint.

### Were there female skalds?

Yes, though few are known. Two late tenth-century stanzas survive by the Icelander Steinunn Refsdóttir, mocking a Christian missionary. A poet named Jórunn, nicknamed *skáldmær*, "skald-maiden", is also listed among the skalds.

## What the Skalds Left Behind

You can read the skalds as poets, but they were also press officers, chroniclers and diplomats for the men they served. Their verse was a currency: a praise poem brought rings and swords, an insult could wreck a reputation, a memorial poem turned a dead king into Odin's guest. That Sigvatr could lecture a young king in verse, and the king listened, says more about the standing of this art than any rule of dróttkvætt.

Without the stanzas quoted in the sagas we would know far less about the Viking Age. And the hall into which Eyvindr escorted his king lives on in Norse-inspired music, for example with [Domsgard](https://domsgard.com/), whose track above takes the journey to Valhalla as its theme.

Anyone decoding a kenning today is doing exactly what Snorri hoped his young readers would do eight hundred years ago. The riddles have not changed. Only the pay has got worse: no gold ring, not even a duck's egg.
