The **Gokstad ship** is a Viking ship a little over 23 metres long, built of oak around 890 and buried around 900 with a high-ranking man in the King's Mound near Sandefjord, Norway, where it was dug up in 1880. It is one of the best-preserved Viking ships ever found, and in 1893 a faithful replica proved, quite literally, what these ships could do by crossing the North Atlantic.

## The Discovery Beneath the King's Mound

The find began with a treasure hunt: in early 1880 the sons of the farmer at Gokstad dug into the mound the locals had long called the "King's Mound", Kongshaugen, and hit the bow of a ship. The mound measured about 50 by 43 metres, although ploughing had already reduced its height to around five metres. It had sat undisturbed for centuries beneath a thick layer of blue clay that kept oxygen largely out, preserving the wood from decay. When antiquarian **Nicolay Nicolaysen** arrived in February 1880, he stopped the digging and had the mound opened properly from the side, exposing the bow again on the second day. The excavation was completed that same year, and carefully documented.

Dendrochronological analysis dates the ship's timber to around 890 AD, while the burial itself took place in the first decade of the 10th century, around 900 to 905 AD. The ship had therefore already seen years of use before it was buried. Most researchers read it as a working, ocean-going vessel that had been to sea, not a ship built solely for the grave.

## How Big Is the Gokstad Ship? Size, Oars and Crew

Published figures vary slightly depending on how the hull is measured. The Museum of Cultural History in Oslo gives a length of about 23 metres; other references give 23.8 metres, with a beam of roughly 5 metres. Either way, the Gokstad ship ranks among the larger surviving Viking ships. It was built mostly of oak, with some pine, using the classic **clinker technique**: 16 overlapping strakes per side give the whole hull remarkable flexibility - the same principle that defines the famous [longship](/en/blog/the-longship/).

The ship carried 16 pairs of oars, 32 in all, worked through oar holes in the upper strakes that could be closed with shutters when the ship was under sail. A square sail of roughly 110 square metres drove it, the mast could be raised and lowered, and a steering oar on the right-hand side, the origin of the word "starboard", controlled the course. The Norwegian encyclopedia *Store norske leksikon* reckons the crew at well over 30 people.

Unlike the more ceremonial [Oseberg ship](/en/blog/oseberg-ship/), the Gokstad find was a genuine ocean-going sailing vessel: sturdily built, with a deep keel that gave it markedly better handling on the open sea.

Aboard, archaeologists found 64 round shields, painted black and yellow and tied along the top of the hull above the oar holes - an imposing, intimidating sight for anyone who encountered the ship at sea. Long treated as purely ceremonial grave goods, the shields were re-examined in 2023, and the study suggested they may in fact have been used in combat. The grave also contained three smaller boats, a sled, tent frames, cooking equipment, and a gaming board, apparently meant to serve the deceased on his journey into the afterlife.

## Who Was Buried in the Gokstad Ship?

At the center of the wooden burial chamber, built behind the mast, lay the skeleton of a powerfully built man, around 1.81 to 1.83 metres tall - well above the average for his time. In 1928 the bones were sealed in a lead coffin and reburied in the mound. In 2007 researchers led by the anatomist **Per Holck** exhumed them again, worried that trapped moisture would destroy them, and their re-examination changed the picture.

The man was probably in his forties when he died, younger than earlier estimates of 50 to 70. A thin blade, probably a sword, had cut into his left shinbone, and a cut to the inside of his right thigh may have severed the femoral artery. Because the wounds show no sign of healing, the researchers concluded that he was killed in battle.

> Exactly who was buried in the Gokstad ship remains one of Norse archaeology's open questions - a man of high rank, killed by weapons, whose name history never recorded.

Norwegian saga tradition has linked various names to the grave, including the semi-legendary king **Olaf Geirstad-Alf**, associated in later sources with the Vestfold region; in the 1920s the archaeologist A. W. Brøgger argued for this identification. It can't be proven, however - the sagas in question were written down centuries after the events they describe, and the tradition has Olaf dying of an ailment in his foot, which fits poorly with a man cut down by blades. What archaeology can say with confidence is far more modest: a man of high status, powerfully built, killed in a fight, buried with extraordinary effort.

The grave was also looted in antiquity, likely within a few generations of the burial. The robbers broke in amidships, damaging the hull, and weapons and jewelry are almost entirely absent - a sign that the looters knew exactly where and what to look for.

## Three Small Boats and a Blueprint for Archaeology Itself

Alongside the large ship, excavators found three smaller boats, likely used as tenders for going ashore, fishing, or ferrying people and goods. Two of them survive well enough to compare different building techniques - a rare stroke of luck that shows Viking Age shipyards built not just one ship model, but a whole range of boat types for different purposes.

The Gokstad excavation is also considered something of a founding moment for modern Norse ship archaeology. Nicolaysen's methodical approach - numbering every plank, documenting its exact position, and reconstructing the entire hull piece by piece afterward - later became the standard procedure for subsequent Viking ship excavations, including the Oseberg find two decades later.

## Animals for the Final Journey

Around the deceased lay the bones of twelve horses and several dogs, together with birds that included at least one peacock - an exceptionally exotic bird for the Viking Age, underscoring the far-reaching trade connections the buried man must have had. The exact counts differ between publications: some list six dogs and one peacock, others eight dogs, two peacocks and two goshawks. Such animal offerings were part of the burial ritual, likely meant to serve the deceased on his journey into the afterlife or to demonstrate his status even in death.

## 1893: The Gokstad Replica That Crossed the Atlantic

Perhaps the most striking chapter in the Gokstad ship's story didn't happen during the Viking Age at all, but in 1893. For the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, a faithful replica named simply **"Viking"** was built, and Norwegian captain **Magnus Andersen** sailed it from Bergen with a crew of eleven. It crossed the North Atlantic to Newfoundland in under a month, then continued via New York, up the Hudson, through the Erie Canal and across the Great Lakes, arriving at Jackson Park in Chicago on 12 July 1893.

The voyage was a resounding success. Andersen reported speeds of 10 to 11 knots under sail - a striking practical demonstration that Vikings really could cross the Atlantic with their ships, long before Columbus was even born. The replica still exists today and stands on display under a canopy at Good Templar Park in Geneva, Illinois.

A century later the feat was repeated. The replica *Gaia*, built in 1989 and 1990, was sailed to North America by the Norwegian adventurer Ragnar Thorseth in 1991 and has been based in Sandefjord since 1993, a short distance from the mound where the original was found.

## Where Is the Gokstad Ship Now?

The original ship has stood on the Bygdøy peninsula in Oslo since the 1930s, in the **Viking Ship Museum (Vikingskipshuset)**, alongside the Oseberg ship and other major Viking Age finds. That museum is being rebuilt and extended into the new **Museum of the Viking Age**, designed to give the fragile ships more stable conditions. On 29 October 2025 the Gokstad ship was rolled about 94 metres on an overhead steel rail system into its place in the new wing - the first time it had been moved in one piece since its burial. The museum is scheduled to open in 2027.

## Gokstad Compared: Not Every Viking Ship Was Built Alike

A look at other major Norwegian ship finds shows just how differently Viking ships were built depending on their purpose:

| Ship | Discovered | Length | Built (approx.) | Primary Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gokstad | 1880, Sandefjord | c. 23 m | c. 890 AD | Ocean-going sailing ship, burial ship |
| Oseberg | 1904, Tønsberg | 21.58 m | c. 820 AD | Ceremonial, calm waters |
| Tune | 1867, Rolvsøy | c. 20 m (fragmentary) | c. 900 AD | Likely a war/burial ship |

The comparison makes clear why the Gokstad find mattered so much for the question of Viking seaworthiness: its deeper keel and sturdier construction set it apart from the more fragile Oseberg ship, and those are exactly the qualities the "Viking" confirmed on the Atlantic in 1893. ## FAQ

### How big is the Gokstad ship?

A little over 23 metres long and about 5 metres wide; some references give 23.8 metres. It had 16 pairs of oars, a square sail of roughly 110 square metres and room for a crew of well over 30.

### Who was buried in the Gokstad ship?

A man of high status whose name is unknown. A re-examination after 2007 found he was probably in his forties, about 1.81 to 1.83 metres tall, and killed in battle by blade wounds to his legs. Saga tradition links the grave to the Vestfold king Olaf Geirstad-Alf, but that identification has never been proven.

### How old is the Gokstad ship?

Tree-ring dating shows the ship's timber was felled around 890 AD, and the burial took place in the first decade of the 10th century, around 900 to 905. That makes the ship well over 1,100 years old.

### Where is the Gokstad ship now?

In Oslo, on the Bygdøy peninsula, in the building that is becoming the new Museum of the Viking Age. In October 2025 it was moved into the museum's new wing, and the museum is scheduled to open in 2027.

### Did a Gokstad replica really sail across the Atlantic?

Yes. In 1893 the replica "Viking", captained by Magnus Andersen, sailed from Bergen to Newfoundland in under a month and then on to the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. In 1991 the replica *Gaia* sailed to North America again.

## A Ship That Proved More Than Any Other

What makes the Gokstad ship so remarkable is the rare combination of archaeological quality and practical proof. While many Viking ship finds mostly raise questions, the 1893 replica answered one of the central questions in Viking scholarship with wind, water, and an Atlantic crossing of under a month: yes, these ships really could conquer the open ocean.
