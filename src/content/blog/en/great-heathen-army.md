The **Great Heathen Army** was a coalition of Scandinavian war bands that landed in East Anglia in 865 and by 878 had brought down three of England's four Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, with modern estimates of its size ranging from under a thousand to several thousand men. The **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle** simply calls it the *mycel hæþen here*, the "Great Heathen Army" - not an ordinary raiding party, but a force that stayed for over a decade and eventually left an entire region under Danish rule. What began as a landing ended as a political reordering of England, one whose traces still show up in place names and legal traditions today.

## The Opening Move: Landing in East Anglia, 865

Unlike the notorious hit-and-run raids of earlier decades, such as the [raid on Lindisfarne](/en/blog/lindisfarne-raid/) in 793, the Great Heathen Army was oriented toward conquest from the start, not quick plunder. The Chronicle records that the force wintered in the kingdom of East Anglia, where the local king supplied the Vikings with horses, likely as a form of tribute meant to spare his own territory for the time being. That strategy of buying peace would prove, in hindsight, shortsighted: the horses turned a seaborne force into one that could move fast overland.

## Who Led the Great Heathen Army? Ivar, Halfdan and Ubba

The English sources closest to the events name two leaders: **Ivar the Boneless** (Old English *Ingwær* or *Hingwar*) and **Halfdan**. A third name, **Ubba**, first appears in the *Passio sancti Eadmundi*, written by Abbo of Fleury in the late 10th century, while the Chronicle only mentions, under 878, an unnamed "brother of Ivar and Halfdan" whom tradition identifies as Ubba. Ivar is often identified with **Ímar**, a king of Dublin whose death the Irish annals record in 873, though not all historians accept that they were the same man.

The famous claim that all three were sons of the semi-legendary **Ragnar Lodbrok** comes from much later texts such as the *Annals of St Neots*, Saxo Grammaticus and the Icelandic sagas, and is best read with caution - more in our article on [Ragnar Lothbrok](/en/blog/ragnar-lothbrok-legend-truth/). Leadership also changed: a Danish king named **Bagsecg** fell at Ashdown in 871, and from the mid-870s the dominant figure in the south was **Guthrum**, alongside the kings **Oscetel** and **Anwend**. The army was never one man's army, but an alliance of commanders who joined, split and regrouped as opportunity dictated.

## How Big Was the Great Heathen Army?

No source gives a figure for 865. The Chronicle calls the army "great" and leaves it there, so every estimate rests on indirect evidence.

The laws of King Ine of Wessex, issued around 694, define a *here* as any raiding force of more than thirty-five men. The historian Peter Sawyer argued that the Viking armies were smaller than traditionally thought: assuming no more than 32 men per ship, he put the Great Heathen Army at no more than about a thousand. The Chronicle offers one hard data point: in 878 the brother of Ivar and Halfdan landed in Devon with 23 ships, and 840 of his men were killed there - roughly 36 men per ship, in just one detachment.

Archaeology has pushed the estimates up. The fortified winter camp at Repton covers only about 1.46 hectares, while the camp at Torksey in Lincolnshire, used in 872-873, covered roughly 26 hectares. The archaeologists Dawn Hadley and Julian Richards argue that the lower estimates of 300 to 500 men are too conservative and that the force probably numbered in the thousands. Others caution that a camp full of traders, craftsmen and families says little about how many of its people actually fought.

**Where the debate stands:** most historians now place the army between under a thousand and the low thousands of fighting men, with a few estimates above 5,000. Its size also changed: it was reinforced in 871, lost men in every battle and split in two in 874. Our reading: a few thousand people at its peak, warriors and followers together, fits both the camps and the ship numbers best.

The pattern runs through the entire campaign: one kingdom after another is destabilized, conquered, or forced into paying tribute, while the army regroups and moves on.

## York Falls: The End of Northumbria

In November 866 the army pushed north and captured **York**, the capital of the kingdom of Northumbria, which was already weakened at the time by an internal power struggle between two rival kings, **Ælla** and **Osberht**. When both kings jointly attempted to retake the city in March 867, the counterattack ended in a crushing defeat for the Anglo-Saxons. Both kings were killed in the fighting.

> Norse saga tradition claims Ælla was executed through the gruesome "blood eagle" ritual - a story most modern historians treat as later literary embellishment rather than a reliable historical account.

York remained under the army's control and developed over the following decades into **Jorvik**, one of the most important Viking trading centers in England: conquest was quickly followed by economic settlement. What the Coppergate excavations reveal about that town is the subject of our article on [Jorvik](/en/blog/jorvik-viking-york-coppergate/).

## The Death of Edmund: A Kingdom Extinguished

In 869 the army returned to East Anglia, whose king had apparently hoped that tribute payments would buy lasting tolerance. That hope went unfulfilled: King **Edmund** was defeated in battle, captured, and killed. Later hagiography, especially Abbo's *Passio Sancti Eadmundi*, cast Edmund's death as a martyrdom - he supposedly refused to renounce his Christian faith and was tied to a tree and shot full of arrows as a result.

How accurate this account is can hardly be verified, but the cult of Saint Edmund grew into one of the most significant in England - the town of **Bury St Edmunds** still carries his name.

## 871: The Year of Battles

In 871 the army was reinforced by a second fleet, which the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle calls the *micel sumorlida*, the "Great Summer Army." That same year the Chronicle counts nine major battles against Wessex south of the Thames, including Reading and **Ashdown**.

At Ashdown, on 8 January 871, Alfred's older brother, King **Æthelred of Wessex**, led the Anglo-Saxon forces alongside Alfred himself and won one of the few clear victories of that grim year. Æthelred died a few months later, and Alfred ascended the throne of Wessex under about as unfavorable circumstances as imaginable. Before the year was out, the new king paid the army to leave Wessex, buying time he would badly need.

## What the Archaeology Confirms: Repton and Torksey

Excavations at **Repton** in Derbyshire, where the army wintered in 873-874, moved the story beyond the written sources. Archaeologists found a fortified, D-shaped enclosure on the River Trent and, beneath a mound, the jumbled remains of at least 264 people: mostly men aged 18 to 45, several with signs of violent injury, but also around a fifth women. A 2018 study corrected the radiocarbon dates for the effect of a fish-rich diet and found them consistent with a single 9th century date, making the link to the army very likely.

**Torksey** in Lincolnshire tells a different story. Metal-detecting surveys there had logged over 1,500 objects by 2016, including more than 300 coins, over 100 of them fragments of Arabic dirhams, more than 100 weights, hacksilver and hundreds of lead gaming pieces: the debris of weighing, trading and killing time through a long winter. Together, the two sites confirm what the Chronicle only hints at: the army was a mobile society with its own economy, and women travelled with it.

## The Army Splits: Halfdan in the North, Guthrum in the South

At Repton the army drove out King Burgred of Mercia and installed a ruler who owed his position to the Vikings. Then, in 874, it divided. **Halfdan** took his men north, and in 876, the Chronicle notes, he "shared out the land" of the Northumbrians, whose new owners began ploughing and supporting themselves - the earliest direct record of the army turning into settlers. Halfdan's own rule was short: he is generally thought to have died at Strangford Lough in Ireland in 877. **Guthrum**, Oscetel and Anwend led the second band to Cambridge, then struck at Wessex itself, occupying Wareham in 876 and Exeter in 877.

## Was the Great Heathen Army Repelled? Chippenham and Edington

The short answer is: from Wessex, yes; from England, no.

In January 878, just after Twelfth Night, Guthrum's army fell on the royal estate of **Chippenham** in a midwinter surprise attack. Alfred escaped with a small following into the marshes of Somerset and built a stronghold at **Athelney**. In early May he summoned the levies of Somerset, Wiltshire and Hampshire and beat the Danes at **Edington** in Wiltshire. The survivors held out for about two weeks before suing for peace, offering hostages and swearing to leave the kingdom.

Around three weeks after the battle, Guthrum was baptised at Aller in Somerset with Alfred as his sponsor and took the Christian name **Æthelstan**. In 879 his army moved to Cirencester and the following year settled in East Anglia, where he ruled as a Christian king until his death around 890.

Edington saved Wessex and ended the war of conquest, but it did not undo the conquest. Northumbria, East Anglia and eastern Mercia stayed in Scandinavian hands, and a later treaty between Alfred and Guthrum turned that into a negotiated border.

## FAQ

### How big was the Great Heathen Army?

No contemporary source gives a number. Estimates range from under a thousand men, based on ship counts, to several thousand, based on the 26-hectare winter camp at Torksey. Most historians settle on several hundred to the low thousands of warriors, plus camp followers.

### Who led the Great Heathen Army?

Contemporary English sources name Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan, and Ubba appears in sources from the late 10th century onward. From the mid-870s Guthrum led the southern part of the army. The idea that they were sons of Ragnar Lodbrok comes from much later texts.

### Was the Great Heathen Army repelled?

Only from Wessex. Alfred the Great defeated Guthrum's army at Edington in May 878 and forced it to leave his kingdom. By then the army had already conquered Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia, which remained under Scandinavian control as the Danelaw.

### How long did the Great Heathen Army stay in England?

As a campaigning army, from 865 to 878, moving between winter camps almost every year. Parts of it never left: Halfdan's men settled in Northumbria from 876, and Guthrum's in East Anglia around 880.

## The Legacy: The Danelaw and Permanent Settlement

Out of Edington, the treaties that followed and the settlements of the 870s emerged the so-called **Danelaw**, a vast region in eastern and northern England where Danish law and Danish customs applied, and where large numbers of Scandinavian settlers put down permanent roots. Unlike earlier raids, the Great Heathen Army ultimately aimed not just at plunder but at land itself - a fact still visible today in hundreds of English place names with Scandinavian endings like "-by" or "-thorpe," found in towns like Derby, Whitby, and Scunthorpe.

Our take: what sets the Great Heathen Army apart from earlier Viking raids isn't its brutality, but its staying power. It targeted entire kingdoms, negotiated when it paid off, and settled the moment military success made lasting control possible. If you want to understand how feared raiders like the [berserkers](/en/blog/viking-berserkers/) eventually became settled lords of the land, this decades-long campaign is one of the most revealing examples from the entire Viking Age.
