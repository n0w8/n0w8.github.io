**Kaupang**, on the Viksfjord near Larvik in southern Norway, was the country's first town: founded around 800 AD, it was lived in year-round until about 930, and the 1999-2002 excavations alone recovered more than 100,000 finds. The name says what the place was for. Old Norse *kaupangr* means a trading place, built from *kaup* ("buying, trade") and probably *angr* ("bay").

Modern archaeology at Kaupang owes a surprising debt to a winter supply of turnips. In 1947 Astri and Hans Christian Jahnsen, the siblings who farmed Nordre Kaupang, dug a storage pit on the low hill of Bikjholberget and turned up five bronze brooches and a sword. The finds went to the University of Oslo's antiquities collection, and within a few years one of Norway's longest-running digs had begun.

That makes Kaupang Norway's answer to [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/) on the Schlei and [Birka](/en/blog/birka-swedens-first-town/) on Lake Mälaren. It was smaller than either, but it probably appears in a travel account written down at an English king's court around 890.

## Where Is Kaupang? The Bay Behind Lamøya

Kaupang lies in the former municipality of Tjølling, now part of Larvik, about five to six kilometres northeast of the town centre. The settlement ran along the shore of a bay called **Kaupangkilen**, part of the Viksfjord, which branches off the Larviksfjord near the mouth of the Oslofjord. Lamøya, east of the bay, sheltered the harbour from wind and weather, and Jutland was just a crossing of the Skagerrak away.

Stand there today and you look at pasture, not water. The land has risen since the Viking Age, and the shoreline of the time lay about 3.5 metres higher than today's. The town was a narrow strip along that old beach, at its largest roughly 750 metres long and 20 to 90 metres wide, about 5.4 hectares in all.

## When Was Kaupang Founded? Tree Rings and Town Plots

The timber gives the sharpest dates. A tree-ring study by the National Museum of Denmark (Niels Bonde, NNU Rapport 9, 2001) found that posts from around the so-called House I were felled in the summer of 803. A well lined with a log-built timber box, one of the earliest examples of the technique in Norway, dates to the summer of 808, and another well to around 814. On current evidence, Kaupang was laid out around 800 on land that had not been settled before.

Was Kaupang just a summer market or a genuine town? The excavations led by Dagfinn Skre settled it: houses stood on plots of roughly 6 by 8 to 12 metres, divided by paths, fences and ditches, and they had hearths. That points to people living there all year round rather than a seasonal fair, although the fringes of the site may only have been used part of the time.

A typical house was small, with two pairs of roof-bearing posts, a central hearth and an earth floor, and some look strikingly like houses from Viking Age Dublin. How many people lived here is disputed. The Norwegian history portal norgeshistorie.no reckons on an average of about 200 inhabitants, the encyclopedia Store norske leksikon on around 500. Farmers in the surrounding countryside kept them fed with grain and beef.

## What Did Ohthere Tell King Alfred About Sciringes Heal?

Around 890 a northern Norwegian called **Ohthere** (Ottar in Norwegian), whose home in Hålogaland may have been near modern Tromsø, described his voyages to his lord, **King Alfred of Wessex**. His account was added to the Old English version of Orosius's world history. The oldest copy was written in Wessex between about 892 and 925 and is now in the British Library.

In the south of the country, Ohthere said, there was a port called **Sciringes heal**. Sailing there, you would have land on your port side the whole way, land he calls *norðweg*. From Sciringes heal he sailed to Hedeby, Old English *æt Hæþum*, in five days: three with Denmark to port and open sea to starboard, then two more with Jutland, Sillende and many islands to starboard. This passage contains *dena mearc*, the earliest known written mention of Denmark, and *norðweg* is often counted among the earliest written forms of the name Norway, the "north way" whose name this blog also carries.

> "Þyder he cwæð þæt man ne mihte geseglian on anum monðe gyf man on niht wicode and ælce dæge hæfde ambyrne wind."
>
> He said that one could not sail there in a month, if one camped at night and had a fair wind every day.

Taken literally, the coastal voyage took more than a month. But Ohthere is speaking generally ("one") rather than about his own trip, and *ambyrne* occurs nowhere else in Old English: most scholars render it "favourable", a handful "contrary".

So is Sciringes heal really Kaupang? Since at least 1850, when the historian Peter Andreas Munch made the case, scholars have equated it with **Skiringssal**, the old name for the district around Tjølling. Archaeology has backed that reading, and Dagfinn Skre accepts it, but it is not proven: the name survives only in its Old English form. The historian Kjell-Olav Masdalen and others look for Sciringes heal west of Lindesnes instead, perhaps at Lunde on the Lista peninsula. The same text goes straight on to the report of one Wulfstan, who sailed from Hedeby to Truso on the Baltic in seven days and nights.

## Skiringssal: The Royal Hall at Huseby

Skiringssal also turns up in the Icelandic sagas, and 15th-century documents place farms in Tjølling "i Skirisall". In 1986 the place-name scholar Kåre Hoel suggested that Skiringssal was the older name of the farm **Huseby**, about a kilometre north of Kaupang. The name is often read as "the shining hall".

In 1999 Skre's team spotted a levelled terrace on the hill of Husebyhaugen and excavated it in the summer of 2000. A large hall had been built in the second half of the 8th century on top of an older burial mound from the 4th or 5th century, and it stood throughout the 9th century. It was a little over 30 metres long and around 10 to 11.5 metres wide, depending on the reconstruction. Halls on artificial terraces were known from Sweden, at Gamla Uppsala, Helgö and Hovgården near Birka, but this was the first found in Norway. From the hall you look straight down on the town and the bay.

## Who Excavated Kaupang? From Nicolaysen to Skre

The first excavations came in 1867, when the antiquarian **Nicolay Nicolaysen** opened 79 burial mounds on the Kaupang farms. Most held Viking Age cremations, mainly of men and many with weapons. The same Nicolaysen went on to uncover the nearby [Gokstad ship](/en/blog/gokstad-ship/) in 1880, and in 1902 Gabriel Gustafson, who would later dig the Oseberg ship, opened a few mounds on Lamøya.

Modern research begins with **Charlotte Blindheim** (1917-2005), a niece of the Nobel Prize-winning novelist Sigrid Undset, who in 1946 became the first woman on the permanent academic staff of the University of Oslo's museum. From 1950 she dug every summer at Bikjholberget and on Lamøya, and by 1957 she had investigated 77 graves. Then she noticed the unusually dark soil in the field west of the cemetery, much like the "Black Earth" at Birka. Until 1967, with one more season in 1974, she exposed parts of the settlement itself. Her full publication only appeared in 1999.

**Dagfinn Skre** had taken over the project in 1997. After survey work from 1999, his team excavated the settlement from 2000 to 2002 and the harbour in 2003. The results came out in three volumes: *Kaupang in Skiringssal* (2007), *Means of Exchange* (2008) and *Things from the Town* (2011).

## What Was Made and Traded at Kaupang?

According to norgeshistorie.no, glass bead makers and jewellery casters were among the first to settle. Blacksmiths, amber workers and textile workers followed, and much of what they made was produced in series for sale.

![Two crucibles, metal ingots, lumps of lead and a soapstone mould against a dark background](/images/kaupang-4.jpg)
*Tools and waste from a workshop: crucibles, a brass ingot and a lead ingot, lumps of lead and a soapstone casting mould. Photo: Eirik Irgens Johnsen, Kulturhistorisk museum, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

The imports read like a map of the [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/): drinking vessels from the Frankish realm, pottery from the Rhineland, Frisia and the Slavic lands of the Baltic, glass beads from Byzantium and slate from western Norway. Most of the coins are Arabic dirhams, with some Frankish and Anglo-Saxon pieces alongside. People paid in silver by weight, which is why weights turn up among the finds. Going the other way were Norwegian goods such as iron and soapstone, and perhaps fish. Skre has even argued that Frankish traders settled in the town ("From Dorestad to Kaupang", 2010).

## Kaupang's Cemeteries and Boat Graves

Several cemeteries ringed the settlement. The archaeologist Frans-Arne Stylegar estimates that Kaupang and its neighbouring farms once held as many as 1,000 graves. Nicolaysen's mounds mostly covered cremated men buried with weapons. Bikjholberget looks different: there the dead were mostly buried unburnt under flat ground with no mound, a good number of them in boats, and men and women in roughly equal numbers.

Blindheim took this as a sign that the people buried here lived differently from Vestfold's farmers, by trade and craft. Stylegar thinks around 80 graves may still lie untouched at Bikjholberget.

## Kaupang vs Hedeby, Birka and Ribe

| Trading Town | Location | Period | Distinguishing Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaupang | Viksfjord near Larvik, Norway | c. 800-930, traces until c. 970 | Lived in year-round, royal hall within sight |
| Hedeby | Schlei, Schleswig-Holstein | 8th-11th c. | Baltic-North Sea land bridge, Ohthere's destination |
| Birka | Björkö, Lake Mälaren, Sweden | 8th-10th c. | Central Sweden's leading trading centre |
| Ribe | Southwest Jutland, Denmark | from c. 704-710 | Scandinavia's oldest marketplace |

Who founded Kaupang? Blindheim credited the local kings of Vestfold. Skre thinks it more likely that the king of the Danes, then the dominant power in south-western Scandinavia, was behind it. The Royal Frankish Annals offer a clue: under the year 813 they record that the Danish kings marched with an army into "Westarfolda", that is Vestfold, because its leaders and people refused to submit to them. Norgeshistorie.no therefore describes Kaupang as most likely a border town in the Danish king's realm.

## Why Was Kaupang Abandoned?

Around 930 town life ended fairly abruptly, with only scattered activity until roughly 960 to 980. Nobody knows exactly why. Blindheim blamed the rising land and the silting of the bay, which left the harbour unusable. Skre points to politics: Danish overlordship of Vestfold faded in the first half of the 10th century, and with it, probably, the town's reason to exist. Later ploughing destroyed the youngest layers, so the answer may never be clear. Birka and Hedeby went the same way, succeeded by Sigtuna and Schleswig.

## FAQ

### Where is Kaupang?

In Tjølling, Larvik municipality, Vestfold, about five to six kilometres northeast of Larvik town centre, on the bay of Kaupangkilen off the Viksfjord.

### When was Kaupang founded?

Around 800 AD. Tree-ring dates show timber for one building was felled in the summer of 803 and for a well in the summer of 808. The town was lived in year-round until about 930.

### Was Kaupang really Norway's first town?

Kaupang is the oldest known town in Norway. The 2000-2002 excavations revealed plots, paths and houses with hearths, pointing to a permanent settlement rather than a seasonal market.

### Is Kaupang the same as Skiringssal?

Skiringssal was the name of the district and probably of the royal hall at Huseby, while Kaupang was the trading town within it. That Ohthere's Sciringes heal refers to this Skiringssal is the usual reading, but it has not been proven.

### Can you visit Kaupang?

Yes. The protected site is now sheep pasture. At Bikjholberget an information board and a relief model explain the layout, and a small museum opens in summer, otherwise by appointment. The finds are held by the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo.

## Our Take

Kaupang tends to be filed away as Hedeby's and Birka's little brother, and that sells it short. Tree rings, town plots and a written account from Wessex meet here unusually closely. Most intriguing to us is the argument over who the town belonged to: if Skre is right, Norway's first town was not a Norwegian project at all, but an outpost on the edge of Danish power. Not a bad twist for a country whose name probably makes its first written appearance in the very same account.
