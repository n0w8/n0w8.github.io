**Viking music** survives without a single written note: the oldest secular melody known from the Nordic countries sits on the last page of the Codex Runicus, copied around 1300, more than two centuries after the Viking Age (roughly 793 to 1066) ended. Everything else comes from lyre bridges and bone flutes, travellers' notes and sagas written down long afterwards. One of those travellers, Ibrahim ibn Yaqub al-Tartushi, heard the people of [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/) sing around 965 and was not impressed. In paraphrase: he had never heard uglier singing, a rumbling from the throat like a dog's, only more bestial.

It is a rude remark, and an invaluable one, because it is among the very few moments where someone in the tenth century tells us what they actually heard. The rest is a jigsaw with half the pieces missing.

## What Did Viking Music Sound Like? The Short Answer

The honest answer has three parts. First, the Vikings certainly had music: instruments turn up in the ground, and sagas and outsiders mention singing at feasts, rituals and funerals. Second, no melody survives, because nobody wrote one down. Third, much of what is sold today as the "Viking sound", from war drums to throat singing, is modern interpretation.

What we can say with some confidence is that lyres and flutes were small and fairly quiet, made for a hall or a farmyard, while horns did the loud work. Nothing points to part-singing in the Viking Age. Six kinds of evidence get us that far:

| Clue | Example | What it tells us | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instrument finds | Ribe lyre (8th c.), Birka lyre bridge, York panpipe | Which notes were possible | No melodies; most wood has rotted |
| Outside witnesses | al-Tartushi (c. 965), Ibn Fadlan (921-922), Adam of Bremen (c. 1075) | That people sang, and where | Hostile outsiders' view |
| Eddas and sagas | Odin's spell-songs, Gudrid's chant, Gunnar's harp | What music meant in myth and life | Written down from the 13th c. |

| Images | Hylestad stave church portal (12th c.) | What instruments looked like | Already Christian period |
| Later records | Codex Runicus (c. 1300), Icelandic rímur, Faroese chain dance | How verse may have been delivered | Centuries after the Vikings |

## Why Not a Single Note Survives

Viking-Age Scandinavia ran on memory. Runes were carved for names, memorial stones and short messages, and there was no system at all for writing down pitch. Songs, poems and even laws lived in people's heads.

Writing music down arrived with the Church. An early example with a Nordic connection is the two-part hymn "Nobilis, humilis" to St Magnus, Earl of Orkney, preserved in a thirteenth-century manuscript now in Uppsala.

The famous exception is secular. The **Codex Runicus** (AM 28 8vo), a vellum manuscript of about 1300 containing mainly the Scanian Law, is written entirely in runes, long after the Latin alphabet had taken over, most likely out of deliberate nostalgia. On its final page, two staves of notation sit above a line of verse: "Drømde mik en drøm i nat um silki ok ærlik pæl", I dreamt a dream last night of silk and fine fur. It is regarded as the oldest known secular song in the Nordic countries.

The pitches are mostly agreed on. The rhythm is not, because the notation gives no note lengths. Danes know the tune anyway: Danmarks Radio used it for many years as an interval signal between programmes.

So the oldest Nordic tune anyone can sing today is some 230 years younger than the Battle of Stamford Bridge, and whether it says anything about the Vikings is anyone's guess.

## What Outsiders Heard: Hedeby, the Volga and Uppsala

The earliest descriptions of Norse singing come from people who disliked it.

**Al-Tartushi in Hedeby (c. 965).** Ibrahim ibn Yaqub, a traveller from the Caliphate of Córdoba, toured central and western Europe in the mid-tenth century. His account survives only in excerpts by later scholars, among them the cosmographer al-Qazwini in the thirteenth century. Our reading of the famous line is modest: a low, rough sound, heard by a man whose ears were tuned to the music of al-Andalus. Nothing more.

**Ibn Fadlan on the Volga (921-922).** Travelling with an embassy from the caliph in Baghdad, Ibn Fadlan watched the ship funeral of a Rus chieftain. The enslaved girl chosen to die with him sings over the cups she is handed. When she is killed in the tent, men beat their shields with sticks so that her screams cannot be heard. That is ritual noise, not music, but it is the clearest early mention of anything like percussion. How closely the Volga Rus resembled Scandinavians at home is debated.

**Adam of Bremen on Uppsala (c. 1075).** The Bremen canon describes the great sacrificial feast at Uppsala and adds, roughly, that the songs sung at such rites are many and indecent, so better left unmentioned. Adam knew Uppsala only by report and wrote as a chronicler of the northern mission. What survives his disapproval is the fact that cult and song went together.

## What Instruments Did the Vikings Play?

Finds are scarce because wood, horn, bone and gut rarely survive. The key pieces are below, with the full story in our guide to [Viking instruments](/en/blog/viking-instruments/):

- **Lyre:** the main stringed instrument. Excavations at Ribe in Denmark, announced in 2018, produced part of a lyre with six tuning pegs from a layer of the first half of the eighth century. Loose bridges are known too, such as a horn bridge from Birka's "Black Earth" and an amber one from a grave at Broa on Gotland.
- **Bone flute:** a tenth-century bone flute from Birka can still be played. Bone flutes are among the most common musical finds of all.
- **Panpipe:** the Coppergate excavations in York (1976 to 1981) produced a tenth-century boxwood panpipe, its pipes bored into a single block.
- **Horn and lur:** cow horns with finger holes served for signalling and herding. The Oseberg burial of 834 held a wooden tube just over a metre long that is often read as a lur, though not with certainty.

Do not confuse these with the great bronze lurs: the famous Brudevælte lurs from Zealand were made around 800 to 700 BC, some fifteen centuries before the Vikings. One absence speaks volumes, too: not a single Viking-Age drum has been found.

The best-known complete lyre from the North is much later: the **Kravik lyre** from Numedal in Norway, long dated to the fourteenth century until a 2015 radiocarbon test showed it was made after 1475, probably after 1556. Many stage lyres today are modelled on it.

## Songs, Spells and Horns in the Eddas and Sagas

Old Norse literature is full of sound and almost never describes it. In **Hávamál**, part of the Poetic Edda, Odin lists eighteen *ljóð*, songs or charms: against grief and sickness, against fetters, against fire in the hall. The word for magic itself, *galdr*, is related to the verb *gala*, to sing or cry out. In the North, magic was something you sang.

The loveliest scene comes from the thirteenth-century **Eirik the Red's Saga**. In a famine winter in Greenland, the seeress Thorbjorg needs *varðlokkur*, songs that draw the spirits close, before she can prophesy. Only Gudrid knows them, learned as a child from her foster mother. A Christian, she refuses at first, then sings so beautifully that the spirits come.

Horns appear as signals. In Vatnsdæla saga they summon men to battle, and in Völuspá Heimdall blows the Gjallarhorn to announce the end of the world. Then there is Gunnar, the Burgundian king of the Nibelung legend, thrown into a snake pit by Atli. Bound, he plays the harp, with his toes in some versions, until the snakes fall asleep, all but one. In the later twelfth century the scene was carved into the portal of Hylestad stave church in Setesdal (our header image), now in the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo. Norway's encyclopedia Store norske leksikon notes that such depictions show a lyre, and that Old Norse *harpa* probably covered both instruments.

String playing carried status: Rögnvaldr Kali Kolsson, Earl of Orkney from 1129 to 1158, lists nine accomplishments in Orkneyinga saga, from board games and runes to harp playing and poetry. Yet people rarely sing in the Icelandic family sagas, whose authors cared about feuds, not about how the evening sounded.

## Skalds: When Poetry Was the Real Music

If you want to know how the Vikings sounded, start with their words. The art they prized most was **skaldic poetry**: praise poems, memorial verse and insults in metres such as *dróttkvætt*, with fixed syllable counts, alliteration, internal rhyme and kennings, those elaborate poetic periphrases. Myth explained the gift as stolen goods: Odin took the [mead of poetry](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/) out of a giant's mountain.

**Egil's Saga** shows what a poem could be worth. Shipwrecked in Northumbria, the Icelander Egill Skallagrímsson ends up in York before his enemy, King Eirik Bloodaxe. Advised by his friend Arinbjorn, he spends the night on a twenty-stanza praise poem, *Höfuðlausn* or "Head-Ransom", in an end-rhymed metre unusual for skaldic verse. He performs it in the morning and keeps his head.

How he performed it, nobody knows. The Old Norse verb used, *kveða*, means to say, speak or recite. Whether skalds spoke, chanted or sang, and whether a lyre ever joined in, remains one of the big open questions, explored in our article on the [skalds, the Viking poets](/en/blog/skalds-viking-poets/).

Two living traditions offer hints. Icelanders performed *rímur*, long rhymed narrative poems, well into the twentieth century in a style halfway between speech and song, also called *kveða*, and the Reykjavík society Iðunn (founded 1929) began recording the tunes in the mid-1930s. Faroese dancers still sing ballads of hundreds of verses in the chain dance, with no instruments at all. Neither tradition reaches back to the Viking Age, but both show how verse sounds when the words call the tune.

## Three Myths: War Drums, Throat Singing and the Viking Clap

Stream a "Viking music" playlist and you will usually hear the same three ingredients. None of them comes from the sources.

### Myth 1: The Thundering War Drums

No drum has been found from the Viking Age, although a study of Viking-Age Scania thinks some kind of drum probably existed, since wood and hide rot. The Sámi frame drums often used as a model survive mostly from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the nearest thing in the record is Ibn Fadlan's shield-beating. For battle cries and fighting songs, see our piece on [Viking war chants](/en/blog/viking-war-chants/).

### Myth 2: Throat Singing

The overtone singing of Tuva and Mongolia, brought to the stage by bands such as Heilung, is not attested anywhere in Scandinavia. Al-Tartushi's "rumbling from the throat" gets quoted as proof, but it records an impression, not a technique. Rough and low, perhaps; throat singing in the modern sense remains a hypothesis.

### Myth 3: The Viking Clap

The slow, collective clap with its booming "Huh!", made famous by Iceland's fans at Euro 2016, is about a thousand years younger than the Vikings. By consistent accounts, supporters of the Icelandic club Stjarnan picked it up in July 2014 from fans of Scotland's Motherwell, whom Stjarnan played in a Europa League qualifier.

Such images have a pedigree that starts, of all places, in music: Wagner's Ring cycle at the first Bayreuth Festival in 1876 made the horned helmet famous, as our article on the [horned helmet myth](/en/blog/viking-horned-helmet-myth/) explains.

## Modern Viking Music: What Became of It

Modern Viking music started in the rehearsal room, not the museum. The Swedish band Bathory turned the Vikings into a metal subject with Blood Fire Death (1988) and Hammerheart (1990) and is usually credited with founding Viking metal. Einar Selvik took another route, founding **Wardruna** in Bergen in 2003; once he joined the score for season two of the TV series Vikings in 2014, that sound went worldwide. The same year **Heilung** formed in Copenhagen, calling its music "amplified history from early medieval northern Europe".

On those stages the centuries blur: deer-hide frame drums with no Viking-Age find behind them, lyres modelled on the early modern Kravik lyre, lurs of the Bronze Age type. That is not a criticism. It is art drawing on two and a half millennia of northern history, often magnificent, but not a field recording from the ninth century.

## FAQ

### What did Viking music sound like?

Nobody knows exactly, because no melody from the Viking Age was written down. The finds point to lyres with around six strings, bone flutes, panpipes and horns. The traveller al-Tartushi described singing in Hedeby around 965 as a rumbling from the throat.

### Did Vikings have music?

Yes. Archaeologists have found lyre parts, bone flutes and a panpipe from the eighth to tenth centuries, and eyewitnesses such as Ibn Fadlan and al-Tartushi mention singing.

### What instruments did the Vikings play?

The evidence covers lyres, bone flutes, panpipes like the one from York, and horns of cow horn or wood, but no drums. Our guide to [Viking instruments](/en/blog/viking-instruments/) walks through each find.

### Are there any surviving Viking songs?

The words, yes; the tunes, no. Eddic poems and skaldic stanzas survive as texts without their melodies. The oldest notated secular melody from the North, "Drømde mik en drøm i nat", appears around 1300 in the Codex Runicus.

### Did Vikings use drums?

Possibly, but there is no evidence: no drum has been found from the Viking Age. The closest thing is Ibn Fadlan's account of men beating their shields with sticks at a funeral on the Volga.

### Is Wardruna real Viking music?

Not in the strict sense. Wardruna, founded in Bergen in 2003, writes new music with historical and historically inspired instruments and old texts: Nordic folk, an interpretation rather than a reconstruction.

## Our Take: The Silence Is More Honest Than the Soundtrack

The gap is not a weakness in this subject; it is the most important thing it tells us. For the Vikings, music was carried in memory and made in the moment: a seeress's chant, a horn across the farmyard, a stanza before a king. A culture that preferred speaking its knowledge to writing it down was never going to leave sheet music.

So when you listen to Wardruna, Heilung or Domsgard, you are hearing what our own age imagines the North sounded like. That is legitimate and often beautiful. It is just worth knowing the difference, the way it is worth knowing that the horned helmet comes from Bayreuth, not Birka.

The best evidence may still be that rude sentence from Hedeby. Somebody there, around 965, sang roughly enough that a visitor from al-Andalus wrote it down. We do not know how it sounded. We do know that it sounded.
