The **Viking instruments** archaeology can actually prove are the lyre, bone flutes and horns: Birka has produced a horn lyre bridge notched for seven strings, Hedeby and Birka have yielded bone flutes, and the Oseberg ship burial of 834 held a wooden lur 106.5 centimetres long. Add iron rattles, small bells and, at the very end of the period, the first reed pipes. It is a much quieter line-up than film soundtracks suggest, and much of what passes for "Viking sound" today is either up to 2,000 years older or several centuries younger.

Why so little? Instruments were made of wood, gut, horn and hide, the materials that rot first. What survives are the small hard parts: a bridge, a tuning key, a bone with holes in it. For singing, skalds and what foreign travellers made of Norse music, see our overview of [Viking music](/en/blog/viking-music/). This piece sticks to the instruments and how solid the evidence for each one is.

## What Instruments Did Vikings Play? The Short Answer

For Viking Age Scandinavia, roughly 793 to 1066, four groups are securely attested:

- **Lyres**, known from bridges of amber and horn and from a rune-inscribed tuning key
- **Bone flutes** with a handful of finger holes, from Hedeby and Birka among other sites
- **Horns and wooden lurs**, used for signalling
- **Iron-ring rattles** and small bells, whose purpose is not always clear

Uncertain or disputed: animal horns with finger holes, early bowed instruments, and reed pipes that only turn up in the eleventh century. Not attested at all: drums, Jew's harps, the tagelharpa and the hurdy-gurdy. And the great bronze lurs belong to another age entirely.

## The Viking Lyre: What Actually Comes From Scandinavia

The main stringed instrument of the Germanic-speaking world was the **lyre**, often called the round lyre: a narrow, flat wooden body with two arms joined by a yoke that holds the tuning pegs. The awkward part is that the best-preserved lyres are not Viking at all:

- The **Trossingen lyre** comes from grave 58 of an Alamannic cemetery in south-west Germany, excavated in 2001/02. Tree-ring dating of the chamber timbers puts the burial in 580. Made of maple, it is almost complete, shows wear from playing and is in the Archaeological State Museum of Baden-Württemberg in Konstanz.
- The **Sutton Hoo lyre** belongs to the Anglo-Saxon ship burial in Mound 1 in Suffolk, excavated in 1939 and dated to around 625. Its fragments are in the British Museum. The first reconstruction, in 1948, made it a harp; only later was it recognised as a lyre.

Both are around two centuries older than the raid on Lindisfarne and neither is Scandinavian, but they show how the lyres played in the North were built.

![Incised bearded warriors with spears and round shields on the wood of the Trossingen lyre](/images/viking-instruments-2.jpg)
*Detail of the Alamannic Trossingen lyre (c. 580): warriors with spears and shields incised into the maple body. Archaeological State Museum of Baden-Württemberg, Konstanz. Photo: Wolfgang Sauber, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

From Scandinavia itself, almost only the durable small parts survive:

- **Ribe, Denmark:** In 2018 the Northern Emporium excavations turned up a piece of a lyre with tuning pegs in a layer from the first half of the eighth century. Aarhus University calls it the oldest stringed instrument ever found in Denmark. Strictly speaking it is just pre-Viking.
- **Broa, Gotland:** The horseman's grave found in 1899, whose bridle mounts gave the Broa style its name, contained a lyre bridge of amber from around 800.
- **Birka, Sweden:** The "Black Earth" of the trading town produced a horn lyre bridge just 4.9 centimetres wide, notched for seven strings, now in the Swedish History Museum in Stockholm and shown in the header image.
- **Sigtuna, Sweden:** In October 1999 archaeologists found a tuning key of elk antler, just under 13 centimetres long, in a layer from around 1100. Its runes call the instrument a *harpa*; the runologist Helmer Gustavson reads them roughly as a man named Erri having made this harp with skilful hands. It is the earliest Scandinavian record of the word.

A harp on a lyre's tuning key is less of a contradiction than it sounds. Gustavson shows that *harpa* could cover harps, lyres and other plucked instruments in the North. The pictures agree: the twelfth-century baptismal font from Norum in Bohuslän, the oldest image of Gunnar in the snake pit, shows the bound hero playing a round lyre with his feet.

> In Old Norse texts the lyre is almost always called a *harpa*. When a saga mentions a harp, picture a lyre.

No complete Viking Age lyre has ever been found. The surviving **Kravik lyre** from Numedal in Norway, now in the Norsk Folkemuseum in Oslo, was long thought to be medieval, but a radiocarbon sample taken in 2015 showed it was made after 1475, and probably after 1556.

## Bone Flutes, Panpipes and the First Reed Pipes

The commonest instrument in the archaeological record is also the simplest: an animal bone with finger holes and a fipple at the top, working much like a recorder. From [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/), the great trading town on the Schlei, the archaeologist Christine Brade published a whole group of these **bone flutes** in 1978. Another well-known flute, with three finger holes, comes from Birka and is dated to the tenth century. Three holes do not give much range, but plenty for a herding call or a dance tune.

A small **panpipe** of boxwood came out of the Coppergate excavations in York (1976 to 1981), dated to the tenth century, when the city was Anglo-Scandinavian. Its pipes are holes of different depths bored into a single block of wood. You will sometimes read about a panpipe from Hedeby; we could not find one in the specialist literature.

At the very end of the period come the first **reed pipes** in the North. The Lund pipe, found in 1972 and dated to between 1020 and 1200, is about 20 centimetres long, made of elder, with four finger holes; whether it belonged to a hornpipe or a bagpipe is unresolved. A similar pipe from a Slavic-influenced shipyard on Falster dates to around 1050 to 1100.

## Horns and Wooden Lurs

The Old Norse word *lúðr* meant a wind instrument of wood or horn. In the sagas lurs are blown to call men to battle, as in Vatnsdæla saga, and in Völuspá Heimdallr sounds his horn as the end of the world begins.

The key find is from the Oseberg burial of 834: a tube 106.5 centimetres long, made from two hollowed halves of black alder and once held together by five rings. It lay in an iron-bound oak chest and was first catalogued in 1904 as a sheath for a stick. Most researchers now read it as a **wooden lur**, as the Norwegian encyclopedia Store norske leksikon puts it. Not everyone agrees: the same chest held textile tools, and a staff remains possible. The object belongs to the Museum of Cultural History at the University of Oslo.

With animal horns the evidence thins out. A cow horn with four finger holes from Västerby in Sweden, now in the Dalarnas museum in Falun, is often presented as Viking Age, but we could not find a secure date for it, so we list it as possible rather than proven.

## Rattles, Bells and Jingles

The Oseberg burial also held five iron **rattles**, *rangle* in Norwegian: large rings with smaller rings that jingle against them. According to the Museum of Cultural History, each rattle belonged with one of the carved animal-head posts from the grave. Rattles like these are known from a few hundred Viking Age graves, often alongside horse gear.

![Iron rattle made of large and small rings with two decorated handles from the Oseberg burial](/images/viking-instruments-3.jpg)
*Iron rattle (rangle) from the Oseberg burial of 834, Museum of Cultural History, Oslo. Photo: Peulle, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

Whether they count as musical instruments is open. The excavators Haakon Shetelig and Gabriel Gustafson pictured them as a ritual barrier; a 2024 thesis suggests strapping devices on sleighs. Either way they jingled. Small bells belong here too, such as a bell from Hedeby shown in the 1992 exhibition catalogue "From Viking to Crusader".

## Four Myths About Viking Instruments

### Did Vikings Use War Drums?

Not a single drum is known from Viking Age Scandinavia, either as a find or as a clear reference in the texts. Wood and hide decay quickly, so absence is not proof, but there is no evidence. The closest thing is Ibn Fadlan's account of the Rus on the Volga in 921/922: at a funeral, men beat their shields with sticks so that the screams of a slave girl being killed would not be heard. That is noise, not music.

The **frame drum** on so many "Viking" stages today comes from Sámi tradition. Historia Norwegie describes a Sámi drum in the late twelfth century, but the 70 to 80 Sámi drums that survive were mostly collected or confiscated in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

### Are Bronze Lurs Viking Horns?

The curving **bronze lurs**, up to two metres long, are the most spectacular wind instruments in the North, and they date from the Bronze Age, roughly 1300 to 600 BC. Around 60 whole or partial lurs are known, most from Denmark, often deposited in pairs in bogs. The six lurs from the bog at Brudevælte on Zealand were found in 1797 and are dated to about 800 to 700 BC.

![Curving Bronze Age lur with a round decorated bell disc on a white background](/images/viking-instruments-4.jpg)
*Bronze lur from the bog at Brudevælte, Late Bronze Age, National Museum of Denmark: about fifteen centuries older than the Vikings. Photo: Roberto Fortuna and Kira Ursem, Nationalmuseet, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

They only got their name in the nineteenth century, when Danish antiquarians borrowed the Old Norse *lúðr* for them. Between these lurs and the Vikings lie 1,500 to 2,000 years. It is the same shortcut that gave us the [Viking horned helmet](/en/blog/viking-horned-helmet-myth/): the famous horned helmets from Veksø are Bronze Age too.

### Was the Tagelharpa a Viking Instrument?

The **tagelharpa**, or talharpa, is a bowed lyre strung with horsehair, *tagel* in Swedish. It is documented in the folk music of the Estonian Swedes, above all on the island of Vormsi (Ormsö), where the Finland-Swedish musicologist Otto Andersson sought out players in 1903 and 1904. His dissertation appeared in 1923, the English version, "The Bowed Harp", in 1930. Its close cousin is the Finnish jouhikko.

The bow itself only becomes visible in Europe from the ninth and tenth centuries. Hedeby has produced a 43-centimetre piece of an alder-wood instrument from the tenth or eleventh century, published by Graeme Lawson in 1984, which some read as an early bowed instrument. That still does not put a bowed lyre in Viking Age Scandinavia.

The **hurdy-gurdy** and the nyckelharpa are younger still. One of the earliest images of the organistrum, the two-player ancestor of the hurdy-gurdy, is carved on the twelfth-century Pórtico da Gloria at Santiago de Compostela, and a relief at Källunge on Gotland from around 1350 may show early nyckelharpas. Both are high medieval.

### Did Vikings Play the Jew's Harp?

The Norwegian music archaeologist Gjermund Kolltveit surveyed the European Jew's harp finds in 2006. The oldest examples from datable contexts are from around 1200, the bulk are late medieval or later, and he calls the hints of Viking Jew's harps vague. Language adds a twist: according to Gustavson, the Old Norse *munnharpa*, literally "mouth harp", meant no instrument at all, but the cramped tightening of the lips in the cold.

## How Modern Bands Rebuild Viking Instruments

With no complete originals, every reconstruction starts from drawings and fragments. The playable Sutton Hoo copy in the British Museum was made from the museum's drawings in the Dolmetsch workshop at Haslemere. The Scandinavian finds leave more to guesswork: a bridge from Birka gives the number of strings, not the shape of the body.

A pioneer was the Swedish music archaeologist Cajsa Lund, whose 1987 record "Fornnordiska klanger / The Sounds of Prehistoric Scandinavia" features reconstructions including horse bells, lyre, panpipe, wooden lur and the Lund pipe. It sounds far sparer than any film score.

Bands take more liberties. Wardruna, founded in 2003 by Einar Selvik with Gaahl and Lindy-Fay Hella, uses deer-hide frame drums, flutes, the Kravik lyre, Jew's harp, goat horn and lur, plus the Finnish jouhikko. The result mixes periods and cultures, which is an artistic choice rather than a reconstruction. How that scene grew is the subject of our guide to [Nordic folk music from Wardruna to Heilung](/en/blog/nordic-folk-music-wardruna-heilung/).

## FAQ

### What instruments did Vikings play?

The archaeology points to lyres, bone flutes, horns and wooden lurs, plus rattles and small bells, with reed pipes appearing at the very end of the period. Drums, Jew's harps and bowed instruments are not attested in Viking Age Scandinavia.

### What did a Viking lyre look like?

Probably much like the round lyres from Trossingen and Sutton Hoo: a flat, elongated wooden body with two arms, a yoke and usually six or seven strings. From Scandinavia itself mostly bridges survive, in amber from Broa and in horn from Birka.

### Did Vikings use drums?

There is no evidence for it, neither a find nor a clear passage in the sources. Ibn Fadlan describes the Rus beating shields with sticks, nothing more. The frame drums used by modern bands come from Sámi tradition.

### Is the tagelharpa a Viking instrument?

No. The tagelharpa is documented as a folk instrument of the Estonian Swedes, studied above all by Otto Andersson from 1903. There is no secure find of a bowed instrument from Viking Age Scandinavia.

### Are lurs Viking instruments?

Wooden lurs like the one from the Oseberg burial of 834 are. The famous bronze lurs are not: they date from the Bronze Age, roughly 1300 to 600 BC, and were only given the name in the nineteenth century.

## Our Take: Quiet, Spare and Mostly Sung

Lay the finds side by side and the picture has little to do with pounding drums. Viking music, as far as we can grasp it, was quiet and personal: a lyre with six or seven strings, a bone flute with three holes, horns and lurs for signals across fjord and pasture. The loudest instrument was probably the human voice.

Our reading is that the lyre belonged to the hall, not the battlefield. Its find spots in rich graves and trading towns point that way, and so does Gunnar playing for his life in the snake pit. Not proof, but a far better fit than any war drum.

Which makes today's "Viking sound" not a fraud but a new creation, built from Bronze Age lurs, Sámi drums and Estonian-Swedish bowed lyres. Enjoy it. Just know which note comes from which millennium.
