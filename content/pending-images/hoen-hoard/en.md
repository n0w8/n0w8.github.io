In 1834, a farmhand digging a drainage ditch on the Nedre Hoen farm near Ovre Eiker, in the Norwegian county of Buskerud, struck something hard in the wet ground. It was a lead box, and inside it lay the **Hoen hoard**, still the largest single gold find of the Viking Age ever recovered in Norway. More than 2.5 kilograms of gold, worked into rings, beads, pendants and coins, had lain buried for close to a thousand years before a spade brought it back into the light.

What sets the Hoen hoard apart from other Viking Age deposits is not only its sheer quantity of precious metal. It is the mix of where that metal came from: Norwegian goldsmithing sitting alongside a Byzantine coin and several pendants derived from Carolingian coinage. A single hoard from a bog near Eiker tells a story of trade and raiding routes that stretched from Constantinople through the Frankish empire all the way to rural Buskerud.

## The find in the bog: what actually happened in 1834

The discovery is unusually well documented for a find of this age. A farmhand working for the landowner was digging a drainage ditch through damp pastureland when his spade hit a lead container. Packed tightly inside was the entire gold hoard. According to the surviving accounts, word of the find spread quickly, and the landowner's bailiffs seized the box to hand it over to the Crown, as Norwegian law required for treasure finds of this kind. Each of the labourers involved is said to have kept a single coin as an informal share before the rest went through official channels into the national collections.

That the hoard survived complete enough to be properly studied later is far from guaranteed. Many comparable nineteenth-century finds were broken up, sold off piece by piece, or melted down long before any museum or antiquarian heard of them. The Hoen hoard, aside from those few coins taken by the workmen, passed almost intact into the care of Norway's antiquities authorities, making it one of the best-preserved hoards of its kind.

## What is actually in the Hoen hoard

The modern scholarly study of the find, published as *The Hoen Hoard: A Viking Gold Treasure of the Ninth Century* and edited by Signe Horn Fuglesang and David M. Wilson, counts 206 to 207 individual pieces. Of these, 54 objects are gold or silver-gilt, 20 are coins or coin-pendants, and 132 are beads of glass and gold. The Norwegian reference work *Store norske leksikon* puts the total weight of the metal at over 2.5 kilograms, noting that one of the two massive neck-rings alone weighs more than a kilogram of gold.

Among the most striking pieces:

- **Two massive gold neck-rings**, one of which ranks among the heaviest single objects of Viking Age Norse goldsmithing known anywhere.
- **Roughly 20 finger-rings and arm-rings**, decorated with filigree and granulated surfaces, some in local Norwegian style, others closer to continental workshop traditions.
- **Filigree pendants and brooches**, built from fine twisted gold wire laid into rosettes and interlace patterns, a technique that demanded considerable craft skill.
- **132 beads** of gold and coloured glass, likely strung together as one or more necklaces.
- **20 coins and coin-pendants**, including one Byzantine piece and several imitations of Frankish coinage, most reworked with soldered loops so they could be worn as jewellery rather than spent.

## Byzantium and Francia: what the coin pendants reveal

Among the hoard's twenty coins and coin-pendants, one object stands out above the rest: a Byzantine piece, examined in its own chapter by David Buckton in the Fuglesang and Wilson volume. Byzantine gold was a genuine rarity in Viking Age Scandinavia, and its presence in a Norwegian bog find shows just how far the trade and tribute networks of the period actually reached, stretching across the Baltic, along the river systems of what is now Russia, and ultimately to Constantinople itself. A later, more direct Norse connection to the Byzantine world is told in our article on the [Varangian Guard](/en/blog/varangian-guard-byzantium/), where Norse warriors entered imperial service outright.

The larger share of the coin-pendants, however, traces back to the Carolingian Empire. Several pieces are imitations or direct reworkings of solidi originally struck under **Louis the Pious**, son of Charlemagne. The numismatic analysis in Kolbjorn Skaare's chapter places this group of coin-pendants firmly in the ninth century, close in time to similar Frisian imitation coinage that tends to turn up in hoards from the 870s and 880s. That these coins were not spent as currency but worn as jewellery, fitted with soldered loops, fits a recurring pattern of the Viking Age: foreign gold was rarely used as found. It was almost always translated into a local vocabulary of ornament.

> A single Byzantine coin and several Carolingian imitations buried in one Norwegian bog show that the Viking world, seen from Buskerud, already reached as far as the Bosphorus long before a single ship set out east.

This connection to long-distance trade is part of why the Hoen hoard receives so much scholarly attention. While many Norwegian Viking Age finds speak mainly to local craftsmanship, Hoen ties Norwegian goldsmithing directly to objects that had already travelled thousands of kilometres before ending up buried near Eiker.

## Why was it buried, and never reclaimed?

The hoard's deposition is dated by its youngest coins to the third quarter of the ninth century, with *Store norske leksikon* placing it roughly between 875 and 900 AD. Why such an enormous fortune disappeared into the ground at that point cannot be established with certainty, but the sheer scale of the find makes simple explanations unlikely.

A fortune of over two kilograms of pure gold was extraordinary even for the upper ranks of Viking Age society. It most likely belonged not to an ordinary farmer but to a significant family or a local power broker with access to far-reaching trade networks, or direct involvement in raiding. That the hoard was buried as a single sealed unit inside a lead box suggests careful, planned storage rather than a hurried escape from some immediate threat. Unlike many English silver hoards that cluster tightly around specific military events, Hoen has no comparably clear historical crisis to which its burial can be tied.

The more straightforward reading is that the Hoen hoard was simply meant as secure storage, a form of savings in a world without banks or vaults, where the exact location was known only to one person or a small circle. If that person died unexpectedly, or the family lost its standing, the gold simply stayed where it was. This is the same basic pattern found in other major hoards of the period, as our article on [Viking silver hoards](/en/blog/viking-silver-hoards/) explores in more depth, though that piece focuses on silver finds from England and the Baltic, a different landscape of hoarding from golden Hoen.

Some researchers have also raised the possibility of ritual deposition, where particularly valuable objects were deliberately taken out of circulation. Against a purely ritual reading, though, the find spot itself, an ordinary wetland with no obvious ceremonial marker, shows none of the clearer parallels seen at sites more confidently interpreted as votive offerings. The most likely explanation therefore remains the least dramatic one: a substantial fortune, carefully stored, whose owner never returned to recover it for reasons now lost to us.

## The Hoen hoard today: inside the Museum of Cultural History

Since its discovery, the Hoen hoard has belonged to the collection of the **Museum of Cultural History** (Kulturhistorisk museum) at the University of Oslo, where it remains one of the most significant single pieces in the national collection of Viking Age gold. The objects have not always stayed quietly on display, however. During the German occupation of Norway in the Second World War, parts of the country's gold collections, including items from Hoen, were moved out of Oslo for safekeeping, among other places to the Fagernes region, specifically to keep them out of the occupying authorities' hands.

The full scholarly publication of the find did not appear until 2006, more than 170 years after its discovery, as an international collaborative work edited by Signe Horn Fuglesang and David M. Wilson. That a find of this importance waited so long for complete academic treatment says something about the sheer amount of archaeological and numismatic detail work required to correctly identify, classify and contextualise 206 individual pieces, from local goldsmithing to Byzantine coinage.

Anyone looking at the Hoen hoard in Oslo today is therefore seeing more than gold. They are seeing the end point of a trade network that connected Norway through the Frankish empire to the edges of the Byzantine world, and an act of storage whose last owner never came back to claim it. Other richly furnished finds belonging to Norwegian elites, such as the [Oseberg ship](/en/blog/oseberg-ship/), show from a different angle just how much wealth individual Viking Age families in this region actually commanded.

## Conclusion: a bog find with global reach

Two centuries after its discovery, the Hoen hoard remains Norway's most impressive Viking Age gold find. Over 2.5 kilograms of gold, spread across rings, beads, brooches and coin-pendants, testifies to goldsmithing skill of the highest order and to connections that reached far beyond the hoard's own region. The Byzantine coin and the Carolingian imitation pendants in the find make clear that Viking Age Buskerud was never isolated, but part of a trade and raiding network running from Scandinavia all the way to the Bosphorus.

That the hoard resurfaced almost by chance during drainage work in 1834, and survived nearly intact ever since, is a stroke of luck for archaeology. It offers a rare, self-contained look at what a prominent Viking Age Norwegian family actually possessed, and it leaves us facing the same question that sits at the end of every great hoard find: why did nobody ever come back for it?
