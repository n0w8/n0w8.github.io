Walk through the narrow streets of modern Sigtuna on the shore of Lake Malaren today, and you are standing on the oldest continuously inhabited town ground in Sweden. While other Viking Age settlements such as Birka became silent excavation sites centuries ago, **Sigtuna** has lived on, without interruption, for more than a thousand years. The town was founded around 970 to 980 AD by King Erik the Victorious (Erik Segersall), not as another trading post, but as a royal power base on Lake Malaren from which a new, Christian-leaning Sweden would take shape. The story of this Sigtuna viking town is above all a story of royal authority, coinage and religious change, not of long-distance trade.

## A Founding with a Purpose: Erik the Victorious and the New Center of Power

Archaeological research consistently dates the laying out of Sigtuna to the 970s or 980s AD. Dendrochronological analysis of timber from the town center has even produced a precise building date for some of the earliest structures: 981 AD. The founder is traditionally identified as **King Erik the Victorious** (Erik Segersall), who was consolidating his power over the Lake Malaren region at the time. Unlike many older trading sites, Sigtuna did not grow organically out of a settlement of merchants and craftsmen. The excavated evidence instead points to a planned town from the very start: regular plots, a deliberate street layout, a place that was laid out from above rather than grown from below.

That marks a fundamental difference from its famous predecessor. Birka, on the island of Bjorko in the same Lake Malaren, was first and foremost a trading town, a hub for merchants moving between Scandinavia and the Islamic world. Sigtuna was something else from the outset: a fortified royal base through which Erik the Victorious sought to make his authority over Uppland and the entire Lake Malaren region visible and permanent. For more on the trading town that Sigtuna effectively succeeded, see our article on [Birka, Sweden's First Town](/en/blog/birka-swedens-first-town/).

The location itself was no accident. Sigtuna sits on a narrow, easily controlled inlet of Lake Malaren that guards access to the fertile hinterland of Uppland, the region from which the early Swedish kings drew much of their power, their warriors and their tribute. Whoever controlled this inlet effectively controlled access to the heartland of the early Swedish crown. Unlike Birka, whose island position mainly served to protect trade, Sigtuna's location was primarily strategic and political: close enough to open water to receive messengers, retainers and tribute quickly, but far enough inside Lake Malaren to avoid being an open port for just any seafarer.

## Sigtuna versus Birka: Two Towns, Two Functions

Around 960 to 970 AD, Birka rapidly lost importance and was abandoned over the following decades. The exact reasons are not fully settled, but the timing is striking given how closely it overlaps with Sigtuna's founding. Most historians today agree that Sigtuna was deliberately positioned as Birka's successor, though with a different orientation: not as an open emporium for international merchants, but as a control point for access to the Uppland hinterland and as a seat of royal authority.

That difference in function can be traced across several points:

| Feature | Birka | Sigtuna |
|---|---|---|
| Founding period | mid 8th century | 970s/980s AD (dendro-dated 981) |
| Main function | long-distance trading town, eastern hub | royal power center, administrative seat |
| Founder | kings of Svealand (collectively) | King Erik the Victorious (named individually) |
| Location | island of Bjorko, secluded | gateway to the Malaren hinterland, Uppland |
| End / continuity | abandoned c. 960-975 | continuously inhabited to this day |
| Distinctive feature | dirham hoards, trade as far as Baghdad | Sweden's first minted coinage, dense runestone cluster |

> Sigtuna was not founded to carry on Birka's trade. It was founded to demonstrate a different kind of rule: royally administered, Christian-leaning, built to last rather than built around the next trading season.

## Silver Bearing a King's Name: Sweden's First Minted Coinage

Arguably the most consequential step in Sigtuna's early history came around the year 995 AD, when Erik's son and successor **Olof Skotkonung** established a royal mint in the town, the first of its kind anywhere in Sweden. The silver pennies struck there were unmistakably modeled on English coinage, in particular the Anglo-Saxon "Crux" type, pointing to direct contact with English moneyers. Some of the earliest surviving coins carry the inscription "Rex Situen" (King of Sigtuna), before later issues used the broader title "King of the Swedes" (Rex Sveunum).

That small shift in wording is historically telling. It shows how Olof Skotkonung first defined his kingship through his residence town of Sigtuna, before extending his claim to rule over "the Swedes" more broadly in a second step. Contemporary numismatic evidence also records the names of individual moneyers on these coins, including one called Godwine, alongside others such as Leofman and Snelling, names with a distinctly Anglo-Saxon ring to them. That underlines just how closely early Swedish coinage was tied, both technically and personally, to English models. Estimates suggest that over the following four decades or so, until around 1030, several million of these small silver coins were struck in Sigtuna before production eventually slowed.

- **Model:** the Anglo-Saxon "Crux" coin type
- **Period:** beginning around 995 AD, continuing to roughly 1030
- **Commissioned by:** King Olof Skotkonung, son of Erik the Victorious
- **Inscription:** first "Rex Situen" (Sigtuna), later "Rex Sveunum" (Swedes)
- **Significance:** the first independent minted coinage on Swedish soil

For the Swedish crown, this mint was far more than an economic facility. Striking one's own silver coinage bearing one's own royal name was, in tenth-century Europe, an unmistakable signal of sovereignty, comparable to a modern national flag or a written constitution. Olof Skotkonung was deliberately placing himself alongside the Christian kings of England and the continent, whose coin systems he copied both technically and visually, while also distinguishing himself from his father, who had possessed no coinage of his own. That the mint was set up specifically in Sigtuna, rather than in one of the old trading towns, underscores once again how thoroughly Sigtuna was conceived from the start as a seat of royal rather than mercantile power.

## Stone by Stone: Sigtuna's Exceptional Concentration of Runestones

Few places in medieval Sweden are as densely covered in runestones as Sigtuna. According to an overview on runic research presented at the University of York in 2023, the town of Sigtuna holds more runestones than any other place in medieval Sweden, well ahead of Uppsala (then known as Ostra Aros), which comes second with 36 known stones. This concentration is all the more remarkable given that Sigtuna was, in terms of sheer area, a comparatively small town.

Many of these stones were raised by wealthy families and royal officials who lived in or were connected to the rising town. The inscriptions range from classic memorial texts for deceased relatives to references to the craftsmen and patrons who helped build the young settlement. The sheer number and quality of Sigtuna's runestones makes the town one of the most important sites anywhere for the study of the Younger Futhark across the whole Baltic region. Readers interested in the broader technique and meaning of such inscriptions can find a useful point of comparison in our article on the [Jelling Stones in Denmark](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/), which are far fewer in number but far more monumental and royal in character, an instructive contrast to Sigtuna's more civic, town-based runic tradition.

Some of Sigtuna's stones still stand at their original find spots in the middle of the modern old town, while others were reused, in some cases already during the Middle Ages, as building material in church walls, a circumstance that, paradoxically, helped preserve them for later study.

## From Pagan Power Center to Christian Bishopric

Sigtuna also played a pivotal role in Sweden's religious transformation. By the mid 11th century at the latest, under King Stenkil, the town was given its own bishopric, with Adalvard the Younger recorded as its first bishop. Toward the end of the 11th century, Sigtuna also saw the construction of one of the region's earliest stone churches, a visible sign that the young town was increasingly positioning itself as a Christian center, even though burials with pagan grave goods are still attested from the same period.

This overlap of old belief and new church was not a contradiction but typical of a transitional era in which Christian and pre-Christian practices coexisted for decades. In the longer run, however, Sigtuna could not hold on to its status as a bishop's seat. Nearby Gamla Uppsala, once the most important pagan cult center in Sweden, gained the upper hand in church politics and was even elevated to an archbishopric in 1164, while Sigtuna's episcopal residence increasingly fell into disuse by comparison. Readers curious about the pagan counterpart to this development can find it in our article on the [burial mounds and temple of Old Uppsala](/en/blog/gamla-uppsala-burial-mounds/).

## Decline, Continuity and a Living Present

In the late medieval period, Sigtuna lost considerable political and economic weight to rising towns such as Stockholm. Unlike Birka, however, the town was never fully abandoned. It shrank, and it lost its rank as a leading center, but it remained continuously inhabited, a crucial difference from nearly every other major site of the Swedish Viking Age.

The timeline below summarizes the key stages:

1. **c. 970-980 AD:** founding by Erik the Victorious, construction dendro-dated to 981
2. **c. 960-975 AD:** in parallel, the decline and abandonment of Birka
3. **c. 995 AD:** establishment of Sweden's first royal mint under Olof Skotkonung
4. **mid 11th century:** founding of the Sigtuna bishopric under King Stenkil
5. **late 11th century:** construction of one of the region's first stone churches
6. **1164:** Gamla Uppsala becomes an archbishopric, Sigtuna's ecclesiastical importance fades
7. **to the present day:** continuously inhabited, regarded as Sweden's oldest surviving town

This very continuity makes Sigtuna something of an archaeological stroke of luck. Because the town was never abandoned, many of its oldest layers lie directly beneath the cellars and gardens of the present old town, regularly uncovered during construction work and systematically documented for decades by the Sigtuna Museum. Walking today along Stora Gatan, one of the oldest street lines still in use anywhere in Scandinavia, means moving along a route that has changed remarkably little in roughly a thousand years. No other place in Sweden connects the founding era of the Viking Age so directly to a living present as Sigtuna does.
