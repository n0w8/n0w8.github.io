Picture a low ridge overlooking the Limfjord, across the water from Aalborg, that spent close to a thousand years quietly buried under drifting sand. **Lindholm Hoje** is one of Scandinavia's largest and best preserved burial grounds from the Germanic Iron Age and the Viking Age: roughly 700 graves have been archaeologically recorded here, and about 150 of them still survive as visible stone ship settings. Nowhere else in Scandinavia has such a dense concentration of this particular grave form come down to us so intact.

Walk the site today and the first impression is simply scale: field after field of grey stones arranged in deliberate patterns, spread across the gently sloping ground. Only on closer inspection does it become clear how many distinct grave shapes sit side by side here, silent evidence of a community that kept burying its dead on the same hillside for roughly five centuries, right up until the sand arrived and swallowed the whole site.

## Lindholm Hoje: a burial ground preserved by the thing that buried it

What sets Lindholm Hoje apart from comparable sites is not only its size but the unusual way it survived. The hill itself is a natural glacial ridge that served as a burial place for generations. Toward the end of the Viking Age, around AD 1000, windblown sand began drifting across the ridge in large quantities. Within what was, in archaeological terms, a relatively short span of time, the entire burial ground disappeared under a layer of sand that in places reached a depth of up to four metres.

That sand acted like a natural time capsule. While countless comparable burial grounds across Denmark were gradually destroyed or disturbed over the centuries by ploughing, erosion, or later building work, Lindholm Hoje stayed essentially untouched beneath its sand cover. The stone settings, the grave shapes, even the precise position of individual stones relative to one another, survived exactly as the people of the Iron Age and Viking Age had laid them out. Only as the sand was later reworked and partly cleared did sections of the site resurface.

> Without the drifting sand that covered this burial ground for centuries, Lindholm Hoje would most likely look today the way most comparable sites do: scattered, heavily disturbed stones with little of their original arrangement left to read.

## Thorkild Ramskou and six years in the field

Stone settings at the site were first noted as far back as 1889 and again in 1896, but a systematic scientific excavation had to wait decades longer. The decisive campaign ran from **1952 to 1958**, directed by the archaeologist **Thorkild Ramskou**, who oversaw the entire six-year dig in his role as the National Museum's responsible superintendent. The work was carried out jointly with the Aalborg Historical Museum, the forerunner of today's Nordjyllands Historiske Museum.

Over those six years, Ramskou's team cleared and documented roughly 589 of the site's approximately 700 known graves in detail. He eventually published the results in the comprehensive monograph *Lindholm Høje: Gravpladsen*, issued in 1976 in the Nordiske Fortidsminder series, which still serves as the standard scholarly reference on the site and includes an English summary for readers outside Denmark.

What stands out about the excavation is not just its scope but the care taken in recording every stone setting, every grave shape, and every find in context. That methodical thoroughness turned Lindholm Hoje into one of the most thoroughly documented burial grounds of its kind anywhere in Scandinavia, and it still underpins comparative research into Viking burial custom today, including studies of the [Viking ship burial](/en/blog/viking-burial-ship/) tradition more broadly. For a field team working in the 1950s, decades before any kind of digital record-keeping, consistently logging tens of thousands of individual stones, soil marks, and artefacts across six full seasons was itself a considerable achievement, and it is exactly that consistency that still makes the Lindholm Hoje dataset useful for international comparison today.

## From triangles to stone ships: five centuries of changing grave shapes

Lindholm Hoje was not first used as a burial ground in the Viking Age. Burials here go back considerably further, into the Germanic Iron Age from roughly the 5th century AD onward. Over those centuries, the preferred shape of grave markers shifted noticeably, which lets archaeologists place individual graves within a rough chronological sequence even where no other dating evidence survives.

In the earliest phases of the cemetery, simple triangular, rectangular, and round stone settings dominated. From around the 8th century, the oval shape became the most common form, before the Viking Age proper brought the **stone ship setting** to the fore as the dominant grave type: an arrangement of upright stones tracing the outline of a ship's hull, with larger stones marking bow and stern. The broad sequence looks roughly like this:

| Period | Dominant grave shape | Burial practice |
|---|---|---|
| c. 5th-7th century | Triangle, rectangle, circle | mostly cremation burials |
| c. 8th century | Oval | cremation burials with stone setting |
| Viking Age (c. 9th-10th/11th century) | Stone ship setting | cremation burials, often with grave goods |
| around AD 1000 | Sand covers the whole site | active use ends, preservation begins |

With roughly 150 stone ship settings still identifiable today, Lindholm Hoje holds the largest connected assemblage of this grave form anywhere in Scandinavia. The overwhelming majority of graves here, regardless of shape, were cremation burials: the dead were cremated and their remains interred within the stone setting itself, frequently together with grave goods.

## What the dead were sent off with

A good many of the excavated ship settings contained grave goods that offer a direct window into the everyday lives of the people buried here. Finds include **pottery vessels**, iron tools, items of jewellery, combs, and brooches made of both stone and metal. Weapons do appear, though less often than one might expect, which some researchers take as a sign that Lindholm Hoje was less a warrior cemetery in any narrow sense than an ordinary burial ground for a farming and craft-working community.

The sheer volume and variety of these grave goods, ranging from plain everyday pottery to more finely worked jewellery, points to a community with a visible range of social standing that kept burying its dead at the same spot across many generations. For a sense of how differently a grave could be furnished when the person buried held real political power, our article on the [Jelling Stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/) offers a striking contrast: royal authority expressed through monument and inscription rather than through a buried ship of stone.

It is also worth noting that weapon burials are comparatively rare across the whole cemetery, which lines up with what the settlement evidence nearby seems to suggest as well: a community defined mainly by farming and craft rather than by a dedicated warrior elite. That ordinariness is, in a sense, exactly what makes the site so valuable archaeologically. It does not preserve one spectacular exception; it preserves the everyday life of an entire community across many generations.

## The settlement that grew up beside the graves

One of the most informative discoveries of the 1950s excavation was not within the cemetery itself but immediately north of it. There, excavators uncovered traces of a Viking Age settlement dating to the 10th and 11th centuries, a period that overlaps with the cemetery's later phases of use. Postholes revealed sizeable buildings, some over 17 metres long, including an oval house of the so-called Trelleborg type, a form also known from other major Viking Age sites of the period.

Alongside these larger structures, excavators recorded roughly twenty smaller pit-houses sunk into the ground, each measuring about 3 to 5 metres in length and 2 to 3 metres in width, with rounded corners and their long axis consistently running east to west. Hearths, cooking pits, wells, fences, and even the remains of a road that had apparently been reinforced with timber at one point were also found. Together, these settlement remains show that Lindholm Hoje was never only a place for the dead. For centuries it was also a living space for a community that drew its livelihood from fishing, farming, and trade along the Limfjord.

The overlap in time between settlement and cemetery is particularly telling. While people were living in houses and pit-dwellings right next to the burial ground during the 10th and 11th centuries, burials were still apparently taking place in the cemetery's older sections, some of which had already been in use for generations by then. The line between the world of the living and the world of the dead was, at Lindholm Hoje, evidently far less sharply drawn than a modern visitor might assume. House and grave stood within easy sight of one another.

## From excavation site to museum

The scientific importance of Lindholm Hoje eventually led to the construction of a dedicated museum right beside the burial ground. It opened in 1992, funded by a donation from the cement manufacturer Aalborg Portland A/S to mark its own centenary, and was expanded in 2008 with a new exhibition covering the wider prehistory of the Limfjord region. Today it operates as part of the Nordjyllands Historiske Museum and displays much of the pottery, tools, and jewellery recovered during the 1950s excavation alongside reconstructions of everyday settlement life.

For visitors, this combination is unusual: an open, walkable burial ground with its stone ship settings still visible on the ground, paired with a museum that lays out the excavation history and the finds in context right next door. Few places in Denmark offer such a direct sense of how closely burial practice and everyday settlement life were actually intertwined during the Viking Age.

## A burial ground that tells its own story

What ultimately makes Lindholm Hoje so valuable is this rare combination of scale, preservation, and documentation. Few other burial grounds let researchers trace the evolution of grave forms across several centuries at a single site with this degree of continuity, from the simple triangles of the early Iron Age through to the elaborate stone ship settings of the Viking Age. That it took a natural disaster, in the form of sand drift around AD 1000, to preserve that long arc of development right down to the present day counts among the sharper irony of Nordic archaeology.

Our take: Lindholm Hoje deserves more attention outside Denmark than it generally gets. Where sites like Jelling or Norway's great ship burials draw attention through single spectacular finds, the strength of Lindholm Hoje lies precisely in its sheer volume and completeness, an entire centuries-old community preserved, not just a single grave, under the very sand that once buried it.
