Hedeby on the Schlei and Birka on Lake Malaren usually get top billing as the great trading hubs of the Viking Age, but Norway had its own entry in that story: **Kaupang**. On a quiet stretch of coastline in what is now Vestfold, around the year 800 AD, the first real town of the Norwegian kingdom took shape, a seasonal trading site where merchants from half of Northern Europe crossed paths. Kaupang was smaller than its Swedish and Danish counterparts, but no less significant for Norwegian history, and its very name spells out exactly what the place was for: **Kaupang** is simply the Old Norse word for "marketplace" or "trading site".

## The Location: Kaupang on the Viksfjord near Larvik

Kaupang sits on the **Viksfjord**, a sheltered arm of the Oslofjord in southeastern Norway, near present-day Tjolling and only about five kilometers east of the town of Larvik. The bay offered ships a calm, easily defensible anchorage with direct access to the open Skagerrak, the strait separating Norway from Denmark. That put Kaupang on one of the most important sea routes of the entire Viking Age, roughly halfway between the Danish trading sites to the south and the fur- and resource-rich regions of northern Norway.

The town's name points directly back to its function: **kaupang** means "market" or "trading place" in Old Norse, related to the modern Norwegian verb "kjope" (to buy). The modern place name is therefore not some romantic later label, but very likely simply what residents and visitors actually called the site while it was active: the place where you buy and sell.

Archaeological evidence dates the earliest permanent buildings at the site to around 800 to 810 AD, and a jetty has been dated to roughly 803. The settlement likely reached its peak around 875 AD, when, according to cautious estimates, somewhere between 400 and 1,000 people lived and worked there, at least seasonally, far fewer than at Hedeby or Birka, but remarkably dense by the standards of Norway at the time.

## Skiringssal and Ohthere: What a Norwegian Seafarer Told King Alfred

What sets Kaupang apart from other early medieval trading sites is a written source that originated far outside Scandinavia. Around 890 AD, the Norwegian seafarer **Ohthere** of Halogaland gave an account of his travels along the Norwegian coast at the court of **Alfred the Great**, King of Wessex. His account was inserted into the Old English translation of the late antique world history by Orosius and survives to this day.

In it, Ohthere describes a weeks-long sea voyage from his home in the north to a trading site called **Sciringes healh**, Skiringssalr in Old Norse, roughly "Skiringssal" in English. He reports that the voyage alone, in fair weather, took about a month, anchoring every evening, a detail that hints at just how demanding and carefully planned such journeys along the jagged Norwegian coastline really were.

The large majority of scholars identify this place with Kaupang, or more precisely with a neighboring hall complex at Huseby whose name still echoes that tradition today. A few researchers have proposed other sites along Norway's southwestern coast, in Rogaland or Vest-Agder, as alternative candidates, but most, including the archaeologist Dagfinn Skre, continue to regard Kaupang as the most likely location of Skiringssal. The account doesn't stand alone, either: the same Old English Orosius manuscript also contains the travel report of a certain Wulfstan, who sailed from Hedeby to the Baltic trading site of Truso, giving historians a rare, near-contemporary point of comparison between two very different Viking Age trade routes.

> That an English king, on the far side of the North Sea, even knew where in Norway one could go to trade shows just how tightly the web of seafarers, merchants, and information already stretched across the North in the 9th century.

## Excavations: From Nicolaysen to Blindheim to the Kaupang Excavation Project

The scholarly exploration of Kaupang goes back further than one might expect. As early as 1867, the Norwegian antiquarian **Nicolay Nicolaysen** mapped one of the burial fields around the former trading site and excavated 79 burial mounds there, a remarkably systematic achievement for its time.

The real modern excavation of Kaupang, though, is inseparable from the name **Charlotte Blindheim** (1917-2005). The Norwegian archaeologist began systematic digs at the settlement and its surrounding cemeteries, including the sites of Bikjholbergene and Lamoya, in 1947, and published her final findings as late as 1999. For decades, Blindheim's work formed the essential foundation of everything known about Kaupang.

A second major push came between 1998 and 2003 with the so-called **Kaupang Excavation Project**, led by archaeologist **Dagfinn Skre** of the University of Oslo. The project carried out large-scale, modern, carefully documented excavations and brought the results, along with a reassessment of Blindheim's earlier finds, together in a multi-volume scholarly series, including the volumes "Kaupang in Skiringssal" and "Things from the Town". These publications remain standard references in Norwegian Viking archaeology to this day.

## Crafts, Trade, and Imported Goods

The excavations carried out by both Blindheim and the Kaupang Excavation Project paint a picture of a place where people actually worked, not just traded. Documented activities at the site include:

- **Metalworking**: bronze casting along with the production of jewelry and fittings, some modeled on continental and British examples.
- **Glass bead production**, along with the reworking of imported glass into new vessels and ornaments.
- **Textile production**: numerous spindle whorls, loom weights, and other equipment point to active weaving and cloth-making on site.
- **Soapstone working**: vessels and everyday objects carved from the soft stone found in larger quantities across Norway.

At the same time, the excavations turned up a dense network of imported goods that confirm Kaupang's international reach: Arabic silver coins (dirhams) that traveled to Scandinavia along far-reaching trade routes, a gold coin from the Frankish trading town of **Dorestad** in what is now the Rhine delta, pottery, jewelry, and metalwork showing continental and British influence. Many of these finds are now held and displayed at the **Museum of Cultural History at the University of Oslo**, Norway's main repository for Viking Age artifacts.

That combination of local production and far-flung imports shows that Kaupang, despite its relatively modest size, was firmly woven into a trade network linking Scandinavia with the Frankish Empire, the British Isles, and, via intermediary routes, even the Islamic world. The scholarly analysis of these finds, especially the jewelry and metalwork modeled on continental and British originals, formed a dedicated focus of the Kaupang publication series, and shows how precisely individual object groups can be traced to particular regions of origin, even when the objects themselves carry no written record of where they came from.

## The Dead of Kaupang: Cemeteries and Boat Burials

Several burial fields surround the former trading site, together offering one of the richest pictures available of life and death in an early Norwegian trading settlement. Most of the burials date to roughly 840 to 940 AD and range from simple earthen graves to elaborate mounds with rich grave goods.

Particularly striking are several **boat burials**, in which the deceased were interred together with a boat, a practice researchers often associate with wealthy long-distance traders, possibly even Danish merchants who lived in Kaupang permanently or seasonally. The site also had its own cremation cemetery, where the dead were burned according to pagan rite.

Many of the grave goods did not originate in Norway at all, but in Western Europe and the British Isles, from jewelry to everyday objects. These imported grave goods underline, once again, just how deeply the people of Kaupang were tied into long-distance trade networks, even in death.

## Kaupang, Birka, and Hedeby: Norway's Own Trading Town, Compared

Kaupang was considerably smaller than the other two great Viking Age trading hubs, but no less important in its own right for the region it served. A brief comparison shows where the differences lie:

| Trading Town | Location | Active Period | Distinguishing Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaupang | Viksfjord, Vestfold, Norway | c. 800-950 AD | Norway's oldest known trading site, likely Skiringssal |
| Birka | Bjorko, Lake Malaren, Sweden | 8th-10th c. | Central Sweden's leading trading center, links as far as Baghdad |
| Hedeby | Schlei inlet, modern Schleswig-Holstein | 8th-11th c. | Baltic-North Sea land bridge, own coinage |

Unlike [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/) or [Birka](/en/blog/birka-swedens-first-town/), Kaupang was probably never a permanently inhabited town with several hundred year-round residents, but rather a **seasonal marketplace**, busiest during the warmer months when the sea routes were safest to travel. Some researchers have also suggested that Danish kings such as Sigfred or Godfred encouraged Kaupang's growth as a way of extending their influence over Norwegian trade, a theory that is debated among scholars but not conclusively proven.

## Decline and Legacy

Like many early trading sites, Kaupang didn't vanish in a single, clearly datable catastrophe, but faded gradually over the course of the 10th century. The exact reasons aren't fully settled. Likely factors discussed by researchers include shifting trade routes, political changes in the control of Norwegian coastal trade, and the slow silting-up of the bay, which made access to deep water increasingly difficult for smaller settlements.

What remained was a site remarkably well preserved underground, one that only became visible again through the work of Nicolaysen, Blindheim, and finally Skre's Kaupang Excavation Project. Today, Kaupang stands as the clearest evidence that Norway, not just Denmark and Sweden, already had its own internationally connected trading site at the dawn of the Viking Age, even if it was smaller and likely more seasonal than its more famous neighbors to the south and east.

For visitors today, the former settlement area is mostly a quiet landscape of meadows, low coastline, and the characteristic burial mounds still visible across the terrain in several places. Information panels on site and the finds held at the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo together give a sense of just how busy this unassuming stretch of coast must have been more than a thousand years ago.

## Our Take

Kaupang lives a little in the shadow of Hedeby and Birka, and that's a shame. Its connection to Ohthere's account to King Alfred makes it a rare case where archaeological evidence and a near-contemporary written source actually reinforce each other, rather than just loosely touching. Anyone who wants to understand how differently the great Viking Age trading sites actually operated, from Hedeby's land bridge to Birka's eastern connections to Kaupang's seasonal character on the Norwegian coast, can hardly skip this comparatively small but historically well documented place.
