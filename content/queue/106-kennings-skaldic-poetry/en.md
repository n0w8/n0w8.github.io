Around 1220, Snorri Sturluson wrote an entire handbook just to explain why a poet may call gold "the hair of Sif" without anyone in the hall looking puzzled. **Kennings** are the engine of skaldic poetry: phrases that do not name a thing but point to it through an image drawn from myth or everyday life. Once you know how they work, Viking verse stops looking like word salad and starts looking like a riddle game with firm rules.

This article shows how a kenning is built, which examples Snorri and the other sources really preserve, where scholars remain unsure, and why the skalds bothered at all.

## What is a kenning?

The word comes from the Old Norse verb *kenna*, "to know, to designate". You name something **by** something else: the sword is not called a sword but described as a thing tied to battle. At its core a kenning has two parts. The **base word** says what kind of thing is meant, and the **determinant** narrows it down. "Hair of Sif" means hair belonging to one specific goddess. Since every listener knew that Sif's hair was forged from gold, the solution was obvious.

In the **Skáldskaparmál**, the second part of his Prose Edda, Snorri distinguishes the simple kenning from the extended one. A two-part kenning is *tvíkennt*, "doubly designated". When the determinant is itself replaced by another kenning, the chain grows, and "fire of the sea" for gold can become a three-part construction. Poets played with how far they could lead an audience before the riddle became unsolvable.

Kennings must be told apart from **heiti**, plain poetic synonyms without an image. A heiti for the sea is a rare word, not a periphrasis. Snorri collects both because a skald needed both: the heiti fills the line, the kenning gives it colour.

> The kenning is not decoration on top of the poetry. It is the tool. - editorial assessment

## Five examples that are actually attested

Popular lists online deserve suspicion: many "Viking kennings" are modern inventions that merely sound medieval. The following five belong to the secure core found in Snorri and the Eddic poems.

| Meaning | Kenning (literal) | Background |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | "Sif's hair" | Loki cut off the goddess Sif's hair, and dwarfs forged a replacement from gold |
| Gold | "otter's ransom" | The gods filled and covered an otter skin with gold to settle a blood debt |
| Gold | "Fáfnir's lair" | The dragon Fáfnir lay on the hoard until Sigurd killed him |
| Poetry | "Odin's mead" | The mead of poetry that Odin took from the giants |
| Sky | "Ymir's skull" | The gods shaped the world from the body of the primeval giant |

Notice that every kenning **brings a story with it**. A listener who heard "otter's ransom" thought automatically of Hreidmar, Andvari and the cursed ring. The kenning summoned not just an image but a whole myth, a slice of shared knowledge that needed no explanation. That is why kennings are so hard for outsiders: they presuppose a stock of myths that was perfectly ordinary in a culture without books.

More on the figure who brought the poets' mead into the world can be found in the portrait [Odin: Allfather and Seeker of Wisdom](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/).

## How the skalds used kennings

Skaldic poetry is not what most people picture when they think of a Viking song. It was composed in praise of chieftains and kings, follows strict syllable counts, alliteration and internal rhyme, and is so dense that single half-stanzas were embedded in prose narratives as evidence. The kenning is the means of filling the line while raising the image.

Here is the principle, without inventing a specific verse: if a skald wanted to praise a warrior, he could not simply say "warrior". He spoke of a "tree of battle" or an "increaser of gold", and the audience at once saw a man who fights and gives generously. Those are the two classic virtues of a good retainer. The kenning is thus also a **value catalogue in miniature**.

That we can read these stanzas at all is thanks to painstaking editorial work. The project Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages (skaldic.org) publishes texts and translations online, and Sveinbjörn Egilsson's older lexicon already gathered numerous attestations in the nineteenth century. Anyone trying to understand a stanza usually puts the two side by side.

For the cultural history of the poets themselves, see [The Skalds: Poets of the Vikings](/en/blog/skalds-viking-poets/).

## Myth vs. fact: five common misconceptions

Skaldic imagery attracts half-knowledge. Five classics, briefly sorted:

1. **"Kennings are Viking songs."** No. They come from praise poems and were spoken or recited, not sung. Whether melodies existed, we do not know.
2. **"Anything that sounds like a metaphor is a kenning."** No. A kenning follows the base-plus-determinant rule. A free image such as "the sky weeps" is not one.
3. **"Every example online is attested."** Sadly not. Popular lists contain inventions no skald ever spoke. Reliable sources are Snorri, the Edda and the stanzas in the kings' sagas.
4. **"Kennings were a purely Norwegian invention."** The earliest well-preserved skalds are Norwegians and Icelanders, but the principle is older. Old English poetry also knows periphrases such as the "whale-road" for the sea (*hronrad*) in Beowulf.
5. **"You can read them like a dictionary."** No. Meaning depends on context, and some kennings are still not securely interpreted.

That last point deserves honesty: for some stanzas, specialists still argue about the reading of a single word, and translations can differ markedly.

## Typical subject areas

Kennings cluster in a few fields because those are the themes of the praise poem: battle, gold, ships, the sea, poetry and the ruler himself. A few attested examples from these fields:

- **Sea:** Snorri gives "Ymir's blood", because the flood at creation came from the blood of the slain giant.
- **Gallows:** "Odin's horse", because according to the Hávamál Odin hanged himself on the tree, and because the name Yggdrasil is read as "horse of the terrible one" (Ygg is a name of Odin).
- **Poetry:** besides "Odin's mead" also "Kvasir's blood", both from the story of the mead of poetry.
- **Ship:** "sea-horse" and similar horse images, a sign of how familiar horses were as a means of travel.
- **Gold:** apart from the three examples above, many more periphrases pointing to dragon lairs and treasure tales.

Once you notice how often Odin appears in these examples, you see why the skalds regarded the Allfather as the god of poetry. The tale behind it is told in the article on [Mimir's Well and Odin's Wisdom](/en/blog/mimirs-well-odin-wisdom/).

## Why so complicated? An editorial view

It is fair to ask why poets deliberately set their audiences puzzles. Three explanations are plausible, and probably all are partly true.

**First, the craft.** Skaldic stanzas demand exact syllable counts and rhymes. The kenning supplies synonyms of any length: a word like "gold" can be padded to two or three syllables at will. The form forces the richness.

**Second, prestige.** Whoever understood a stanza belonged to the elite. A ruler who had such poems recited showed his education, and the poet proved his mastery. Poetry was political capital: a successful praise poem spread the ruler's reputation, a mocking verse could damage it.

**Third, memory.** In an oral culture, fixed form helps preserve verses. A stanza that cannot shift in rhyme and count survives centuries. That we can read verses composed in the tenth century and written down only in the thirteenth supports the principle.

My own assessment: anyone who dismisses kennings as mere flourish overlooks their function. They are **compressed social history**, a kind of shorthand in which myth, craft and praise of rulers are laced together. That they read like riddles today is not the poets' fault but ours: we lack the stock of myths every listener then carried in their head.

## Building your own kennings

The nicest way in remains doing it yourself. The rule is simple: take a base word naming the category and a determinant pointing to a myth or a feature. Then check whether a listener could guess the solution.

Easy practice questions: how would you describe a raven without naming it? What is a "field of gulls"? (The sea.) What might "storm of spears" mean? (Battle.) Such coinages follow the principle even though they do not come from the sources. A modern attempt helps understanding but must not be booked as a historically attested kenning.

## Conclusion

Kennings are not a game but the operating system of skaldic poetry: built in two parts, saturated with myth and designed for insiders. The securely attested examples such as "Sif's hair", "otter's ransom" or "Odin's mead" show how much history a single image can hold. Anyone who wants to take Viking poetry seriously should start with Snorri, keep the skaldic database beside them, and above all stay careful: not every pretty image circulating online was ever spoken by a Viking.
