**Sigtuna**, on Lake Mälaren just under 40 kilometres northwest of Stockholm, was laid out as a planned royal town around 980 AD, and it was here, from about 995, that King Olof Skötkonung struck Sweden's very first coins. The town is still lived in today and markets itself as "Sveriges första stad", Sweden's first town. That claim comes with an asterisk, because Birka, some 30 kilometres away on the same waters, is older. But Birka was abandoned around 975, while Sigtuna has kept its main street in the same place for more than a thousand years.

You notice it the moment you walk down Stora gatan. Between colourful wooden houses, the street runs almost exactly where the first settlers pegged it out, back then right on the shoreline. Beneath it lies "the black earth", occupation layers up to three metres deep in places, a gift to archaeologists.

## When Was Sigtuna Founded, and by Whom?

Excavations date the earliest plots to around 980. The founder is traditionally named as **Erik the Victorious** (Erik Segersäll), who ruled from roughly 970 until his death in 995 at the royal estate of Gamla Uppsala. That attribution is not watertight: the written sources are thin, and some accounts credit his son Olof Skötkonung instead. Our money is on Erik, simply because the town seems to have been standing by the time Olof took the throne.

What nobody disputes is that Sigtuna did not slowly grow out of a village. The large excavations of 1988 to 2006 reveal a town built to a plan. Along a straight main street on the old shoreline, long narrow plots about eight metres wide were marked out, each with its short side to the street, and the lanes down to the water began life as wooden jetties. A royal estate sat in the middle of town, on the very plot now occupied by the **Sigtuna Museum**.

The site was no accident. Mälaren was still an inlet of the Baltic, with water levels several metres higher than now, and Sigtuna sat on the waterways into fertile Uppland, the heartland of the Svea kings.

## Sigtuna vs Birka: What's the Difference?

Birka, on the island of Björkö, had been a trading place since around the middle of the 8th century, a hub for Arab silver, furs and slaves, until it was abandoned around 975 for reasons still debated. We tell that story in our article on [Birka, Sweden's first town](/en/blog/birka-swedens-first-town/). Sigtuna took over as the leading centre on Mälaren, but it was a different kind of place: from day one, it was the king's project.

| Feature | Birka | Sigtuna |
|---|---|---|
| Origins | around the mid 8th century | around 980 |
| Setting | island of Björkö | mainland, gateway to Uppland |
| Character | trading place | planned town with a royal estate |
| Money | foreign coins, mostly dirhams | its own coinage from about 995 |
| Church | missions from Ansgar onwards | bishop's seat around 1060 |
| Fate | abandoned around 975 | still inhabited |

> Birka was a market the kings made money from. Sigtuna was a royal project the merchants made money from.

## Sweden's First Coins: Olof Skötkonung's Sigtuna Mint

Around 995, **Olof Skötkonung** succeeded his father. He is regarded as the first Swedish king to be baptised and remain Christian until he died, and the first to have coins struck in Sweden. For that he brought in moneyers from England. The reverses of the Sigtuna pennies carry names such as **Godwine**, Leofman, Snelling and Ulfketill. A Godwine also turns up on Norwegian and Danish coins of the same years, so it may have been one man working for three kings.

The models were the silver pennies of the English king Æthelred II, first the Crux type and later the Long Cross type. Early dies call Olof "OLAF REX AN SITUN", king in Sigtuna, and later ones "OLAF REX SVEVORUM", king of the Svear. One spelling of the town, SIDEI, has been read as "Si(gtuna) Dei", God's Sigtuna.

![Silver penny of Olof Skötkonung from Sigtuna with a stylised royal bust in the inner circle, above a millimetre scale](/images/sigtuna-2.jpg)
*A penny of Olof Skötkonung struck in Sigtuna, its royal bust copied from English coins, now in the Royal Coin Cabinet in Stockholm. Photo: Helena Bonnevier (SHM), CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

The historian Jonas Ros reads that change of title as a sign that Olof's power base widened around the year 1000: first lord of Sigtuna and its hinterland, then king of a whole people. After that, though, the coins grew much cruder: the die-cutters apparently could not read, and one die even calls Olof "REX ANCOL", king of the English, copied off an English original without a second thought.

- **Start:** around 995 under Olof Skötkonung
- **Continuation:** under his son Anund Jacob, ending around 1030
- **Output:** estimates run to roughly two million coins
- **Proof on site:** a coin die found in Sigtuna itself

The point was most likely prestige, ruling like a proper Christian European king. It did not end the bullion economy: silver in the North went on being weighed rather than counted for a long time, as our article on [Viking silver hoards](/en/blog/viking-silver-hoards/) explains. Sweden would not strike coins again until the 12th century.

## Who Lived in Viking Age Sigtuna?

For the years around 1000 to 1050, researchers estimate somewhere between about 750 and a little over 1,000 inhabitants, based on burials and the number of plots. The more interesting question is where they came from. A 2018 study in Current Biology (Krzewińska and colleagues) analysed DNA and strontium isotopes from burials of the 10th to 12th centuries and found the townspeople more genetically diverse than several Iron Age and modern populations of central and northern Europe. According to Stockholm University, around half of those studied had not grown up in the region.

How these people wrote shows up in more than a hundred runic inscriptions on objects and bones, mostly cattle ribs, from the 11th to the 13th century: names, owner's marks, practice alphabets, charms against fever, Christian prayers. Our favourite is a rib from a layer of around 1100 that says, roughly: "The king is most lavish with food. He gave the most. He is gracious." The king was probably Inge the Elder. Back then, generosity was not a personality trait but the foundation of power.

## How Many Runestones Are in Sigtuna?

With just over 30 known **runestones**, Sigtuna is, according to the Sigtuna Museum, the place in Sweden with more runestones than any other. Many survive only as fragments, and most date from the second half of the 11th century.

The custom of raising runic memorial stones began in Denmark in the 960s and spread to Norway and Sweden, its most famous early example being the [Jelling Stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/). In Sigtuna, though, it was not kings putting up stones but most likely well-off farmers who kept a town plot alongside their country estates. Two rune carvers are known by name: **Fot** and **Torbjörn**.

![Runestone U 379 with red-painted runes and a cross, on a lawn in front of the brick wall of St Mary's Church in Sigtuna](/images/sigtuna-3.jpg)
*Runestone U 379 south of St Mary's Church, raised by the "guild brothers of the Frisians". Photo: Arild Vågen, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

The best known is **U 379** by St Mary's Church (Mariakyrkan), 1.84 metres tall. Its inscription translates as: "The guild brothers of the Frisians had this stone raised in memory of Torkel, their guild brother. May God help his spirit. Torbjörn carved." A second stone, U 391 on Prästgatan, also remembers a member of the guild. Whether these were Frisian merchants or Swedes who traded with Frisia is still argued over.

Right on Stora gatan, outside the museum entrance, stands **U 389**, found in the 1850s built into a cellar near the ruined church of St Lars. A signposted trail now links 15 stones, twelve in the town centre and three by the Garnsviken inlet.

## The Bishop's Town: St Per, St Olof and St Lars

Around 1060, Sigtuna became the seat of the first bishop in Svealand. The German missionary bishop **Adalvard the Younger**, sent out from the orbit of the archdiocese of Hamburg-Bremen, worked here with the backing of King Stenkil. Adam of Bremen, writing around 1075, places the pagan temple at Uppsala not far from Sigtuna. In his account Adalvard planned to destroy it, but Stenkil held him back for fear of reprisals against the Christians. For what that sanctuary may have looked like, see our piece on the [Gamla Uppsala burial mounds](/en/blog/gamla-uppsala-burial-mounds/).

Towards the end of the 11th century, the first stone church in the Mälaren region went up on the royal estate as the bishop's church. Its wall remains lie beneath the museum grounds, where a bishop's grave was found in 1993.

![The ruin of St Per's church in Sigtuna, a tall fieldstone central tower with round-arched windows among old broadleaf trees](/images/sigtuna-4.jpg)
*The ruin of St Per, widely thought to have been Sigtuna's cathedral. Photo: Arild Vågen, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

In the early Middle Ages the little town gained six or seven more stone churches. Three still stand as ruins:

- **St Per:** at the western edge of the old town, built in stages during the 12th century and seen by many scholars as the cathedral before the bishopric moved to Uppsala.
- **St Olof:** dedicated to the Norwegian saint Olaf and fairly well preserved. Excavations in 2005 found the foundations of an even older stone church beneath it.
- **St Lars:** only the collapsed tower is left.

Sigtuna did not stay a bishop's town for long. The diocese of Uppsala was created in the 1130s, and in 1164 Uppsala, meaning today's Gamla Uppsala, became an archbishopric. The town kept some religious weight: in 1237 the Dominicans founded a friary here, and its brick church, **St Mary's**, is still the parish church.

## Why Did Sigtuna Decline?

The decline is often pinned on a raid in 1187, when "pagans from the east", perhaps Curonians, Estonians or Karelians, burned the town. Excavations have not confirmed large-scale destruction, though, and a new friary fifty years later hardly fits a town wiped out overnight.

Geology and competition mattered more. Land uplift turned Mälaren into a lake towards the end of the 13th century, so cargo had to be transferred where Stockholm now stands, and new towns such as Stockholm and Uppsala drew trade and power away. After the Reformation and three town fires between 1648 and 1666, just 108 people lived in Sigtuna by 1700, and fewer than 600 around 1900, which made it the smallest town in Sweden. In 2020, with Arlanda Airport ten kilometres away, it had 9,689 inhabitants.

## Visiting Sigtuna Today: What Is Left to See

Start on **Stora gatan**, regarded as the oldest street in Sweden still following its original course. The Sigtuna Museum, on the plot of the old royal estate, displays runestones and inscribed bones from the excavations, and the runestone trail starts outside its entrance, with a sign at each stone explaining the inscription. The three church ruins and St Mary's are an easy walk away. Our tip: once you know that jetties ran out into the water here a thousand years ago, the little lanes down to the shore look very different.

## FAQ

### Is Sigtuna the oldest town in Sweden?

Sigtuna is regarded as the oldest town in Sweden that still exists, founded around 980. Birka is older but was abandoned around 975 and is now an archaeological site.

### Who founded Sigtuna?

Tradition credits King Erik the Victorious (Erik Segersäll), who ruled from about 970 to 995. The sources are thin, and some writers name his son Olof Skötkonung instead. What is certain is that the town was laid out to a plan around a royal estate.

### When were Sweden's first coins minted in Sigtuna?

From about 995, Olof Skötkonung had silver pennies struck in Sigtuna on the English model, with English moneyers such as Godwine. His son Anund Jacob carried on, and minting ended around 1030. Estimates put the total at roughly two million coins.

### How many runestones are there in Sigtuna?

The Sigtuna Museum counts just over 30 known runestones in the town, more than anywhere else in Sweden. On top of that come more than a hundred runic inscriptions on bones and objects.

### What is the difference between Birka and Sigtuna?

Birka was an island trading place from about the mid 8th century until around 975. Sigtuna was founded soon afterwards on the mainland as a planned royal town, with a royal estate, its own coinage and later a bishop.

## More Than a Pretty Old Town

Sigtuna is usually sold as a day trip of wooden houses and cafés. But under the cafés lies the moment when the Viking North tried to turn itself into a European-style kingdom: planned plots, English moneyers, a German bishop, newcomers from far away. The project did not collapse in one blow; it faded over centuries, worn down by rising land and new rivals. Sigtuna was never big, but it was early, and you can still walk that early chapter down a single street.
