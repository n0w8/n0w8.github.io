# Brunanburh 937: The Battle That Shaped England

In 937, King Athelstan defeated a coalition of the Norse king of Dublin, the king of the Scots and the king of Strathclyde at **Brunanburh**, yet nobody can say for certain where the battle was fought. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle gives it a victory poem of its own, Irish annals speak of countless dead, and medieval English writers simply called it "the great battle". Even so, Brunanburh sits far behind Hastings in popular memory. This article sorts out what is secure, what is speculation, and why 937 may matter more for the making of England than 1066.

## The Background: A Kingdom That Did Not Yet Exist

When Athelstan became king of Wessex and Mercia in 924 or 925, "England" was still an idea rather than a state. His grandfather Alfred had stopped the Viking armies, and his father Edward and aunt Æthelflæd had reconquered the southern Danelaw. But the north, around the Viking kingdom of York, remained under Scandinavian rule. In 927 Athelstan took York and then received the submission of several northern rulers. Coins and charters soon called him "king of all Britain" (*rex totius Britanniae*), a claim that reached well beyond the English heartland.

That success created enemies. Part of the Scandinavian aristocracy in northern England and Ireland saw its independence under threat. There was also the matter of the northern neighbours: Constantine II of Alba, king of the Scots, had been forced to acknowledge Athelstan for a time. In 934 Athelstan marched deep into Scotland with an army and a fleet. That pressure produced the coalition that struck three years later.

## Who Fought Whom?

The sources allow a fairly clear line-up of the main players:

| Side | Leader | Background |
|---|---|---|
| Wessex and Mercia | Athelstan, with his young half-brother Edmund | King since c. 924/925, conqueror of York in 927 |
| Dublin Norse | Olaf Guthfrithson | King of Dublin, from the Uí Ímair dynasty, claimant to York |
| Alba (Scotland) | Constantine II | King since c. 900, humbled by Athelstan in 934 |
| Strathclyde | Owain | Ruler of the British kingdom in the north-west |

Scandinavians, Scots and Britons marching together against an English king is the real heart of the story. This was not a Viking invasion in the classic sense, like the one the [Great Heathen Army](/en/blog/great-heathen-army/) led a century earlier. It was a political alliance against one king's supremacy, and the Dublin Vikings were only one partner among several.

## What the Sources Say

Brunanburh is unusually well attested, but every source has its slant.

- **The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle** (entries for 937 in several manuscripts) contains an Old English poem celebrating the victory. It names five kings and seven earls among the enemy dead, a son of Constantine killed, and Olaf fleeing across "deep water" to Dublin. As a victory song it is partisan and full of heroic formulas.
- **The Annals of Ulster** record a "great, lamentable and terrible battle" between Saxons and Norsemen in which countless Norsemen fell, and note that Olaf escaped with a few men.
- **Egil's Saga**, an Icelandic saga of the thirteenth century, tells of Egill Skallagrímsson and his brother Þórólfr, who fought as mercenaries for Athelstan. Þórólfr died there. The saga names the place Vínheiðr near Vínuskógar. It must be read with caution, having been written down roughly three centuries after the events.
- **Later English chroniclers** such as John of Worcester in the twelfth century embroidered the story, for example with 615 ships said to have sailed into the Humber under Olaf. Such figures deserve scepticism.

The poem ends by saying that never before, since Angles and Saxons came from the east across the sea, had there been greater slaughter on this island. It is rhetoric, but it shows how contemporaries rated the day.

## The Course of the Battle: What We Can Reconstruct

There is no detailed battle report. From the poem we can infer that the fight lasted all day, from the hour when the sun rose in the morning until the noble candle of God went to rest. Athelstan and Edmund led an army of West Saxons and Mercians, and the poem singles out the Mercians for praise. Their opponents fought with round shields and spears in the **shield wall**, the formation explained in our article on [the shield wall and Viking battle tactics](/en/blog/viking-shield-wall-battle-tactic/). In the end the coalition broke: Olaf escaped by sea, Constantine went north, and his son lay dead on the field.

Beyond that, little is certain. How many men stood on each side is unknown. The figures often quoted are later estimates without a source behind them.

## Where Was Brunanburh? An Unsolved Puzzle

Perhaps the most astonishing fact is that the site is unknown. The name appears as Brunanburh in the Chronicle, and other sources give Brunnanburh, Weondune or Brune. Several candidates are debated:

| Candidate | Region | Argument |
|---|---|---|
| Bromborough | Wirral, north-west England | Name resemblance, location by the Irish Sea; favoured by many scholars |
| Burnswark | Dumfriesshire, Scotland | Name echo, but far to the north |
| Brinsworth | South Yorkshire | Name resemblance, location in the contested York region |
| Other sites | Lincolnshire, Northumbria | Mostly based on single name forms |

The Wirral theory gained weight because Dublin Norse settled there and the route across the Irish Sea suited Olaf. Archaeologically, however, nothing is confirmed: no mass grave and no weapon deposit can be tied clearly to a battle of 937. Anyone who claims to know the site claims more than the finds allow.

## Timeline

- **924/925:** Athelstan becomes king.
- **927:** Conquest of York and submission of northern rulers.
- **934:** Campaign against Constantine II of Alba.
- **937:** Battle of Brunanburh.
- **939:** Athelstan dies. Olaf Guthfrithson seizes York and much of the Midlands.
- **941:** Olaf dies. England is regained under Edmund.

The timeline shows that the victory of 937 did not settle everything at once. Hardly was Athelstan dead when Olaf returned. The conflict was resolved for good only in the following years.

## Why Brunanburh May Matter More Than Hastings

The usual story makes 1066 the turning point of English history. But already in 937 the question was whether one king would rule Anglo-Saxons, Danes and Norsemen. Had the coalition won, the realm would probably have dissolved again into smaller kingdoms, with Dublin and York as a Scandinavian power axis around the Irish Sea. Athelstan's victory kept alive the idea of a single kingdom, which his successors carried forward.

None of this should be overstated. The Viking Age in England was not over. Later Danish kings conquered the country, and in 1066 another battle with Scandinavian opponents followed, told in our article on [Stamford Bridge](/en/blog/battle-of-stamford-bridge/). The [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/) likewise shows how long Norse pressure on England lasted. Brunanburh was a turning point, but not one that decided the future alone.

## Editorial Assessment

Brunanburh is a lesson in how significance and record come apart. By every source the event was enormous, yet we know neither the place nor the numbers. What I find most persuasive is the reading that this was less a Viking raid than a power struggle between kings. The Norse stood here not for plunder but for political claims. Telling Brunanburh as a "victory over the Vikings" shortens it: Athelstan beat rivals who were partly Scots and Britons themselves. I would also resist selling the Wirral site as settled. Plausible, yes. Proven, no.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was the Battle of Brunanburh fought?

The battle is dated to 937. The exact day is not recorded. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and the Irish annals agree on the year.

### Who won at Brunanburh?

King Athelstan of Wessex and Mercia won. Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin escaped with a few men, and Constantine II of Alba lost a son on the field.

### Where was Brunanburh?

Unknown. Bromborough on the Wirral is the most likely candidate for many scholars, but there is no archaeological proof. Other proposals include Brinsworth and Burnswark.

### Why does Brunanburh matter for England?

The victory held together the newly formed rule of an English king over Wessex, Mercia and the north, and for the time being prevented a Scandinavian-led realm around York and Dublin.

### Were the Vikings alone at Brunanburh?

No. Olaf Guthfrithson fought in alliance with Constantine II of Alba and Owain of Strathclyde. It was a coalition of several rulers of the British Isles.
