There is a craft in the Old Norse texts that everyone seems to know about and nobody bothers to describe. It is called **seidr**, and it is the strangest corner of Norse magic we have any record of. The sagas mention it the way you might mention a neighbour's trade: casually, assuming the audience already understands. They name it, they fear it, they hurl it at one another as an insult. Not one of them tells us how it was actually done.

What survives is a silhouette. Seidr appears in the sources as a form of sorcery bound up with three things: seeing what is hidden or still to come, reaching into fate and fortune, and working on other people's minds. It also carries a social charge that no other magic in the North does. For a man to practise it was a disgrace. The highest god in the pantheon did it anyway.

## The trouble with our sources

Any honest account of this subject has to open with an admission. We do not possess a single voice from the pagan Viking Age explaining what seidr meant to the people who performed it. Almost everything we can hold in our hands was written down in the thirteenth century, in Christian Iceland, several generations after the old religion had ended.

The key texts are few enough to list. The **Poetic Edda** preserves poems in which seeresses speak and in which seidr becomes an accusation. Snorri Sturluson's **Heimskringla** opens with the **Ynglinga saga**, which recasts the old gods as migrating kings and, in doing so, turns surprisingly specific about their magical skills. And among the **Icelandic sagas**, the **Saga of Erik the Red** gives us the single most detailed surviving account of a prophecy actually being performed.

So we are reading about heathen magic through baptised scribes with a clerical education, working in a tradition where sorcery was a loaded theological category. Some of them clearly wanted the old practice to look bad. What reaches us labelled as shameful need not have carried that label in the pagan centuries. Keep that caveat within reach for the rest of this article.

## Seidr, galdr and spá: Norse magic had more than one name

Popular accounts tend to sweep everything uncanny in the North into one bin and write "seidr" on the lid. The sources are considerably more careful than that. They use several distinct words, and they do not use them interchangeably.

**Seidr** (Old Norse *seiðr*) is the practice at issue here. Its etymology is disputed. Scholars have proposed a link to a word for cord or binding, and separately a connection with singing. Neither reading is settled.

**Galdr** is something else. The word belongs to the verb *gala*, to crow or to chant, and it means the spoken or sung charm, the formula that does the work. Galdr is language with an effect.

**Spá** is prophecy itself, the act of foreseeing. The same root sits behind the old northern British *spae-wife* for a fortune-teller. The woman who practises it is the **völva**, whose title connects to *völr*, a staff. She is, quite literally, the staff-bearer. Hold on to that detail; it matters again when we reach the archaeology.

| Term | What it denotes | Where it becomes visible |
|---|---|---|
| **seiðr** | Sorcery involving divination, working on fate, and influencing the minds of others | Ynglinga saga (Heimskringla), Lokasenna, Icelandic sagas |
| **galdr** | The spoken or chanted charm; the effective word | Poetic Edda, including the charm-lists of the Hávamál |
| **spá** | Prophecy, the act of foreseeing; the work of the *völva* | Völuspá ("Prophecy of the Seeress"), Saga of Erik the Red |
| **ergi** | Accusation of unmanliness and lost honour; adjective *argr* | Lokasenna, Ynglinga saga, Old Norse legal material |

The categories bleed into one another in practice, and the texts are not perfectly consistent. But they show that the Norse held no vague, undifferentiated notion of "magic". They separated the chanted word from the act of seeing, and both of those from the particular, socially awkward craft called seidr.

## Freyja brings the craft to the Aesir

The Ynglinga saga tells us where seidr came from, and the answer is not Asgard. It belonged to the **Vanir**, the second and older family of gods, the one associated with fertility, wealth and growing things. It was [Freyja](/en/blog/freyja-goddess) who brought the art among the Aesir once the two families had settled their war.

That is worth pausing over, because it is genuinely odd. The most powerful knowledge-technology in the Norse cosmos is an import. It does not originate with the warrior gods around Odin. It arrives from a sphere concerned with abundance and increase, and it arrives in a woman's hands.

And [Odin](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom) takes it up. The same saga describes him as a master of seidr, able to discern the fates of men and to bring misfortune, sickness and death, to strip a man of his wits or his strength. Anyone who knows the Odin material will not be startled. The eye in Mimir's well, the nine nights on the tree: this is a god who pays whatever a piece of knowledge costs. Seidr is one more such payment. The difference is that the currency this time is not his body but his standing.

## Ergi: the word that made seidr dangerous for men

Here is the heart of the matter. In the Ynglinga saga, Snorri states plainly that so much disgrace attached to the working of seidr that men were held unfit for it, and that the craft was therefore carried on chiefly by women. The Old Norse term carrying that charge is **ergi**, and its adjective is **argr**.

*Ergi* resists tidy translation. "Unmanliness" gets part of it, "cowardice" another part, and neither is sufficient. It names an attack on the core of a man's honour, and it carries a sexual implication: the suggestion that he has taken the passive, receiving role understood as belonging to women. The insult weighed heavily enough that Old Norse legal material treats it as the kind of slander a man could not simply absorb.

Which is exactly what Loki throws at Odin. In the **Lokasenna**, the flyting poem of the Poetic Edda in which Loki works his way around the hall naming each god's worst secret, he accuses the Allfather of having practised seidr and of having conducted himself as a völva does. This is not light mockery. It is the gravest charge available in that culture, aimed at its highest god, in public, in front of everyone.

What strikes our editorial team most is that the scene only works if both halves stay true at once. Odin is the supreme knower, and Odin is shameable. The poem does not let him off the hook. It records that he crossed a line, and it records that the crossing carried a cost he understood in advance and accepted regardless. That is not a flaw in the portrait of Odin. It is the portrait sharpened to its finest point.

## Thorbjorg on the high seat

All of this stays abstract until one particular passage. The **Saga of Erik the Red** describes the visit of a seeress named **Thorbjorg** to a farm in the Greenland settlement during a winter of hunger and poor catches. It is the fullest surviving description of such an occasion, and the remarkable thing is how little it resembles sorcery and how much it resembles procedure.

Thorbjorg is received with ceremony. The saga lingers over her equipment in a way that stands out sharply against the usual economy of saga prose: a blue cloak, a staff, gloves of catskin. She is fed, she is questioned, and she is led to a raised seat. Then one thing is still missing. Women are needed who know a particular song, without which she cannot carry out the prophecy at all. Only once a singer is found can the seeress speak and give the household its answer.

There is no need to romanticise the scene, but it would be a shame to miss what it demonstrates. The performance is public. It sits inside a framework of hospitality, rank and obligation. And it depends on the participation of the people watching: without their singing, nothing happens. The seeress is no solitary witch at the edge of the woods. She is a specialist who has been sent for, seated in a place of honour, working for an audience that has to work alongside her.

## Iron staffs in women's graves: what the spade actually gives us

Does any of this leave a trace in the ground? Several richly furnished female burials of the Viking Age have produced elaborate rod-shaped iron objects, among them finds from the cemetery at **Birka** in Sweden and from the vicinity of the Danish ring fortress at **Fyrkat**. The objects are finely made, they turn up in graves with unusually rich furnishing, and they correspond to no clearly identifiable household tool. In scholarship, prominently in the work of Neil Price, they are widely interpreted as **seidr staffs**, an argument that sits comfortably with *völva* meaning staff-bearer.

This is where scepticism earns its keep, and it cuts both ways. The interpretation is well argued, and in our view it is the most convincing reading currently on the table. It is not proven. An iron rod does not come with a label, and the boundary between a ritual implement and a status object is close to invisible archaeologically. Anyone presenting these finds as hard evidence for seidr has gone a step further than the material allows. Anyone waving them away has a conspicuous pattern to explain.

**Myth vs fact: common misunderstandings about Viking magic**

- **Myth:** There was a fixed, elaborated ritual system with a known sequence. **Fact:** No manual, no instructions, no complete description survives. We have mentions, accusations and one detailed scene.
- **Myth:** Seidr was worked by carving runes. **Fact:** The sources tie seidr above all to song, to the seat and to prophecy. Magic through carved signs belongs to other contexts entirely.
- **Myth:** Only women practised it. **Fact:** The texts know perfectly well of men who did, Odin included. The difference lay not in ability but in the social bill that arrived afterwards.
- **Myth:** The "seidr staff" is archaeologically confirmed. **Fact:** A plausible and widely held interpretation, not a demonstrated one.
- **Myth:** Seidr was simply Norse shamanism. **Fact:** The parallels with circumpolar practice are striking and actively discussed, but the equation is contested and reaches beyond what the sources support.

## What seidr actually tells us

We end with a craft whose name we know, whose reputation we can measure and whose method is lost. That is unsatisfying, and it is more honest to leave the gap open than to fill it with invention. Modern reception, from novels to present-day spiritual movements, has long since turned seidr into something else entirely; that is a separate subject and should not be folded back into the medieval record.

What makes seidr worth an article is not the supernatural element. It is the friction. Here was a society that defined manhood through honour, weapons and endurance, and its most powerful technique for knowing things sat in women's hands, and it knew this perfectly well. The greatest of its gods commanded that technique and had to endure being jeered at for it. Both facts sit side by side in the same texts, unreconciled.

Perhaps that is the most interesting thing seidr has to say about the Viking Age. Not that these people believed in magic. Rather that they lived with a practice they held to be both indispensable and humiliating, and that they seem to have tolerated the contradiction without ever tidying it away.
