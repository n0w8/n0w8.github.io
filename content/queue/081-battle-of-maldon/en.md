The **Battle of Maldon** in August 991 killed the English commander Byrhtnoth and was followed almost at once by the first big payment to the Vikings under King Aethelred: 10,000 pounds of silver, according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. It was the start of a policy that cost England dearly for more than twenty years. This article looks at why a defeat on an Essex riverbank mattered so much, and at what we can actually say about that day.

## The Battle of Maldon: What the Sources Say

Be warned first: the evidence is thin. The **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle** covers Maldon in a few lines. A Viking fleet came to Folkestone in 991, moved on to Sandwich, then Ipswich, then Maldon, where Ealdorman **Byrhtnoth** was killed. Afterwards, the Chronicle says, it was decided for the first time to pay tribute to the raiders, on the advice of Archbishop Sigeric of Canterbury, and the sum was 10,000 pounds.

Everything else comes from the Old English poem **The Battle of Maldon**. Only about 325 lines survive, with the beginning and end missing. The sole manuscript burned in the Cotton library fire of 1731, so we depend on a transcript made earlier. Whether the poet was an eyewitness or retold the story is unknown. The tone is heroic: this is poetry, not a report. Still, it is a rare view of the Vikings through the eyes of their opponents.

## How It Unfolded: A Causeway, the Tide and One Costly Decision

In the poem the Norsemen camp on an island in the River Pante, now the Blackwater. At low tide a narrow causeway links it to the shore. Byrhtnoth drew up his men on the bank. A Viking messenger offered peace in return for silver. Byrhtnoth refused, saying in effect that he would pay in spears.

The causeway was so narrow that only a few men could cross at once, and three English warriors held it at first. Then comes the scene scholars still argue about: the Vikings asked to be allowed across for a fair fight, and Byrhtnoth agreed. The poet calls it **ofermod**, a word that can mean overconfidence, pride or simply great courage. Some read it as a leader's blunder, others as a heroic ideal.

The fighting is described with spears, shields and swords, much like the tactics in our piece on the [shield wall](/en/blog/viking-shield-wall-battle-tactic/). Byrhtnoth was wounded and killed. Some of his men fled, among them a Godric, son of Odda, and the poem condemns them harshly. The retainers who stayed fought on, and their vows are the most famous passage of the text.

## Myth or Fact? What Holds Up

| Claim | Status |

| Battle at Maldon in August 991 | secure (Chronicle, poem) |
| Byrhtnoth, ealdorman of Essex, was killed | secure |
| A tribute of 10,000 pounds followed | secure (Chronicle) |
| The Viking leader was Olaf Tryggvason | disputed, later tradition |
| Causeway and tide as in the poem | plausible, not proven in detail |
| Exact number of fighters | unknown |
| Byrhtnoth buried at Ely | tradition of the Liber Eliensis |

The poem does not name the Viking leader. Later sources bring in **Olaf Tryggvason**, later king of Norway, but that is a later attribution, and the Chronicle does not name him for Maldon. Whoever puts him there is relying on legend.

The battle site is not settled either. The island is usually identified with Northey Island in the Blackwater, a tidal island with a causeway. That fits the description, but coastlines and river courses have shifted since the tenth century, so it cannot be proven.

## Byrhtnoth: Who Was He?

Byrhtnoth was ealdorman of Essex and a well-connected magnate who supported monasteries such as Ely and Ramsey. Tradition makes him unusually tall. The **Liber Eliensis**, a twelfth-century text, says the monks of Ely recovered his body; the head was missing and was replaced with a ball of wax. When the tomb at Ely was opened in 1769, a skeleton without a skull was found, of a size that matched the tradition. Whether it really is Byrhtnoth cannot be proven, but the find does not contradict the story.

He was an experienced man, attested as ealdorman since the 950s, who led his retinue in the old spirit of loyalty to a lord. That loyalty is what the poem immortalised in its best-known lines: courage must be the harder, heart the bolder, spirit the greater, as our strength grows less.

## The Viking Army: Who Came to Essex in 991?

We do not know how large the force was. The poem gives no figure and the Chronicle is silent for 991. Some later accounts speak of 93 ships, but that number belongs in the Chronicle to the attack on London in 994 and should not be moved to Maldon. The only certain thing is the route: Folkestone, Sandwich, Ipswich, Maldon. The fleet worked its way up the coast plundering, as such raids usually did. It apparently did not seek a decisive battle, only loot, and Maldon was one stop.

Byrhtnoth's force, meanwhile, was called up from the county. It consisted of the ealdorman's retinue and men of the local defence levy, the fyrd. Unlike the Vikings, many of them were not professional warriors. That explains why the poem names the flight of some men so sharply, and why it stresses the loyalty of the retainers so strongly: neither could be taken for granted.

## After Maldon: The Years of Silver

In 994 Vikings returned, this time under Olaf Tryggvason and the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard, and attacked London. Again silver was paid, 16,000 pounds, and Olaf was baptised and promised not to attack England again. The peace did not last. The cycle of raid, tribute and renewed raid shaped the following decades and ended in 1016, when Sweyn's son Cnut the Great became king of England. Maldon sits at the start of a chain at whose end the Vikings no longer just plundered England but ruled it.

## Why Aethelred Paid: Maldon and the Danegeld

After Maldon the king chose silver. Aethelred II was only in his mid-twenties in 991, having come to the throne as a child in 978. His nickname **"the Unready"** comes from Old English *unræd*, "ill-advised", a pun on his name Aethelred, "noble counsel". It has nothing to do with being unprepared.

The payments rose steadily. According to the Chronicle and later research, about 16,000 pounds were paid in 994, around 24,000 in 1002, 36,000 in 1007 and 48,000 in 1012. How the system worked is described in our article on the [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/): taxes were raised, silver flowed north, and the attackers came back. Finds of Anglo-Saxon silver coins in Scandinavia are the material evidence.

Would defence have been the better choice? Payment bought time and spared harvests and towns, but it made England an even more attractive target. That is our assessment, which matches most of the research but not every historian.

## Our Take: Blunder or Bad Luck?

We think the decision at the causeway is plausible as a mistake, but not as the reason for the defeat. An ealdorman who pins an enemy on an island has a tactical aim: he must stop the army from moving inland. That he let the enemy cross anyway can be read as pride, or as calculation, a wish to force a decision while his men were fresh. The poem does not judge clearly. ## FAQ

### When was the Battle of Maldon?
In August 991, at the River Blackwater in Essex. The sources do not give the exact day with certainty.

### Who won the Battle of Maldon?
The Vikings. The English commander Byrhtnoth was killed, and soon after King Aethelred paid 10,000 pounds of silver.

### What is the poem "The Battle of Maldon"?
An Old English heroic poem of about 325 surviving lines. The manuscript burned in 1731, and the text survives through an earlier transcript.

### Was Olaf Tryggvason at Maldon?
Later sources say so. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle does not name him for Maldon, so it remains disputed.

### How much Danegeld was paid?
According to the Chronicle 10,000 pounds in 991, and later sums up to 48,000 pounds in 1012.

## Conclusion

Maldon was no decisive Viking victory, but it was a turning point. It showed that silver was easier to raise than soldiers, and England paid for years. The poem also preserves the picture of a warrior culture that valued loyalty above survival.
