# Huginn and Muninn: What Odin's Ravens Really Mean

In a single stanza of the Poetic Edda, Odin admits to a worry, and the worry is about two ravens: **Huginn and Muninn** fly out every day, and the Allfather fears they will not come back, Muninn even more than Huginn. That one stanza from Grímnismál is the foundation for almost everything said about the two birds today. The rest is interpretation, retelling and a lot of pop culture. This article separates what the sources actually give us from what later centuries read into them.

## What the sources say about Huginn and Muninn

The names are quickly explained, but even here caution pays. **Huginn** belongs to Old Norse *hugr*, which means more than "thought": mind, intent, reason, even temper. **Muninn** comes from *muna*, "to remember", and may mean "the mindful one". Snorri Sturluson gives the plain glosses "thought" and "memory" in Gylfaginning (chapter 38), and they are still the standard. He also writes that two ravens sit on Odin's shoulders and whisper into his ear everything they have seen and heard. Because of them poets call Odin *Hrafnaguð*, "raven god".

The key evidence at a glance:

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| Grímnismál, st. 20 | Both fly daily over the earth. Odin fears for Huginn, even more for Muninn |
| Gylfaginning (Snorri), ch. 38 | They sit on Odin's shoulders and report all they have seen and heard |
| Ynglinga saga (Snorri), ch. 7 | Odin has two ravens he taught to speak, who bring him news |
| Hrafnsmál (Þorbjörn Hornklofi) | A conversation between a valkyrie and a raven about King Harald Fairhair |
| Landnámabók | Hrafna-Flóki releases ravens to find land and so discovers Iceland |
| Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, 878 | The Vikings carry a banner called "Raven", captured in Devon |

What stands out is how thin the material is at the core. One Edda stanza, one chapter in Snorri, one note in the Ynglinga saga. That the ravens are among Odin's best-known symbols has less to do with the number of sources than with their vividness. To understand them, start with the Grímnismál stanza.

## Grímnismál 20: the Allfather's worry

Grímnismál is a poem of instruction in which Odin, held captive as "Grímnir" by a fire, lists his world: the halls of the gods, the world tree, the animals in its branches. In stanza 20 he says, in essence: Huginn and Muninn fly every day over the wide earth, I fear for Huginn that he will not return, but I worry more about Muninn.

What follows from this? The usual thought is: thought is quick and comes back by itself, while memory is fragile and can be lost. A god whose power rests on knowledge does not fear losing strength but losing memory. That fits a culture where genealogies, laws and poems were passed on orally. A forgotten stanza was a lost stanza.

I find this reading plausible, but it remains a reading. The text says only that Odin is worried. It does not say why. Anyone selling a certainty beyond the stanza is telling more than the source can carry.

> The god who gave an eye for wisdom fears most for his memory.

How Odin came by his knowledge in the first place is covered in [Odin, the Allfather](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/): the sacrificed eye, the runes and the ravens in context.

## Myth or fact: five common claims

A fair amount has piled up around the ravens. Here are the most common claims, checked against the sources:

- **"Huginn and Muninn are Odin's spies."** Partly true. Snorri describes them as informants who tell him all they hear and see. "Spy" is a modern word, but the sense is close.
- **"The ravens always sit on his shoulders."** Only in Snorri, and there they fly out by day and return for the meal. The Edda stanza shows them travelling, not perched.
- **"The Vikings thought ravens were sacred."** There is no evidence for that in such general terms. Ravens were carrion birds of the battlefield and so tied to Odin, god of the slain. Sacred in the sense of taboo, the bird was not.
- **"Huginn and Muninn stand for Odin's mind and soul."** That is a modern interpretation built on the names. Not absurd, but not in the text.
- **"Viking finds are full of images of the ravens."** Again, caution. Ravens and birds appear on helmets, pendants and picture stones, but whether a particular bird means Huginn or Muninn can almost never be shown.

## The raven in the Viking world: navigator, banner, battlefield

The raven was more everyday in the North than mythology suggests. The Landnámabók, the Icelandic Book of Settlements, tells of **Hrafna-Flóki Vilgerðarson**, who sailed to Iceland around 860 with three ravens. He released them one after another. The first flew back to the ship, the second circled and returned, the third flew straight ahead. Flóki followed it and found land. A good story, and one that reflects the practice of using birds to find land.

Then there is the **raven banner**. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle reports for the year 878 a battle in Devon in which a Viking leader fell and the banner they called "Raven" was captured. Later chroniclers link it to the sons of Ragnar Lodbrok, as discussed in the article on [Ragnar Lodbrok](/en/blog/ragnar-lothbrok-legend-truth/). And coins from York in the 940s show a raven, pointing to the imagery of a warlord.

The poem Hrafnsmál by the skald Þorbjörn Hornklofi finally shows the raven as listener and narrator: a valkyrie and a raven talk about the deeds of King Harald. The raven knows what happened on the battlefield because it feeds there. Knowledge and carrion belong together in this bird, which explains why it suits Odin.

## Does Odin fly himself? The shaman reading

The Ynglinga saga adds another dimension. Snorri writes that Odin could change shape: his body lay as if asleep or dead while he travelled to distant lands as a bird or beast. From this grew the idea that Huginn and Muninn are nothing other than Odin's own projected soul parts, that is, what the body leaves behind in a trance.

The reading is tempting and discussed in scholarship. It has two weaknesses. First, the description of the shape-changing comes from Snorri, who wrote in the 13th century, about two centuries after the Viking Age ended, and as a Christian. Second, it remains open whether he reports old tradition or, in euhemerist fashion, recasts a god as a wise human king. The term "shamanism" itself comes from Siberia and is carried over to the North without direct evidence.

My assessment: the ravens as an image of what Odin learns beyond his senses is a sensible reading. As an attested practice of Norse priests or sorcerers it should not be passed off. They fit the worldview anyway, as the [world tree Yggdrasil](/en/blog/yggdrasil-world-tree/) shows, with animals and messengers constantly climbing up and down.

## Timeline: from skaldic verse to pop symbol

- **around 860 (narrated date):** Hrafna-Flóki releases ravens, recorded in the Landnámabók
- **878:** The banner "Raven" is captured in Devon (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle)
- **around 900:** Hrafnsmál by Þorbjörn Hornklofi, a raven as the conversation partner of a valkyrie
- **940s:** Ravens on coins from York
- **around 1220:** Snorri sums up Huginn and Muninn in Gylfaginning and Ynglinga saga
- **19th and 20th centuries:** The ravens become standard motifs of romantic Nordic imagery and later of fantasy

## Why the ravens still fascinate

Two birds named Thought and Memory that fly the world each day: that is an image that needs no explanation. It works in every age because it holds a truth about knowledge, namely that you have to gather it and that you can lose it. That is exactly the worry the Edda puts in Odin's mouth.

Biology helps too. Common ravens are exceptionally intelligent, learn fast, remember faces and can imitate sounds. Anyone who has watched them understands why a people who saw ravens daily at the farm, the harbour and the battlefield tied them to knowledge. The Ynglinga saga even mentions that Odin taught his ravens to speak.

How Odin's path ends at the end of time is the subject of the article on [Ragnarök](/en/blog/ragnarok/). Remarkably, we hear nothing more about the ravens there: the sources are silent on what becomes of Huginn and Muninn when Odin is swallowed by the wolf.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do Huginn and Muninn mean?

Huginn means roughly "thought" or "mind", Muninn "memory" or "the mindful one". The names go back to Old Norse *hugr* and *muna*.

### Why does Odin worry more about Muninn?

In Grímnismál (st. 20) Odin says he fears for both, but more for Muninn. The usual reading: memory is more fragile than thought. The text does not state the reason.

### Were Huginn and Muninn Odin's spies?

According to Snorri they report to him daily what they have seen and heard. "Spies" is a modern word, but the sense roughly fits.

### Is there archaeological evidence for Huginn and Muninn?

Bird motifs appear on helmets, pendants and picture stones, but a clear link to the two ravens is almost never possible.

### What happens to the ravens at Ragnarök?

The sources say nothing. They are not mentioned in connection with the end of the world.
