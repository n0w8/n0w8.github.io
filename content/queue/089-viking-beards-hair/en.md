**Viking hairstyles and beards** are almost entirely missing from the archaeological record: hair rots in the ground, and no Viking Age head has reached us with its haircut intact. What survives is a pile of combs, a few pairs of tweezers, some royal nicknames, a scolding letter from the scholar Alcuin written in 793, and an Arab traveller's report from 922. Out of those thin traces, films have built the braided beard with beads and the shaved-sides look. How much of it holds up? This article sorts what is documented from what is plausible and what costume designers made up.

## Why the evidence is so thin

Hair is organic material, and organic material only survives under special conditions: bog, ice, very dry or permanently waterlogged soil. We know of no Viking Age find that preserves an original hairstyle. Graves do not help much either, because bones say nothing about haircuts. Archaeologists have to work indirectly. Steve Ashby, in a paper in *Internet Archaeology*, points out that beards are all but invisible in the archaeological record and have to be inferred from grooming tools, images and written sources.

That is the honest starting point. Everything beyond it is interpretation, sometimes well supported, sometimes shaky. Anyone selling a particular Viking haircut as "confirmed" is overreaching.

## What combs, tweezers and ear scoops tell us

The hardest evidence is the toolkit. The National Museum of Denmark lists combs of wood, bone and antler among the most common finds of the period, and small grooming sets with tweezers and ear scoops turn up regularly. How the combs were built, and why they were made in bulk at Hedeby and York, is covered in our piece on [Viking hygiene and grooming](/en/blog/viking-hygiene-grooming/). For our question, the point is that hair care was widespread, not a luxury for a few. A comb, though, does not tell you whether its owner wore braids, kept a short crop or was simply fighting lice. It proves care, not style.

One more clue comes from a foreign eyewitness. Around 922 the Arab envoy Ibn Fadlan described how the Rus on the Volga washed their faces and hair every morning in the same basin, which horrified him more than it impressed him. It is no haircut description, but it shows that appearance mattered. Ibn Fadlan is also the main source on Rus body decoration, see [Viking tattoos](/en/blog/viking-tattoos-meaning/).

## Bynames as hair descriptions

The richest source for hair and beards is the nicknames in the sagas, because nicknames often record what contemporaries noticed.

| Byname | Person | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hárfagri | Harald, first king of Norway in saga tradition | "Fairhair" | Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, c. 1230 |
| Lúfa | the same Harald, before his haircut | "Shaggy" | Snorri, Heimskringla |
| Tjúguskegg | Sweyn, king of the Danes, died 1014 | "Forkbeard" | Norse and English tradition |
| Skalla-Grímr | father of the skald Egill | "Bald Grim" | Egil's saga |
| Eiríkr rauði | Erik the Red | "the Red", probably after hair or beard | Sagas written down after 1200 |

The problem is plain: most of these texts were written centuries after the events. Snorri's story of Harald is literary. According to Snorri, Harald vowed not to cut or comb his hair until he ruled all of Norway. Ten years later Earl Ragnvald cut it, and "Shaggy" became "Fairhair". It is a good tale about vows and royal power, but we cannot say it happened that way. What it does show is that long, well-kept hair signalled rank for Snorri's thirteenth-century audience. Uncut hair as a vow has a long tradition in many cultures.

"Forkbeard" is a case of its own. That Sweyn wore a split beard is likely, but it is inferred from the nickname, not confirmed by any portrait. The National Museum words it just as cautiously.

There is also a frequency argument. Names built on *skegg* (beard) recur in the Norse name stock, as do names alluding to hair colour or baldness. That suggests hair and beard caught the eye in daily life and lent themselves to nicknames, much like "Tall" or "Red" today. How men actually trimmed their beards remains open. Reading that out of a byname means reading more into it than it contains.

## Alcuin and the fashion of the heathens

Perhaps the most intriguing source comes from an opponent. The Anglo-Saxon scholar Alcuin of York wrote to the king of Northumbria after the raid on Lindisfarne in 793 and reproached people for imitating the heathens in the way they wore their hair and beards. If that is right, Norse fashion had made enough of an impression for Englishmen to copy it before the Vikings settled in England. Alcuin does not say what the style looked like. But the letter shows that contemporaries perceived a recognisable "pagan" look. The background of the raid is in our article on the [Lindisfarne raid](/en/blog/lindisfarne-raid/).

Editorial view: this is the strongest evidence that hair and beards were a visible marker among the Norse. It is also the only one of its kind, and it comes from a critic with every reason to exaggerate.

## Images and figurines

Pictorial sources are rare and small: metal figurines, textile scenes, picture stones, carvings. They mostly show men with a moustache or full beard, and women often with long hair tied at the nape. But these images are stylised, and we never know whether a figure is a real person, a god or an ideal. Deriving a "typical hairstyle" from a handful of figurines is like reconstructing today's fashion from a comic strip.

## Myth versus fact: five images from film and games

- **"Vikings wore braided beards with beads."** Not documented as a general look. Braids and ribbons are conceivable, but no secure find shows beads in a beard. Popular sites repeat the claim without naming a source.
- **"Everyone had the shaved-sides cut."** Some images are interpreted that way, but there is no broad evidence. The cut is mostly a modern trademark.
- **"Vikings were scruffy."** The mass of combs and the foreign accounts argue against it. Grooming tools this common do not fit a dirty, matted crowd.
- **"A Viking without a full beard was no real Viking."** Pure fantasy. Beards were common as far as we can tell, but nothing says they were compulsory.
- **"Women wore their hair loose and flowing."** Rarely documented. Images point to tied-up styles. More on women's lives in the article on [women in the Viking Age](/en/blog/viking-women-role-society/).

## Eye paint, jewellery and the overall look

Appearance meant more than hair. The Arab-Andalusian traveller al-Tartushi, who visited Hedeby in the tenth century, reports that men and women there wore eye paint that kept their beauty from fading. That fits the grooming tools and shows that vanity in the North is no modern invention. For clothing and ornaments see [Viking clothing and jewelry](/en/blog/viking-clothing-jewelry/).

## How to reconstruct a Viking look today

If you want a defensible portrayal, stick to what is documented: clean, combed, medium-length hair, perhaps tied at the nape, with a moustache or a tidy full beard depending on the person. Anything more should be flagged as interpretation. Good reenactment groups work this way and leave out the beads when they have no source. The upside: a look you can justify is also more convincing.

## Conclusion

Viking hairstyles and beards remain a field of probabilities. Certain: people cared about grooming, shown by countless combs. Plausible: beards and medium-length hair. Pure invention: the uniform look from film and games. The truth is less spectacular, but no less interesting: people over a thousand years ago combed, trimmed and showed their rank on their heads, just as we do.

## FAQ

### Did Vikings have beards?
Probably most men did, although it cannot be proven directly. Bynames like Forkbeard and the images on figurines point that way, as does Alcuin's remark on the hair and beard fashion of the heathens.

### Did Vikings shave the sides of their heads?
That is not documented. Some images are read that way, but no widespread fashion can be derived from them. The shaved-sides cut is mainly a modern image.

### Why was Harald called Fairhair?
According to Snorri Sturluson, Harald vowed not to cut his hair until he ruled Norway. After ten years Earl Ragnvald cut it, and the nickname changed from "Shaggy" to "Fairhair". The story was written down late and is shaped as literature.

### Did Vikings take care of their hair?
Yes. Combs are among the most common finds of the Viking Age, alongside tweezers and ear scoops.
