**Reading runes** takes six steps, and the hardest part is not the letters but the ambiguity: the Younger Futhark of the Viking Age managed with just 16 signs although Old Norse had many more sounds, so a single sign stands for several of them. Once you know that, an inscription stops looking like text in an alphabet and starts looking like a puzzle with a method.

This article follows the route runologists take, from identifying the rune row to the translation. It includes a table of the 16 Younger Futhark runes with their sound values and two real examples, one from around 400 and one from 965. If you need the basics of the older row first, start with our guide to the [Elder Futhark](/en/blog/elder-futhark-runes/).

## Step 1: Identify the rune row

Before you interpret a single sign, you need to know which system you are looking at. There are three main ones:

- **Elder Futhark:** 24 signs, attested from about AD 150 to 800. The Kylver stone from Gotland (around 400) carries the whole row.
- **Anglo-Saxon futhorc:** an extended row of up to 33 signs for Old English.
- **Younger Futhark:** 16 signs, used across Denmark, Norway and Sweden in the Viking Age.

The first clue is the number of distinct signs and the date of the find. A memorial stone of the 11th century from Sweden is almost certainly Younger Futhark. A golden horn from the 5th century belongs to the older row. Dedicated signs for **j**, **p** and **z** no longer exist in the Younger Futhark, so finding them points to the older system.

## Step 2: Spot the direction and the rune forms

Runes are usually read left to right, but not always. Older inscriptions also run right to left, and some change direction line by line (boustrophedon). On rune stones the text often follows a **serpent band** winding around the field, and the start is usually at the lower left.

The Younger Futhark adds a twist: two variants of writing. **Long-branch runes** were common in Denmark, **short-twig runes** in Sweden and Norway. The same rune looks different in each, the short-twig form being much simplified. Both belong to the same row, they are not different scripts.

## Step 3: Split the words

Modern text has spaces, rune inscriptions mostly do not. On many Viking stones **two or three dots** (: or ⁝) separate the words, while older inscriptions have no division at all. Where dividers exist, the work is easy. Where they are missing, you have to guess word boundaries and test whether the result makes sense in grammar and content.

Then there are **bind runes**, in which two signs share one stave. At first glance they look like a single rune but they are two letters. Carvers also made mistakes and corrections, because people made errors back then too.

## Step 4: Transliterate sign by sign

Now each rune is converted into a Latin letter, the **transliteration**. One rule applies: write what is there, not what is meant. The table shows the 16 Younger Futhark runes and the sounds they can carry.

| Rune | Name | Transliteration | Possible sounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| ᚠ | fé | f | f |
| ᚢ | úr | u | u, o, v, y, ø |
| ᚦ | þurs | þ | þ, ð |
| ᚬ | áss | ą | nasal o or a |
| ᚱ | reið | r | r |
| ᚴ | kaun | k | k, g, ŋ |
| ᚼ | hagall | h | h |
| ᚾ | nauðr | n | n |
| ᛁ | ísa | i | i, e |
| ᛅ | ár | a | a, æ |
| ᛋ | sól | s | s |
| ᛏ | týr | t | t, d |
| ᛒ | bjarkan | b | b, p |
| ᛘ | maðr | m | m |
| ᛚ | lǫgr | l | l |
| ᛦ | ýr | R | final R |

The table shows the problem: ᚢ must be read as u, o, v or y depending on context, ᚴ as k or g, ᛏ as t or d. The same string of signs can therefore yield several words. Runologists settle such cases by context, grammar and parallels.

After transliteration comes **normalisation**: the transliteration is rendered as regular Old Norse, with vowels, endings and word forms as grammar requires. Only at this stage does a string of signs become a sentence.

## Step 5: An example in the Younger Futhark

The best-known example is the large Jelling stone in Denmark, which King Harald Bluetooth had erected around 965. The inscription begins in transliteration with **haraltr : kunukR : baþ : kaurua : kubl : þausi**. Normalised this reads *Haraldr konungr bað gørva kumbl þessi*, in English: "King Harald ordered these monuments made." You can watch the ambiguity at work: "kunukR" stands for *konungr*, "king", where ᚴ is read as k, ᚢ as o and ᛦ as final R. More on the site in our article on the [Jelling stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/).

The formula behind it is typical: "X raised this stone in memory of Y." Once you have read a few dozen rune stones you recognise the structure at once and can often fill gaps in the text. The great majority of surviving stones, about 3,000 across Scandinavia and most of them in Sweden, follow this pattern.

## Step 6: An example in the Elder Futhark

The counterpart lies about 550 years earlier: the **golden horns of Gallehus** in Denmark, made around 400. One bore the inscription **ek hlewagastiR holtijaR horna tawido**, "I, Hlewagastir, son of Holt (or: from Holt), made the horn." The horns were stolen and melted down in 1802, and only drawings and replicas survive, which shows why documentation matters. The meaning of "holtijaR" is still not settled, a reminder that even experienced scholars offer different readings of a single word.

The script here is still close to speech. The forms are Proto-Norse, older than anything a Viking stone offers. The futhark itself changed too, shrinking from 24 signs to 16 while the language grew more complex.

## Tools: where to look up real inscriptions

You do not have to travel to Jelling to practise. Specialists use public databases:

- **Rundata** (the Scandinavian Runic-text Database at Uppsala University): transliteration, normalisation and translation of the Nordic rune inscriptions.
- **Kiel Runenprojekt:** a database of Elder Futhark inscriptions.
- **Danmarks Runeindskrifter:** the Danish inscriptions with photographs.

Each inscription has a signum there, for example **DR 42** for the large Jelling stone, **Ög 136** for the Rök stone in Östergötland and **U** for Uppland. With more than 700 signs the Rök stone is one of the longest rune inscriptions anywhere and partly written in cipher runes, a case for advanced readers.

Everyday finds belong here too: at Bryggen in Bergen, more than 600 inscribed wooden sticks turned up, among them trade notes, love messages and prayers. They show that runes were not only for monuments and magic but an ordinary writing tool. That fits the picture of the [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/), where merchants needed such notes.

## Our take: why beginners start at the wrong end

In our view the biggest mistake is the question "What does this rune mean?". That is the esoteric framing. The finds show that runes are sounds, not symbols with fixed meanings. To read, you must learn sound values, ambiguities and formulas, not meanings. It makes the subject more sober but also much more rewarding, because from the third stone on you start recognising words. Can you read an inscription alone after a weekend? Simple memorial formulas, yes. Anything else needs a database and a dictionary.

## FAQ

### Can you read runes without knowing Old Norse?

Transliteration, yes, translation only in part. With the table above you can transliterate a stone. For normalisation you need basic Old Norse or the Rundata database, which supplies ready translations.

### Which direction do you read runes?

Mostly left to right, but there are inscriptions running right to left and lines that change direction. On rune stones with a serpent band you normally start at the bottom left and follow the band.

### Why do runes have no spaces?

The script had no habit of word spacing. On Viking stones dots or colons separate the words, while on older inscriptions there is no division at all and you infer the boundaries from sense.

### How many runes does the Younger Futhark have?

It has 16 signs. Because Old Norse had more sounds, one sign often stands for several, for instance ᚢ for u, o, v and y.

### Where can I look up real rune inscriptions?

In the Rundata database at Uppsala University, in the Kiel Runenprojekt for the Elder Futhark, and in Danmarks Runeindskrifter for Denmark. All three are publicly accessible.
