Most Vikings were not raiders but farmers, and their main fight was not against an enemy but against winter: **Viking farm life** hinged on whether enough hay lay in the barn by autumn. This article follows one farm year from sowing to slaughter, as far as archaeology, the sagas and the Old Icelandic calendar allow. Much of it is solid, some of it is well-argued reconstruction, and where that is so, we say it.

## The Farm as a Business: Who Did the Work?

A typical farm was a family business of a few people, plus servants, maids and unfree workers (thralls in the sources), depending on wealth. The main house, often the [longhouse](/en/blog/viking-longhouse-life/), was kitchen, bedroom and workshop in one, and in some regions the byre stood under the same roof. Outbuildings held stores, a smithy, a weaving hut and barns.

The division of labour was no picture-book cliche. Men ploughed, mowed and built, while women, according to sagas and law texts, ran the household, controlled the stores and processed milk and wool. How strict this was in practice we do not know, because at harvest every hand counts. More on the household in our overview of [Viking daily life](/en/blog/viking-daily-life/).

## Spring: Ploughing, Sowing and Turning Out the Cattle

With the thaw, work began in the fields. The soil was worked with the ard, a scratch plough that does not turn the soil and was therefore often pulled crosswise. Farmers grew mainly barley and oats, also rye, plus peas, beans and flax for linen. In Iceland and the far north, the climate limited arable farming sharply, and livestock took centre stage.

The Old Icelandic calendar counts the start of summer from mid-April. The first summer month was Harpa, followed by Skerpla and Solmanudur, the sun month. The year was roughly split into summer and winter, and the calendar followed the farm's working year, not the other way around.

By late spring the animals left the barn. Cattle, sheep, goats and pigs had spent the winter in cramped conditions and were often lean, which is exactly why last year's fodder mattered so much.

## Summer: Shielings and the Race for Hay

In summer many farms sent part of the herd to remote summer pastures, called seter in Norway and sel in Iceland. There milk was turned into butter, cheese and skyr, usually by younger family members or maids, while the main farm tended the fields. The result is something you can taste yourself with our [skyr recipe](/en/blog/how-to-make-skyr-viking-recipe/).

| Season | Work | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Ploughing, sowing, turnout | Stores run low |
| Midsummer | Haymaking, shielings | Decides the winter |
| Late summer | Grain harvest | Sickles, threshing |
| Autumn | Slaughter | Preserving meat |
| Winter | Stall feeding, crafts | Weaving, smithing, repairs |

July was called Heyannir in Iceland, literally the hay work, and that is no accident: haymaking was the most important job of the summer. Grass was cut with a scythe, dried and carted in. A farmer with too little hay had to slaughter animals in winter or watch them starve. The Icelandic sagas know disputes between neighbours over pastures and meadows, which underlines the point.

> Editorially speaking: if you only know Vikings as warriors, you miss the engine of the age. No hay, no winter survival; no survival, no spring trade; no trade, no ships. Farming paid for everything else.

## Autumn: Harvest, Slaughter Month and Storage

Late summer brought the grain harvest with sickles, then threshing. The grain became porridge, flatbread and ale, as described in our piece on [Viking food](/en/blog/viking-food-diet/). Autumn also brought what gave the Old Icelandic month Gormanudur its name, usually read as the slaughter month, roughly mid-October to mid-November.

The reason is plain: what could not be fed through winter had to be slaughtered now. Breeding stock and dairy animals were kept, the rest was processed. Meat was salted, smoked or dried, fish was dried, and milk became long-lasting cheese or sour whey. Salt was a precious commodity.

Feasts and sacrifices also cluster around autumn in many older accounts, which speak of a winter festival at the onset of the cold season. The sources are thin and partly late, so firm statements about fixed dates are hard to make.

## Winter: Barn, Crafts and Stories

In winter the livestock stayed in and ate the stored hay. People worked indoors: wool was spun and woven, tools repaired, wood carved, nets knotted. In Iceland the midwinter falls in the month of Thorri, which still gives its name to the modern Thorrablot. How old this custom is in its present form is disputed, since it was revived in the 19th century.

Winter was also the time for company and storytelling. The cold was not only a danger on the farm but also a pause in the working year.

## The Animals: Cattle, Sheep, Pigs and the Horse

Livestock was capital, food and raw material in one. Cattle gave milk, meat, leather and dung for the fields, sheep mainly wool but also milk and meat, goats modest milk on poor ground. Pigs were fattened in woodland in many regions and slaughtered in autumn. Horses were for riding, hauling and status, and in Iceland also part of ritual life, as the sagas report. Chickens and geese belonged on many farms as well.

Bone finds from settlements show that the make-up of herds depended heavily on the region: in fertile Denmark cattle and pigs mattered more, on the North Atlantic islands sheep and goats. In Iceland, studies show that settlers brought large herds early, which quickly put pressure on grazing land. What that did to soils and vegetation is still a topic in environmental history.

The fodder problem set how many animals could be carried through winter. A farm with a big herd but a small meadow had a problem that showed in spring, when animals could barely stand. Later Icelandic sources even know customs of helping animals to their feet in spring. That hints at how tight things could get, but it is not proof for the Viking Age itself.

## Tools and Crafts: What a Farm Made Itself

A farm bought little and made much. Iron often came from local bog-ore smelting or from the village smith, and became sickles, scythes, knives, axes, nails and fittings. Wool was spun, woven on the warp-weighted loom and turned into clothing, sailcloth and blankets. More on clothing in our piece on [Viking clothing and jewellery](/en/blog/viking-clothing-jewelry/).

Loom weights of clay or stone are among the most common finds on farm sites. They show how much time textile work took. A single sail, according to experimental archaeology, needs very large amounts of wool and many months of labour. That too was farm work, mostly done in winter.

## Myth Box: Three Misconceptions About the Viking Farm

- **Myth: Vikings lived off plunder.** Fact: the bulk of the population lived from crops, livestock, fishing and hunting. Raiding and trade were an add-on for some of the men, usually sons or followers of larger farms.
- **Myth: everyone lived in the longhouse together with the cattle.** Fact: it happened, but not everywhere. Many farms had separate byres, and building styles differed widely by region and wealth.
- **Myth: winter was a time of idleness.** Fact: quite the opposite. Weaving, smithing, fishing gear and repairs filled the dark months. Only the fieldwork rested.

The picture of the farm as self-sufficient is also too simple. Salt, good iron, whetstones and silver reached the farm through trade networks, as described in our article on [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/). The farm never stood entirely alone but was tied to markets such as Hedeby.

## What Finds Tell Us About Farms

Archaeologically, Viking farms are visible mainly through house plans, animal bones and pollen analysis. The settlement of [Jarlshof on Shetland](/en/blog/jarlshof-shetland-viking-settlement/) shows how a farm was rebuilt on the same spot over generations. In Iceland, bone finds document changing livestock numbers, and pollen profiles show clearing and arable farming since the settlement. A caution applies: finds show what survived, not necessarily what was everyday.

A word on size: not every farm was alike. A small farm barely fed a family, while great farms such as the hall complex at [Borg in the Lofoten](/en/blog/borg-lofoten-viking-chieftain-hall/) were power centres able to sustain retinues, guests and feasts. That the difference lay mainly in land, livestock and labour is plausible, but hard to quantify in any single case. Any figure on farm sizes should therefore be read as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

## Conclusion: The Farm as a Calendar

The Viking farm year followed the rhythm of growth and scarcity. To understand why long-distance trade and raiding looked attractive, first ask how thin a winter could be. Farming was foundation and constraint at once, and the hay in the barn decided more than any battle.
