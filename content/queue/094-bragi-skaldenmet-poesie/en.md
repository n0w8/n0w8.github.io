The **mead of poetry** began, according to the sources, with a murder, the spit of two divine tribes and the honey of dwarves, and it cost Odin nine farmhands, three nights in a giantess's bed and a flight as an eagle. In the old North, anyone who could compose verse was thought to have tasted it. The story is far more than an adventure of the Allfather: it explains where poems come from and why the gift was never shared out fairly.

This article follows the myth step by step, sets the two main sources side by side, and asks in the end what the Norse were trying to say with it.

## The Sources: Hávamál and Snorri's Skáldskaparmál

Two texts carry the story. The **Hávamál**, "Sayings of the High One", sits in the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, written down in the 13th century. In stanzas 104 to 110 Odin himself tells, in short and almost offhand sentences, how he got the mead. **Snorri Sturluson** tells it at greater length in the **Skáldskaparmál**, the second part of his Prose Edda, composed around 1220. Snorri writes as a scholar who wants to explain to young poets where the stock phrases of skaldic verse come from, and he fills the gaps in the poem with a continuous plot.

The two versions agree on the core but differ in detail. That is normal for an orally transmitted story, and it is worth noting the differences, because popular retellings routinely blur them.

| Detail | Hávamál | Skáldskaparmál |
|---|---|---|
| Origin of the mead | not told | Kvasir, made from the spit of Æsir and Vanir |
| Vessels | Óðrerir is named | Óðrerir (kettle), Són and Boðn (jars) |
| Guardian | Suttung, daughter Gunnlöð | Suttung, daughter Gunnlöð |
| The auger | Rati is named | Rati, used by Baugi |
| Transformation | only hinted at | snake in, eagle out |
| Odin's appearance | first person | under the name Bölverk ("Evil-doer") |

## Kvasir: A Sage Made of Spit

It starts with a peace treaty. After the war between the **Æsir and the Vanir**, both sides sealed the truce by spitting into a vessel. From that spit the gods shaped a man called **Kvasir**, so wise that he could answer any question. His name is often linked to words for fermented drinks, which suits a figure that seems to come out of a brewing process. For more on the god who later claims everything for himself, see our portrait [Odin: Allfather and Seeker of Wisdom](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/).

Kvasir travelled the world and taught people. Two dwarves, **Fjalar and Galar**, invited him to their home, killed him and let his blood run into three vessels. There they mixed it with honey. The result was a mead that made whoever drank it a poet or a scholar. To the gods the dwarves claimed that Kvasir had choked on his own wisdom, because nobody had been wise enough to ask him enough questions.

You can read that as a bitter punchline. The knowledge of the gods is literally dissolved in blood, and poetry begins with a crime. Anyone who takes the skalds for harmless bards should keep this in mind.

## From the Dwarves to Suttung

The dwarves did not stay unpunished for long. Snorri says they also drowned the giant **Gilling**, persuading him to go rowing and letting the boat capsize on a rock. His wife they killed with a millstone. Gilling's son **Suttung** took revenge: he left the dwarves on a skerry that the tide would soon cover. To save their lives, they offered him the mead.

Suttung took the vessels into the mountain **Hnitbjörg**, usually translated as "the clashing cliffs". His daughter **Gunnlöð** guarded them. Her name roughly means "the one who invites to battle". So the drink that could have enriched gods and men lay in the hands of a giant who kept it to himself.

> "Odin, I believe, swore an oath on the ring: who can trust his word now?" - freely after Hávamál 110

Here Odin himself asks how trustworthy he is, right after telling the deed as a triumph. That double meaning is one reason the Hávamál feel so alive.

## Bölverk and the Nine Farmhands

The Allfather leaves Asgard to fetch the mead. Snorri has him appear as **Bölverk**, "Evil-doer". He comes to a farm where nine thralls are mowing hay. **Baugi**, Suttung's brother, is their master. Odin offers to sharpen their scythes and does so with a whetstone so fine that all of them want it. He tosses it into the air. The thralls rush for it, and in the scuffle they cut each other down with their scythes.

So Odin has done his host a harm that he must now make good. He offers to do the work of nine men for a summer. His wage is to be one sip of Suttung's mead. Baugi agrees but has no say: Suttung refuses to give up even a drop.

Read this closely. Odin is not simply a hero taking something from a villain. He first causes nine deaths that his host might mourn, and then deceives the owner's brother. It is a story told with open moral discomfort.

## The Auger, the Snake and the Eagle

When kindness fails, cunning remains. Odin talks Baugi into drilling a hole into the mountain with the auger **Rati**. Baugi drills and claims the hole goes through. Odin blows into it, and the chips fly back at him: the hole is not through, and Baugi has lied or been mistaken. Odin makes him drill on, and the second time the breath passes through. Odin turns into a **snake** and slips in.

Inside he spends **three nights with Gunnlöð**. She grants him three sips, and with each draught he empties one of the three vessels. Then he turns into an **eagle** and flies off. Suttung notices, takes eagle shape too and gives chase.

The Æsir have set out vessels in Asgard. Odin reaches them and spits the mead in, and Suttung veers away just in time. But part of the mead went out the back, says Snorri, when the pursuer was close behind. That share belongs to nobody: whoever wants can drink it, and Snorri calls it the **poetaster's share** (*skáldfífla hlutr*). The good mead goes only to the gods and to those people who can truly compose.

The Hávamál show Gunnlöð only through Odin's eyes. He reports how she served him from Óðrerir on a golden seat, and admits he repaid her badly. That the woman who kept the drink is left behind at the end can be read as the dark side of the myth.

## Why the Myth Is More Than a Story

The mead of poetry explains a very concrete phenomenon. Old Norse verse is full of circumlocutions, the **kennings**, and many point back to this story. Poetry is called "Kvasir's blood", "the dwarves' drink", "Odin's catch" or "Suttung's mead". A listener who heard such a phrase called up the whole story, and the poet proved he knew it. How the technique works is shown in [Skalds: The Poets of the Vikings](/en/blog/skalds-viking-poets/).

The myth also answers a social puzzle. Why are people not equally gifted? Because the good mead stayed with Odin and only the chosen taste it. Why are there bad poets? Because they drank from the poetaster's share. It is said with a wink, but the sting is real, since for skalds poetry was a profession paid with fame and silver at royal courts.

One example from practice: in the **Sonatorrek**, the lament for his dead sons attributed to **Egill Skallagrímsson**, the poet says Odin gave him the gift of poetry as compensation for his grief. How literally this was meant is open to interpretation, but the idea that poetry is a gift of the Allfather runs through the sources.

And Bragi? The god of poetry is one of the Æsir, his wife is **Idunn**, and in Snorri's Edda he is the narrator who talks with the sea giant Ægir, in the conversation that makes up the Skáldskaparmál. In a sense he keeps telling the myth himself. Whether the god goes back to the historical skald **Bragi Boddason** of the 9th century is disputed, and the sources give no certain answer.

## Our Assessment

We consider the mead of poetry one of the cleverest stories of Norse tradition, precisely because it is so unheroic. A god who reaches his goal through fraud, killing and seduction, and a gift that is revered and suspected at the same time: that describes quite well how the North thought about poets. They were useful, feared and never entirely trustworthy.

What cannot be shown is that the story is ancient in this form. All the detailed versions date from the 13th century, from a time when Christians were already writing. The picture stones of Gotland, in which some scholars see Odin's eagle flight, are a possible hint of older roots, but that reading is contested.

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Mead of Poetry

### What is the mead of poetry?

In Norse mythology it is a honey wine brewed with Kvasir's blood that turns whoever drinks it into a poet or sage (Old Norse *skáldskaparmjöðr*).

### Who was Kvasir?

A being the Æsir and Vanir made from their spit. He was so wise he could answer any question, until Fjalar and Galar killed him.

### How did Odin get the mead?

He slipped into the mountain Hnitbjörg, slept three nights with Gunnlöð, drained all three vessels and fled to Asgard as an eagle. Snorri tells this in detail, the Hávamál only in hints.

### What is the poetaster's share?

During the flight some of the mead was lost. Anyone may drink it, and in Snorri's telling it stands for bad or mediocre poetry.

### Who is Bragi?

Bragi is the god of poetry and husband of Idunn. Whether he goes back to a 9th-century skald is unclear.
