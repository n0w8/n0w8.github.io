The **Havamal**, "Sayings of the High One", run to 164 stanzas in the Codex Regius, the Icelandic manuscript of the Poetic Edda written down around 1270, and most of them are about manners rather than battle. Odin does the talking, and he spends much of the poem advising people on how to behave when they walk into a stranger's house. If you search for Havamal sayings you will first meet the famous line about cattle dying and kinsmen dying, yet the poem's bigger surprise is how practical it is.

This article places the poem in context, walks through its rules for guests and hosts, sets the best-known stanzas in a table and separates what the text actually says from what people like to attribute to it.

## What the Havamal actually are

The Havamal are not a single poem composed in one go. Scholars see a **compilation**: several pieces that a scribe set one after another. Roughly, there is a long section of behavioural advice for guests and friends, often called the Gestathattr ("guest section"), then the Loddfafnismal, in which Odin instructs a listener named Loddfafnir, then the Runatal, the tale of Odin's self-sacrifice on a tree, and finally the Ljodatal, a list of charms. The exact stanza breaks differ between editions, so numbering in books can be off by one or two.

When these parts were composed is uncertain. All we know is that the manuscript dates to the 13th century, roughly two hundred years after the end of the Viking Age, and was written by a Christian Icelander. The verses themselves may be older. One hint: a famous stanza turns up in nearly identical wording in a poem attributed to the skald Eyvindr skaldaspillir about the death of King Hakon of Norway (Hakonarmal, around 960). Who borrowed from whom is open, but both texts show the thought was in circulation in the 10th century.

> The Havamal are the closest thing we have to a Norse etiquette book, which makes them a source for daily life as much as for myth. - editorial view

## Being a guest in a longhouse: the opening stanzas

Stanza 1 begins with caution: check every doorway before you step through, because you never know where enemies are sitting. Stanza 2 then gives the scene everyone remembers: the guest has come in, where should he sit? The host is greeted with "Hail to the givers". Stanza 3 names what a traveller needs first: fire for cold knees, food and dry clothing for someone who has crossed the fells.

This is more than politeness. In a thinly settled land with long winters, a traveller without shelter could die. Hospitality was therefore a mutual insurance scheme: take someone in today, and tomorrow you may be the one at the door. What such a house looked like, with people and animals under one roof, is covered in our piece on [daily life in the Viking Age](/en/blog/viking-daily-life/).

The expectations placed on the guest are just as telling. The clever guest, says stanza 7, is quiet, listens with his ears and looks with his eyes. The man who talks too much reveals his foolishness. The advice is aimed at someone sitting in an unfamiliar house for the first time: observe first, speak later. Drink is addressed too: too much ale steals a man's wits, and a drunk guest can talk himself into serious trouble.

## Friendship and gifts

A second block of stanzas deals with friendship. Stanza 41 advises friends to delight each other with weapons and clothing, and stanza 42 sums up the logic in one line: be a friend to your friend and repay gift with gift. This is not sentiment but an exchange system. A gift creates an obligation, and failing to return it costs you standing. The same principle explains why kings paid followers in rings, and you can follow it along the [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/) as well.

Other stanzas are just as sober. Visit your friend often, or brush will grow over the path. Do not lie to a friend. And stanza 38 says you should never move a single foot's length from your weapons while out in the field, because you never know when you will need the spear. Friendship and distrust sit side by side in the same poem, which is more honest than it first sounds.

## Stanzas 76 and 77: cattle die

The most famous passage reads, in essence: cattle die, kinsmen die, you yourself will die as well. One thing never dies, the verdict on a dead man who has acted well. The Old Norse word is **ordstirr**, "word-fame". It does not mean honour in the modern sense but what people say about you once you are gone. In a society without archives or written contracts, that was real capital. A man with a bad name found no followers, no bride for his son and no witness at the [Thing](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly/).

The poem does not slide into pathos here. It states that everything material fades and only memory remains. Whether a Viking read that as comfort or as a task, the source does not say.

## Odin on the tree: the Runatal

Halfway through, the voice changes. Around stanza 138 Odin tells in the first person how he hung nine nights on a windswept tree, wounded by a spear, "given to Odin, myself to myself". He received neither bread nor horn, looked down and took up the runes. Then he grew fertile and gained in wisdom. The text does not name the tree here, though scholars usually read it as Yggdrasil. More on that tree in our article on [Yggdrasil](/en/blog/yggdrasil-world-tree/).

The idea behind it fits the rest of the poem: knowledge and power are never free, and even a god must pay. He trades suffering for insight, just as a guest trades silence for shelter. How much Odin valued wisdom also shows in the story of [Mimir's Well](/en/blog/mimirs-well-odin-wisdom/), where he gave up an eye.

## Myth vs. fact: what gets attributed to the Havamal

**Myth 1: "The Havamal are the Vikings' law code."** No. They hold advice, not enforceable rules. Legal matters were settled at the Thing.

**Myth 2: "These are genuine Viking quotes from the 9th century."** That cannot be shown. The manuscript is about 400 years younger than the start of the Viking Age. The core may be old, but nobody can say how much was added later.

**Myth 3: "The rules appear as a numbered list."** Also wrong. The "Odin's 10 commandments" lists online are modern selections. The original shifts topic and tone several times.

**Myth 4: "Odin is issuing moral commands."** He gives shrewd, often cynical advice. The tip never to put down your weapons has little to do with morality.

## Editorial view

If you read the Havamal as heroic poetry you will be disappointed. If you read them as a handbook for a hard life, there is surprising observational skill. I find the guest rules the most interesting layer: they show a society that meets strangers with built-in caution and built-in generosity at once. The stanzas on reputation are the most inflated by modern use, mostly because they look so good on a poster.

## FAQ

### What does Havamal mean?
It means "Sayings of the High One". The High One is Odin, who appears under that name several times in the Poetic Edda.

### How many stanzas does the Havamal have?
The Codex Regius has 164 stanzas, counted slightly differently depending on the edition. Further verses survive in other manuscripts.

### Who wrote the Havamal?
Unknown. The text is anonymous and probably combines parts of different ages. The manuscript was written in Iceland in the 13th century.

### What is the most famous Havamal saying?
Usually "Deyr fe, deyja fraendr", the stanza on cattle and kinsmen dying while reputation lasts. A similar version appears in Hakonarmal.

### Are the Havamal Viking laws?
No. They are a poem of advice, not a legal text. Law was spoken at the Thing.

## Conclusion

The Havamal offer a look into a society where a handshake, a warm seat by the fire and a good name counted for more than property. Odin appears as a seasoned wanderer who preaches caution, reciprocity and lasting reputation. As a historical source they need to be read at arm's length, but as a mirror of an attitude they are well worth the time.
