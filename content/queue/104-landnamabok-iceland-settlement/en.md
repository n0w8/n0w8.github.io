The **Landnamabok**, the "Book of Settlements", names several hundred Icelandic settlers together with their origin and farm, yet its oldest, lost version was probably written only around 1100 to 1130, some 250 years after the events, and the copies we have are younger still. So how much of this account of the settlement of Iceland is memory, how much family pride, and how much later invention?

The answer is more surprising than you might expect. Scientific measurements have confirmed some of the book's claims to within a few years, other parts cannot be tested, and a few read more like saga than chronicle. This article sorts the book into "confirmed", "plausible" and "unproven".

## What the Landnamabok actually is

The Landnamabok is not a narrative in the style of a saga but a geographically ordered register. It starts in the south-west and circles the island clockwise. For each settler it gives a name, an origin, the land claimed, often the descendants and now and then a small anecdote. According to commonly cited figures, the work mentions several thousand people and around 1,500 places, of which a few hundred are actual land-takers.

It survives in several versions:

- **Sturlubok**, edited by Sturla Thordarson (died 1284), the fullest version
- **Hauksbok**, edited by Haukr Erlendsson (died around 1334), shorter but with its own additions
- **Melabok**, preserved only as a fragment
- **Thordarbok**, a 17th-century copy based on an older version

There is also a forerunner that is often confused with it: the **Islendingabok** by the priest Ari Thorgilsson "the Learned", written around 1122 to 1133. It is short and sober and gives the settlement only a brief frame. The Landnamabok fills that frame with people and places. How important law was to the island is shown by the [Viking Thing assembly](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly/) that met at Thingvellir around 930, when the settlement was roughly complete by Ari's count.

## Before Ingolfr: Naddoddr, Gardarr and Raven-Floki

According to the Landnamabok, Iceland was not discovered by Ingolfr but by a series of accidental and deliberate visitors. The Norwegian **Naddoddr** is said to have drifted off course on a trip to the Faroes and called the land "Snowland" (Snaeland). The Swede **Gardarr Svavarsson** sailed around the island and wintered there, and the land was briefly known as "Gardarsholmi", Gardarr's Isle.

Best known is the story of **Hrafna-Floki Vilgerdarson**. Tradition says he took three ravens with him to find the way. The first flew back, the second circled and returned, the third flew straight ahead, and Floki followed. It is a nice tale, but it also fits a common narrative pattern in navigation stories. How Vikings really found their way at sea is covered in our piece on [Viking navigation](/en/blog/viking-navigation-sunstone/).

Floki wintered in the north-west and his livestock starved because he had made no hay in summer. When he saw a fjord full of drift ice in spring, he named the land **Island**, Iceland. If the saga is to be believed, the name is not marketing but a name born of frustration.

## 874: Ingolfr and the high-seat pillars

The real founding moment is the story of **Ingolfr Arnarson**. He fled Norway with his foster-brother **Hjorleifr** to escape a blood feud. When Ingolfr neared the coast, he threw his **high-seat pillars** (ondvegissulur) overboard and swore to settle wherever they came ashore. Three years later his men found them in a bay in the south-west. Ingolfr named the place **Reykjavik**, "Smoky Bay", after the steam of the hot springs.

The year 874 comes from medieval scholarly chronology, not from a document. Ari Thorgilsson converted the settlement into a particular regnal year of Norway, and later authors adopted the figure. That is a calculation, not a measured date.

Hjorleifr fares badly in the story: his Irish slaves killed him and fled to the islands off the south coast, which are therefore called **Vestmannaeyjar**, "Islands of the Westmen", since "Westmen" was the Old Norse word for the Irish. Ingolfr avenged his foster-brother and killed them. The tale explains a place name, and it also hints at the make-up of the settlers, as the DNA section below shows.

## Myth or fact? What can be tested

| Claim in the Landnamabok | What research says |
|---|---|
| Settlement begins around 870 | **Confirmed:** An ash layer of about 877 lies in ice cores and soils, with no settlement traces beneath it and plenty above |
| Ingolfr settled in Reykjavik | **Plausible:** Excavations on Adalstraeti found a longhouse hall estimated at about 871 (give or take 2 years) |
| The Vikings found Irish monks (Papar) there | **Unproven:** Sources mention them, archaeology has not yet secured this |
| High-seat pillars showed the way | **Saga:** Such a ritual is attested, the individual case is untestable |
| The settlement took about 60 years | **Plausible:** Fits the Althing date of around 930 |
| About 400 named settlers | **Partly confirmed:** Farm names and family lines can often be matched with later sources |

The key piece of evidence is the **Landnam tephra**, a layer of volcanic ash dated to about 877 that turns up in Greenland ice cores, in Icelandic soils and in excavations. Below this layer there are no traces of human settlement, above it there are. The dating matches the tradition remarkably well: settlement began in the 870s. Anyone who wants to dismiss the book as pure invention has to explain this agreement.

## Who came, and from where? The question of the women

The Landnamabok says the settlers came mainly from western Norway, but many also came by way of the British Isles, Ireland, Scotland and the Hebrides. A famous example is **Audr djupudga**, "the Deep-Minded", who in the story sailed to Iceland with her household as the widow of a king of Dublin and distributed large estates in the west. Whether every detail is right cannot be said, but the route via the British Isles is entirely plausible.

Genetic studies from around 2000 support this picture. They found that the male lines of present-day Icelanders are predominantly Scandinavian, while the female lines contain a high proportion of Celtic, meaning Scottish and Irish, origin. By commonly cited figures roughly 80 percent of male lines are of Norse origin, against roughly 60 percent of female lines of Celtic origin. That fits the idea that Vikings brought women and slaves from the west on their voyages, and it thoroughly undermines the notion of a "purely Nordic" Iceland.

## A land without a king: how settlement led to law

Unlike Norway, Iceland had no king. According to the Landnamabok, land was claimed with fire, and for women the book gives a rule of thumb: as much land as they could walk around in one day leading a two-year-old heifer. Claims like these quickly led to disputes, and the disputes produced the institution Iceland is famous for: around 930 the chieftains met at the **Althing**, a shared court and law assembly. How a case was brought and what fines and outlawry meant is explained in our piece on [Viking law](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly-law/).

The settlement is therefore more than a migration story. It explains why Iceland became a society of farm owners who justified their rights through genealogy. Whoever knew whose descendant he was could press claims. That is why the Landnamabok is also a legal document: being able to cite a land-taker as an ancestor meant standing and land. It also explains why families had an interest in presenting their ancestors in grand terms.

## Settlements in the ground: what archaeology shows

Besides the tephra, excavations provide cross-checks. In **Reykjavik** the Adalstraeti dig shows a longhouse dated to the late 9th century, now displayed in a museum. At **Stong** in Thjorsardalur, a farm traditionally linked to a Hekla eruption bears witness to the everyday life of later generations. Finds such as beads, combs and iron tools match finds in Norway and the British Isles.

So the Landnamabok hits the mark on much: on **time** (from the 870s), on **origin** (western Norway and the British Isles) and on **structure** (farms, families, place names). Where it fails is **literal accuracy**: names, dates and duels are often part of later family storytelling.

## Our assessment

The book is neither a chronicle in the modern sense nor a fairy tale. It is the memory store of a society that organised its origins through places and family trees. The framework, meaning time, routes and places, holds up under every test we have so far. The details, such as who killed whom and when, are best read as tradition. What is especially interesting is that the book records failures too: Floki's starved livestock, Hjorleifr's end. A pure hero story would have been smoother.

## Frequently asked questions

### When was Iceland settled?

According to the Landnamabok and the Islendingabok, from about 870 to 874. An ash layer of about 877 supports this span, and settlement traces appear only above it.

### Who was the first settler of Iceland?

Tradition names Ingolfr Arnarson as the first permanent settler. Before him Naddoddr, Gardarr and Floki visited the island, and Irish monks are said to have been there.

### Where does Iceland get its name?

According to the Landnamabok, from Hrafna-Floki, who saw a fjord full of drift ice in spring and called the land "Island".

### Is the Landnamabok a reliable source?

In outline yes, in detail only partly. Time, origin and structure are supported by tephra, excavations and genetics, while individual stories are tradition.

## Conclusion

The Landnamabok shows how an oral tradition can survive for centuries and still match archaeology to a large extent. It is not an exact chronicle, but it is a serious guide to early Iceland, and its gaps and contradictions are part of what makes it credible.
