The **siege of Paris** began in November 885 and lasted almost a year: a Viking army blocked the Seine, yet the city on the Île de la Cité did not fall, and its defender Count Odo later became king of the West Franks. It was the first big fight over a fortified city in the Viking Age that did not give the attackers the victory they wanted. This article explains how it unfolded, why Paris held, and what rests on a single source.

## Siege of Paris 885: The Source Is a Poem

Almost everything we know comes from **Abbo of Saint-Germain-des-Prés**, a monk who was an eyewitness. He wrote a Latin poem in three books, the *Bella Parisiacae urbis*. The Frankish annals, such as the Annals of St-Vaast, tell the story more briefly.

Abbo has an agenda. He praises the defenders, above all Count Odo and Bishop Gozlin, and paints the attackers as pagans. His numbers need caution: he speaks of 700 ships and 30,000 men, a figure historians regard as greatly inflated. A fleet that size is hard to imagine for the period, even if the army was certainly large. If you want a number, the honest answer is: unknown, but big.

## The Setting: A City on an Island

In the ninth century Paris was essentially the Île de la Cité. Two bridges linked it to the banks, and those bridges were the problem: anyone heading upriver, say towards Burgundy, had to pass Paris, and the bridge blocked ships. The Vikings knew the city already. They had reached it in 845 and left with silver, as described in our article on the [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/).

When the army appeared in November 885, its leaders, named by Abbo as **Sigfred** and **Sinric**, made a demand: free passage in return for sparing the city. Bishop Gozlin and Count Odo refused. That decision had consequences, because it turned Paris into an obstacle.

From the attackers' side the demand was reasonable. An army heading upriver wants neither to lose time on a siege nor to lose men. They wanted to get past, not get in. Paris, on the other hand, had every reason to hold firm: letting one army through unopposed tells everyone that the city no longer protects anything, and opens the road to the hinterland where monasteries, farms and markets lay. Both sides pursued rational aims, which is exactly why it came to a fight.

## How It Unfolded: Tower, Flood and Hunger

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| 25 November 885 | Army reaches Paris, demand for passage refused |
| Late November 885 | First attacks on a tower on the north bank |
| February 886 | Flood destroys the small bridge, a garrison is cut off |
| 16 April 886 | Bishop Gozlin dies of illness (per Abbo) |
| Summer 886 | Disease and hunger on both sides |
| Autumn 886 | Emperor Charles the Fat arrives, pays tribute, army moves on to Burgundy |

The first assaults targeted the **tower** on the north bank, built by the defenders to guard the bridge. The Vikings used siege equipment, according to Abbo catapults, rams and fire ships, and tried to burn the bridge. The defenders replied with arrows, stones and hot oil, as the poet tells it.

Then nature intervened: in February 886 a flood swept away the small bridge. A tower and its garrison, twelve men according to Abbo, were cut off from the rest and overrun. The loss was heavy but not decisive.

## How Do You Block the Seine?

The core of the siege was less the assault on the walls than control of the river. According to Abbo, during the siege the Vikings hauled their ships overland past the city and so could continue upriver. At the same time they plundered the surroundings, a method we also see with the [Great Heathen Army](/en/blog/great-heathen-army/). Paris itself was cut off and supplies ran short.

The key point is that the bridge was both cause and target. As long as it stood and was held, the river stayed closed to large forces, and the fleet could not advance quickly. That explains why a fight for one tower was so bitter. Anyone who studies [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/) will see why rivers mattered so much: they were the highways of the age.

## Odo, the Hero of Paris

Count **Odo** (Eudes), son of Robert the Strong, became the central figure. During the siege he rode out of the city to seek help and, says Abbo, fought his way back through the enemy lines. A relief army under Henry of Saxony reached Paris in winter but withdrew without breaking the siege.

Odo profited from the fame. When Emperor Charles the Fat was deposed in 887, the West Frankish magnates chose **Odo as king** in 888. He came from the family of the later Capetians, not the Carolingians. So the siege was also a political turning point.

## Who Fought for the City?

The defenders were a mixed group. Besides Odo's retinue there were townspeople and monks of the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés. Abbo mentions Gozlin's nephew, **Abbot Ebolus**, who is said to have taken up the bow himself, a detail that stirs the poet to mockery and admiration at once. Clergy in battle were nothing unusual in the ninth century, although the Church officially disapproved.

Faith played its part too. The Parisians, says Abbo, carried relics onto the walls and credited their survival to the saints, especially St Germanus. For the poet it was a miracle; for us it shows how closely defence and piety belonged together then. To the Vikings, churches and monasteries were simply rich targets, as already at the [Lindisfarne raid](/en/blog/lindisfarne-raid/).

## What Remained of the Siege?

Paris lost much of its surroundings, but the city stayed. In the decades after, bridges and fortifications in West Francia were built more systematically, because people had seen that a fortified river crossing could hold an army for a long time. The enemy's strength was not broken, but it had met a limit. In 911 Rollo was granted the land of later Normandy, another way of dealing with the same problem: whoever cannot be beaten is bound in.

## The End: Silver Instead of Victory

In autumn 886 Emperor Charles arrived with an army. Instead of attacking he negotiated. The annals report that he paid the Vikings 700 pounds of silver and allowed them to sail further up the Seine and plunder Burgundy. Paris was saved, but not by victory. The emperor lost much standing through this deal, and chroniclers such as those of St-Vaast criticised him sharply. A year later he was deposed.

## Our Take: Why Did Paris Hold?

We see three reasons. First, the position: an island city with bridges is hard to take without ships and siege craft, and the Vikings were seamen, not siege specialists. Second, leadership, since Odo and Gozlin stayed firm. Third, time: an army that sits before a city for a year pays with hunger and disease itself. That the attackers still got silver shows how little the withdrawal was a Western victory. ## FAQ

### When was the siege of Paris by the Vikings?
From November 885 to autumn 886, almost a year. An earlier siege took place in 845.

### Who defended Paris in 885?
Count Odo and Bishop Gozlin, who died in April 886. Odo later became king.

### How big was the Viking army at Paris?
Unknown. Abbo gives 700 ships and 30,000 men, but historians consider that exaggerated.

### Did the Vikings capture Paris?
No. They failed to take the city but received 700 pounds of silver from Emperor Charles and moved on to Burgundy.

### Was Rollo at the siege of Paris?
Rollo is only linked to Normandy in later tradition. Whether he was at Paris is uncertain and rests on later sources.

## Conclusion

Paris 885 was neither a Viking victory nor a clear Frankish one. The city held, the emperor paid, and a count became king. As the sources tell it, this is the story of a bridge that held up an entire army.
