Five days before Stamford Bridge, on 20 September 1066, England lost a battle it could not afford to lose, and hardly anyone remembers it. The **Battle of Fulford**, fought a few kilometres south of York, was a clear victory for Harald Hardrada and Tostig Godwinson. Without it, King Harold's famous forced march north would probably never have happened. This article puts the forgotten opening act of 1066 in the spotlight: who fought, what the sources say, and where they disagree.

## Why Fulford Lives in the Shadow of Stamford Bridge

Say 1066 and most people think of Hastings. Dig a little deeper and you reach Stamford Bridge. Fulford is usually a footnote, for a simple reason: the English **lost**. The winners did not get to write the history either, because five days later they were destroyed themselves. The defeated brothers Edwin and Morcar survive in the English tradition mainly as supporting players.

Yet Fulford shaped the whole autumn. Without the victory on 20 September, York would not have fallen into Norwegian hands, and without York in enemy hands, Harold Godwinson would have had no reason to rush roughly 300 kilometres north. For the follow-up fight, see our piece on the [Battle of Stamford Bridge](/en/blog/battle-of-stamford-bridge/), where Fulford gets only one section.

## The Setup: A Fleet on the Ouse

The key sources are the **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle** (in several manuscripts) and, from the Norwegian side and much later, **Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla**. They agree on the outline: Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, arrived in England in September 1066 with a large fleet. The Chronicle speaks of about 300 ships, a figure to be handled with the usual medieval caution. Tostig, the brother of the English king who had been driven out of Northumbria in 1065, joined him.

The fleet sailed into the Humber and up the Ouse and made camp at **Riccall**, about 15 kilometres south of York. From there the northern capital was within a day's march. Defending the north were two young men:

- **Edwin**, Earl of Mercia
- **Morcar**, Earl of Northumbria, who had replaced the exiled Tostig in 1065

The brothers faced a problem that no chronicle states outright but the situation makes plain: King Harold and the main royal army were in the south, watching for William of Normandy. The north had to cope on its own.

## 20 September: How the Battle Unfolded

The Chronicle dates the battle to the Wednesday before the feast of St Matthew, that is 20 September. Edwin and Morcar marched out of York and drew up their forces at **Fulford**, about three kilometres south of the city, on the east bank of the Ouse. The Heimskringla describes the ground in unusually vivid terms: a wide, deep ditch full of water protected the English front, and their line rested on the river.

According to the saga, the fighting came in two phases. First the English attacked on one flank and pushed the Norwegians back. Hardrada then had his banner carried forward, the famous **Landeyðan** ("Land-waster"), and led the counterattack himself. The English line broke, and many were driven into the ditch. The saga claims the Norwegians finally crossed the ditch **dry-shod** over the bodies. That is a literary image and need not be taken literally, but it shows how heavy the English losses lived on in memory.

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle is more sober: a great slaughter, many English killed or drowned, and the Norwegians held the field. Both earls escaped, which matters for what came next.

> "The Norwegians had possession of the place of slaughter." - paraphrasing the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle for the year 1066

Barely four weeks separate the first and last dates in this table. Fulford is the first link in a chain that changed England for centuries.

## Myth vs Fact: How Big Were the Armies?

This is where certainty ends. The sources almost never give reliable numbers, and scholars disagree.

- **Myth:** "300 ships means 30,000 men." That is arithmetic, not evidence. The Chronicle gives 300 ships, but crews varied widely and no exact figure survives.
- **Fact:** Both sides fielded several thousand men. Nothing more precise can be said responsibly.
- **Myth:** "Edwin and Morcar were incompetent youngsters." That picture comes from hindsight. They defended their earldoms against an enemy who arrived by water, close to York, with no time to call up reinforcements from the south.
- **Fact:** The Heimskringla is a 13th-century Norwegian source that flatters Hardrada. The Chronicle is closer in time but terse. Reading the two together gives a usable but incomplete picture.

## How Did They Fight in 1066? Equipment and Tactics

Both armies fought in the same basic pattern: a **shield wall**, warriors packed shoulder to shoulder in several ranks. Our article on the [Viking shield wall](/en/blog/viking-shield-wall-battle-tactic/) explains how such a formation worked. On the Norwegian side many fighters carried spear, sword and axe. On the English side the housecarls, professional retainers of earls and king, formed the hard core. Mail shirts were for the wealthier men only. Most warriors had a helmet and a shield, not much else.

Decisive was less the weaponry than the **terrain**. A ditch, a river and soft ground turned an orderly line into a crush within minutes. A man who lost his footing there had little chance, which explains why the sources talk so much about the drowned and the dead in the ditch. An army with its back to the water and a flank rolled up has no way to retreat. Whether Edwin and Morcar used the ground cleverly or were manoeuvred into a trap is a question the sources do not settle.

## After the Battle: York, Hostages and a Mistake

After the victory York submitted. Several sources say Hardrada demanded hostages from the city and that a further hostage exchange and negotiation was arranged for **25 September** at Stamford Bridge, east of York. The date matters: Hardrada evidently expected no serious attack any more, and he left part of his men with the ships at Riccall.

That misjudgement cost him his life. Harold Godwinson, who had heard of the landing, was already on the road. The forced march north and the surprise attack of 25 September are unthinkable without Fulford, because only the earls' defeat turned a regional threat into a national one. More on the man who fell at Stamford Bridge in our profile of [Harald Hardrada](/en/blog/harald-hardrada-last-viking-king/).

Edwin and Morcar survived Fulford. After Hastings they submitted to William the Conqueror and later rose against him. Their story did not end happily, but it shows that Fulford was not the end of their political role.

## The Battlefield Today

Visit Fulford now and you find a suburb of York. The exact location of the fighting is not settled among researchers, and parts of the ground are built over or under development pressure. Local groups have campaigned for years to protect the battlefield and keep the memory alive. As far as we know, finds that can be tied clearly to the battle are scarce, which makes the debate about the precise spot even harder.

## Our Take: Why Fulford Deserves More Attention

We see Fulford as the real **turning point of autumn 1066**, even if Stamford Bridge and Hastings take the headlines. Three reasons:

1. Fulford was the moment when Hardrada's invasion enjoyed its clearest success, and everything after went downhill.
2. The victory made Hardrada careless and drew Harold north, which left him more exposed to William in the south.
3. The defeat showed how fragile English defence was without the king.

That is our reading, not a scholarly consensus. What is certain: anyone who wants to understand 1066 should start with Fulford, not Hastings. For how earlier Norse armies had already shaken the country, see our article on the [Great Heathen Army](/en/blog/great-heathen-army/).
