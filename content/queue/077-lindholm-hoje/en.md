**Lindholm Høje** (often written Lindholm Hoje) is an Iron Age and Viking Age burial ground near Aalborg in northern Denmark with nearly 700 graves, dating from roughly AD 400 to 1000, many of them marked out in stone as triangles, circles, ovals and ships. Towards the end of the Viking Age the whole hill on the north shore of the Limfjord disappeared under windblown sand, up to four metres deep in places. Only the big excavation of 1952 to 1958 brought the stones back to light.

Walk the slope today and at first all you see is stones, hundreds of them, scattered across the grass in rows and rings. Give it a few minutes and the patterns show: a triangle here, a circle there, and in between, long pointed outlines like ships run aground in the turf. Which is exactly what they are meant to be.

## Where is Lindholm Høje?

Lindholm Høje sits in Nørresundby, on the north side of the Limfjord, directly across the water from Aalborg. The hill is a natural glacial moraine, and the location made sense: the fjord narrows here, and the spot was a crossing point between Vendsyssel to the north and the rest of Jutland. According to the Danish heritage agency, ships of the period could sail up the little Lindholm stream right to the foot of the hill.

What you see today is an open, gently sloping hillside covered in stone settings, plus cement blocks in the grass marking the outlines of Viking Age houses. It isn't complete, though: in the 19th century many stones were broken up for road building, and according to the Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde there's no way of knowing how many graves were lost.

## How old are the graves at Lindholm Høje?

People were buried at Lindholm Høje for close to 600 years. The oldest graves date from the late Iron Age in the 5th century, the youngest from the Viking Age in the 10th. You can read that on the ground: the old graves sit on top of the hill, often under small earth mounds, while the later ones lie lower down at the edge of the site, mostly framed by stones.

The shapes changed too. The 1959 excavation report lists triangular, rectangular, round, oval and ship-shaped settings, with the **triangles the oldest** and the **ships the latest**. Some triangles hide a curious detail: fist-sized white flint cobbles set between the corner stones, carried in from a chalk cliff elsewhere.

The Danish heritage agency's information boards on site also link the shapes to gender. Men were mostly buried under triangular and ship-shaped settings, while women's graves were usually marked by round and oval ones. And the same boards make a refreshingly honest admission: almost no two stone settings are alike, and nobody knows why. Perhaps the family decided. We like that candour.

## What is a stone ship setting?

A **stone ship setting** is a grave enclosure of upright stones tracing the outline of a ship, usually with larger stones at bow and stern. There's no actual boat underneath: the ship is a symbol, not a vessel. For how these stone ships relate to real ship graves like Oseberg and Gokstad, see our overview of [Viking ship burials](/en/blog/viking-burial-ship/).

At Lindholm Høje, the ship settings belong to the latest phase, the Viking Age proper. Most are fairly modest: according to the 1959 report, none was longer than eight metres, with a single exception of 23 metres. The Norwegian encyclopedia Store norske leksikon calls Lindholm Høje the burial ground in Scandinavia with the most preserved ship settings. None of the specialist sources we checked gives a reliable total, though, so we've left out the neat round numbers floating around online.

## How were the dead buried at Lindholm Høje?

Most of the dead were cremated in their everyday clothes and jewellery, and afterwards earth was heaped over what was left. According to the excavation report, the remains were usually spread over a patch about a metre across in the middle of the stone setting, along with whatever had survived the fire: bronze ornaments, glass beads, iron knives, whetstones, spindle whorls, gaming pieces, and the bones of dogs, sheep, horses and cattle.

Dogs clearly mattered: **223 graves** of men and women contained one. Many cremation patches also held a pottery vessel, sometimes with food for the final journey, and one oval setting even produced a bone knife handle carved with runes.

There are also inhumation graves, at both the start and the end of the cemetery's life. One early example is a 7th-century warrior's grave with a round earth mound, a ring of large stones and a surface of flint blocks. The man was buried with a single-edged sword, a long seax, and he too was given a dog.

At the very end come some 30 inhumation graves from the 10th century, high up in the sand layer. Burying the dead unburnt again points to Christian influence, the era in which Harald Bluetooth boasted on the [Jelling Stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/) that he had made the Danes Christian. Yet every one of these graves still had grave goods. One held two glass beads and a small **Thor's hammer of amber**, an unmistakably pagan amulet (more in our piece on [Thor's hammer amulets](/en/blog/thor-hammer-amulet-viking-finds/)). Another contained five Arabic silver coins, four of them cut in half, one struck in Tashkent in AD 920. Faith, it seems, did not change overnight.

## Why is Lindholm Høje so well preserved?

In a word: sand. The soil here is light, easy to farm and prone to drifting. Towards the end of the Viking Age so much of it blew across the ridge that, according to the excavation report, the cemetery was completely covered by around 950 to 1000, in places under as much as four metres of it.

> For the farmers of Lindholm the drifting sand was a disaster. For archaeology it was a stroke of luck: it buried graves, houses and even a ploughed field before time and the plough could wipe them out.

People didn't leave straight away; their village actually spread out over the buried cemetery. But the storms kept coming, and by around 1100, according to the Danish heritage agency, the sand drift was so severe that Lindholm Høje was deserted. Some accounts put the end of the settlement as late as 1200.

## The Viking field under the sand

Perhaps the most unusual find at Lindholm Høje isn't a grave at all. South of the cemetery, beneath a small house site, the excavators reached the original surface of a field, and it looked like a giant washboard: plough furrows heaped into ridges up to 25 centimetres high and 75 to 125 centimetres wide. Running across them were the wheel ruts of the farmer's cart on his last trip over the field.

A clue to when the field went out of use comes from a silver coin of the German emperor Conrad II, struck between 1036 and 1039, found in the house later built on top of it. The Danish heritage agency dates the sanding-over to the 11th century and tells it like this: a storm buried the field, the road to the north was blocked, and the farmer drove straight across the sand to inspect the damage. Rye and barley were probably grown here. According to Oscar Marseen's 1959 report, it was the first time this way of farming had been identified in Scandinavia.

## Who lived at Lindholm Høje?

There were at least two villages on and around the hill, probably more. The older settlement lay north of the cemetery and dates from around 700 to 800. It had five wells, a road partly surfaced with timber, and several longhouses, including two around 17 metres long. There were also about 20 sunken huts, three to five metres long. Some held up to 30 loom weights of unfired clay: these were weaving sheds.

The younger village lay south of the cemetery and spread across it once the sand had buried the graves. People lived there from roughly the late 10th century to the early 12th. The excavators found 15 house sites, including a large four-winged farmhouse, outbuildings and a bathhouse. One of the houses now marked out in cement had curved long walls, two rows of internal posts and slanting buttress posts outside, the same type known from the ring fortresses of Fyrkat and Aggersborg (see our article on the [Trelleborg ring fortresses](/en/blog/viking-ring-fortresses-trelleborg/)). By the time that house stood, in the late 10th century, the cemetery had been abandoned and the villagers buried their dead in a Christian churchyard.

The people of Lindholm Høje had contacts well beyond the Limfjord. Finds include shards of drinking glasses, rock crystal, a gold-foil bead and Arabic silver coins. In and around the houses lay 14 Danish, German and English silver coins, the latest from William the Conqueror in 1087. A coin from the reign of King Harthacnut names Aalborg as "Alabu", the first known mention of the town, and a rubbish pit held a silver ornament in the Urnes style from around 1050.

## Who excavated Lindholm Høje?

A few individual stone settings were uncovered as early as 1889 and 1896. The true size of the cemetery emerged by accident: during the Second World War, German occupation troops dug trenches across the site, and stones and cremation graves showed up in the trench walls. By 1949 it was clear that a very large cemetery lay beneath the sand.

The main excavation ran from 1952 to 1958, carried out by the Aalborg Historical Museum with the National Museum in Copenhagen and paid for by the Danish Ministry of Labour and the town of Nørresundby. **Thorkild Ramskou** supervised it for the National Museum throughout and published a book on the site in 1960. **Oscar Marseen** of the Aalborg Museum wrote a summary report for the Danish journal Kuml in 1959, the source of many figures in this article.

## Visiting Lindholm Høje: the Viking Museum

Right next to the burial ground stands the **Vikingemuseet Lindholm Høje** (Lindholm Høje Viking Museum). It opened in 1992 in a new building funded by the cement maker Aalborg Portland and was extended in 2008. Today it is run by Nordjyske Museer. One exhibition covers Viking life at Lindholm Høje, with jewellery from the graves, and a second traces the prehistory of the eastern Limfjord region from the Stone Age to just before the Viking Age.

Our tip: do the museum first, then the field. Once you know that triangles are early and ships are late, the hillside reads very differently.

## FAQ

### How many graves are at Lindholm Høje?

The museum puts the figure at nearly 700, most of them cremation graves, while other counts give 682. There were probably more originally, since many stones were removed in the 19th century.

### How many stone ships are there at Lindholm Høje?

We couldn't find a reliable total in the sources we checked. What is certain is that they date from the Viking Age phase and that no burial ground in Scandinavia is thought to have more surviving ship settings.

### What does Lindholm Høje mean?

*Høje* is the Danish plural of *høj*, "hill" or "mound", related to Old Norse *haugr*, so the name means roughly "the Lindholm hills". Since most keyboards have no "ø", you'll often see it spelled Lindholm Hoje.

### Why was Lindholm Høje buried in sand?

Storms kept blowing drift sand across the light farmland. By the end of the Viking Age the cemetery was completely covered, and that same sand protected the stone settings until the 1950s.

### Can you visit Lindholm Høje?

Yes. The burial ground is in Nørresundby, right next to the Vikingemuseet Lindholm Høje, which is run by Nordjyske Museer.

## Our take

Lindholm Høje isn't a place for one spectacular find. There's no lavish ship burial like Oseberg here and no royal inscription like Jelling. What it offers is rarer: six centuries of an ordinary community in one place, right down to the field someone ploughed before the sand rolled in.

If you want to understand how Vikings actually lived and died, this quiet hillside above the Limfjord will teach you more than many a treasure room.
