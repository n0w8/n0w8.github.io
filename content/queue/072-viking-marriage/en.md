Romantic love played, at best, a supporting role in a Viking Age wedding. A marriage was primarily a **contract between two families**, negotiated much like a land sale, with a price, a counter-payment, and clearly fixed terms for what would happen if things went wrong. That sounds like the opposite of romance, but it also produced something rare across much of medieval Europe: comparatively strong legal rights for the woman entering the marriage.

## The Bride Price: Mundr as a Negotiated Payment

The Icelandic law code **Gragas** and Norwegian legal texts such as the **Gulathing Law** describe in fairly precise detail how a marriage had to proceed to be legally valid at all. Central to this was the **mundr**, a payment made by the groom's family to the bride's family. Unlike in some other cultures, this was not simply a purchase price for the woman, it was part of a reciprocal arrangement: the bride's family in turn brought a **dowry**, Old Norse heimanfylgja, into the marriage.

What mattered most was who actually owned these assets during the marriage. The dowry remained the woman's legal property; the husband merely managed it on her behalf. In the event of divorce, it had to be returned to her in full, often along with a share of wealth acquired jointly during the marriage. This arrangement gave women an economic safety net that was by no means standard in comparable societies of the time, as historian Jenny Jochens documents extensively in her work on women's status in the old Norse world. If the husband died, the dowry stayed with the widow regardless, along with a legally fixed share of jointly acquired wealth, so a widowed woman rarely ended up entirely without means, even if her late husband's family pressed claims on the rest of the estate.

## The Betrothal: Festarmal as a Binding First Step

Before the actual wedding came the **betrothal**, festarmal, a formal act witnessed publicly, at which the terms of the future marriage, including the mundr and the dowry, were fixed. Breaking this betrothal without good cause was no trivial matter, it carried real legal consequences, usually in the form of compensation payments. The legal system treated betrothal almost as binding as marriage itself, a sign of just how much marriage was understood as a contract between families rather than a private matter of the heart. In practice these betrothals were usually negotiated by fathers or, if they were no longer alive, by brothers or uncles, in front of witnesses who could later confirm, if a dispute arose, exactly what terms had actually been agreed.

## The Wedding Feast: Keys, Oaths, and Celebrations Lasting Days

The wedding feast itself, **brudhlaup**, could stretch across several days and was, as far as the hosting family could afford it, a public statement of wealth: abundant food, brewed mead or ale, and guests arriving from several farms. A recurring element was the symbolic handing over of a **ring of keys** to the bride, marking the moment she took over control of the storerooms, chests, and overall household property of the new home. That set of keys was far more than decoration, it was a genuine symbol of domestic authority, and burial finds confirm that keys regularly appear as grave goods in women's graves. The sagas also describe toasts and formal oaths spoken aloud to publicly witness the marriage, usually with as many guests present as possible, since a marriage without sufficient witnesses was hard to prove later if a dispute arose.

A humorous but revealing side glimpse into wedding customs comes from the eddic poem **Thrymskvida**, in which Thor, disguised as a bride, is to be married off to the giant Thrym in order to get his stolen hammer back. During the ceremony, following custom, Mjolnir is placed in the supposed bride's lap, supposedly to bless the marriage and ensure fertility. The scene is comedy, not documentation of real ritual, but it only works if the audience recognizes an actual practice behind the joke: objects meant to symbolize blessing or protection clearly had an established place in the wedding ceremony. Thor's hammer Mjolnir itself, beyond pure mythology, was closely tied to fertility and protection, as our own article on [the Thor's hammer amulet](/en/blog/thor-hammer-amulet-viking-finds) explores, and that very connection is exactly what makes the Thrymskvida scene genuinely funny to its original audience: a fertility symbol gets repurposed here, of all things, to protect a thunder god disguised as a bride.

## Not Every Union Was a Marriage: The Frilla

Alongside formal marriage, Norse society recognized a second, widespread form of partnership: the **frilla**, a concubine without a wife's legal status but with a socially quite accepted role. Unlike in the Christian parts of Europe at the time, such a relationship was not a hidden offense but an openly lived arrangement, often within the very same household as the wife. Children from these unions counted as illegitimate but were by no means automatically without rights: powerful men could acknowledge their frilla-born sons and even let them inherit, as countless Norwegian royal sagas show, in which almost every significant king had sons by several different women, formally married or not. That practice explains why inheritance disputes in the Viking Age were so frequent and so bloody, the legal line between a legitimate and an illegitimate son existed, but was often politically negotiable.

## Divorce: A Right Both Sides Could Use

Perhaps the most surprising part of Norse marital life is just how accessible **divorce** actually was, at least compared with much of the rest of medieval Europe. Gragas lists several recognized grounds, available to both husbands and wives:

- Persistent inability or refusal to provide for the household
- Serious or repeated violence against one's spouse
- Violation of expected social roles, such as a husband wearing conspicuously feminine clothing
- Years-long absence without word, for instance through a long sea voyage with no return
- Poverty so severe the family could no longer be supported

Both the **Laxdaela saga** and **Njal's saga** contain divorce scenes that show these rights in practice. In Njal's saga, Hallgerd leaves two separate husbands, in one case after being slapped, which she pointedly does not let pass without consequence. Scenes like these are literary exaggeration, not court records, but they reflect a legal reality that treated divorce not as scandal but as a regulated, usable way out. It is also notable that a divorced woman in Norse society was in no way considered damaged goods or unmarriageable. Multiple marriages over a lifetime, one after another, were common for women as well as men and appear in the sagas without any moral undertone, a clear contrast to how remarriage was treated across much of the rest of Christian medieval Europe.

## Marriageable Age and the Right of Consent

Under Gragas, a woman could take part in decisions over her own affairs from the age of sixteen, although in practice betrothals were often negotiated earlier by fathers. The bride's consent was formally required; how consistently that requirement was actually enforced remains disputed among scholars, especially in politically significant unions between powerful families, where a father's strategic interests experience suggests weighed more heavily than his daughter's own preference. At the same time, the sagas do report cases of women openly rejecting a union forced on them or divorcing immediately after the wedding, which at least shows that a forced marriage was by no means automatically permanent. ## What Marriage Law Reveals About Norse Society

Our assessment after comparing the legal texts and the sagas: Norse marriage was sober, contractual, and nothing like romantic in the modern sense, yet it was also not a pure instrument of control over women. The legal protection offered by the dowry and a functioning right to divorce gave women a bargaining position far beyond what was available at the same time across much of Christian southern Europe. That exact mix of hard-headed pragmatism and real protection is what makes Norse marriage one of the most revealing windows into everyday life in the Viking Age.

With the Christianization of Scandinavia, this system changed noticeably. Canon law steadily narrowed the recognized grounds for divorce, required a church wedding in place of the secular contract, and gradually pushed the frilla into a morally condemned gray zone. What had been a comparatively pragmatic system of contract, counter-payment, and enforceable rights was reshaped, over generations, into a sacramental, nearly unbreakable bond. That transition was not the event of a single year but stretched across centuries, which is exactly why it is worth looking back at the pagan period: it shows that the later, seemingly self-evident indissolubility of marriage was never a Norse given, it was a later import.
