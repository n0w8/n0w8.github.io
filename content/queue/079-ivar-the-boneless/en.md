**Ivar the Boneless** never appears by name in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, yet he is routinely credited with leading the army that took York in 867 and reshaped England for a generation. That sounds like a contradiction, and it is the heart of his story: the sagas that make Ivar a hero were written down centuries later, while the chronicles of his own day name other men, or nobody at all. Somewhere in between stands a man who may be the same as the Irish king Ímar of Dublin.

This article separates three things that series and novels like to blend: what the sources say about Ivar, what they only say about his army, and what is pure saga. For the family background, see our piece on [Ragnar Lothbrok, legend and truth](/en/blog/ragnar-lothbrok-legend-truth/).

## Who was Ivar the Boneless?

According to the Icelandic sources, Ivar (Old Norse *Ívarr hinn beinlausi*) was a son of Ragnar Lothbrok and brother of Björn Ironside, Halfdan, Sigurd Snake-in-the-Eye and Hvitserk. The key texts are the *Ragnarssona þáttr* and *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*, both written down in the 13th century, plus Saxo Grammaticus, who wrote in Denmark around 1200. These are late witnesses, and they tell family history in heroic mould: the father dies in a snake pit, the sons set out for revenge.

English sources are thinner. The **Anglo-Saxon Chronicle** reports a great heathen army landing in East Anglia in 865 but names no leader. A Halfdan only turns up in the 870s. Ivar enters the western record as **Hinguar** in Abbo of Fleury's *Passio Sancti Eadmundi*, written around 985, and in the Latin chronicle of Æthelweard. Both texts were composed more than a century after the events.

## What does "the Boneless" mean?

Nobody knows; there are only interpretations. The sagas say Ivar had "no bones" and was carried on a shield, and the *Ragnarssona þáttr* adds that he was nevertheless the wisest of men. The usual explanations:

- **A physical condition:** some modern writers suggest a hereditary disorder such as osteogenesis imperfecta, brittle bone disease. This is a guess. No skeleton exists that could confirm it.
- **An insult:** "boneless" may allude to immobility or to lack of virility, as some philologists read it.
- **A metaphor for suppleness:** others see a snake comparison, a sign of agility and cunning, which would fit the snake pit in his father's story.

Our view: a warrior who supposedly cannot walk but commands an army is a gift to a storyteller. We would not read the nickname as a medical report.

## Was Ivar the Same Man as Ímar of Dublin?

The Irish annals know an **Ímar** (the same name as Ívarr) who ruled Dublin together with Amlaíb (Olaf). In 870 the two besieged Dumbarton Rock, the main fortress of the kingdom of Strathclyde, and in 871 they returned to Dublin with about 200 ships and many captives. In 873 the annals record Ímar's death. The overlap in time with the army in England and a rare name have led many to suggest that Ímar and Ivar were one man.

That is not certain. Supporters point to the chronology; sceptics note that no text links the two names and that Ívarr was a common name. As a working hypothesis the identification is widespread, but it should not be sold as fact. What is firm: Ímar founded the **Uí Ímair** dynasty in Dublin, which shaped Ireland and the Irish Sea for generations.

## The Repton Burial: Is This the Boneless?

The army wintered at Repton in Derbyshire in 873/874, and archaeologists found a burial mound holding the remains of more than 250 people. At its centre lay a high-status warrior with a sword, a Thor's hammer pendant and a boar's tusk. Media like to call it "Ivar's grave". That is speculation: the dating fits roughly, the identity stays open. More on such amulets in [Thor's hammer amulets: finds and meaning](/en/blog/thor-hammer-amulet-viking-finds/).

## Blood Eagle and Snake Pit: What Is Saga?

The most famous scene, the **blood eagle** inflicted on King Ælla, comes from sources of the 12th and 13th centuries. Historians disagree on whether this method of execution was ever practised; many think it is a misreading of skaldic verse. The chronicle says only that Ælla fell in 867. The snake pit in which Ragnar died is equally unattested. We treat it for what it is: a revenge story that explains why the sons attacked England.

## How Did the Army Beat England?

The army's success owed less to the feats of single leaders than to timing and logistics. In 865 it landed in East Anglia and was given horses, which gave it a mobility that no Anglo-Saxon levy had. According to the chronicle, the East Angles made peace with the army, which stayed there over the winter of 865/866. Such deals were a forerunner of the system described in our article on [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/): whoever paid or supplied bought time.

The march on York in 866/867 hit Northumbria at a moment when two men were competing for the throne, and the army used the gap. After the city fell and Ælla and Osberht died, the Northmen installed a puppet king whom later chroniclers call Ecgberht. Whether Ivar held the reins is not stated anywhere. We see a pattern that later became permanent in the Danelaw: conquest first, settlement next, rule last.

Edmund of East Anglia shows how thin the tradition is. He died in 869 or 870, and the detailed story comes from Abbo's saint's life of about 985. There the Danes tie him to a tree, shoot him with arrows and behead him because he refused to submit. That is hagiography, a text that shapes a martyr rather than a report in the modern sense. That Abbo names Hinguar as leader proves only that people in the late 10th century linked that name with the army.

Ireland is worth a look too. The annals are closer in time and terser, often written in monasteries, and they note raids, alliances and deaths without dramatic decoration. For that reason many scholars regard them as the most reliable source for Ímar. If you want to find Ivar, you are more likely to find him in Dublin than in York.

## Our Take: Why the Myth Lasts

A man without bones who conquers a kingdom is a perfect figure for narrators. He lets them combine hardness and cunning, and he turns a campaign into a family revenge. We think it likely that 13th-century saga authors fused existing names from England and Ireland into one dynasty, because that suited their idea of royal lines. That does not make the sagas worthless, but they mirror their own time.

In practice: whoever writes or reads about Ivar should always say which source is speaking. The chronicle says there was an army. The annals say there was an Ímar. The sagas say there was a son of Ragnar called Ivar. Only the combination of the three is the hypothesis, and it is plausible but unproven.

## FAQ

### Was Ivar the Boneless a real person?
Probably, at least as an army leader or as Ímar in Dublin. He is firmly named only in late sources, and whether the two are one man is open.

### When did Ivar the Boneless die?
If you equate him with Ímar, in 873. The Annals of Ulster record the death of the king of the Northmen of Ireland and Britain for that year. The sagas give other versions.

### Was Ivar really Ragnar's son?
Only the sagas claim it. No contemporary source states the connection.

### Why is he called the Boneless?
Because the sagas give him "no bones". Whether that means a disability, an insult or an image, we do not know.
