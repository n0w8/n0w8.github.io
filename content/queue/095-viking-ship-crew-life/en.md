**Life aboard a Viking ship** was mostly sailing, waiting and handiwork rather than endless rowing: the Gokstad ship from Norway had 16 oar holes on each side, yet on open water it was driven by its sail. A late 9th century account of a voyage from Hedeby to Truso lasted seven days and nights, and it ran "all the way under sail". Anyone who wants to understand the crew has to drop the picture of the sweating oarsman first.

This article pieces together what we can show about the daily routine, the jobs and the gear of a ship's company. It keeps a clear line between what finds and sources support and what is merely plausible, because we know surprisingly little about the inside of a longship. We know the ships well and the people aboard only indirectly. For how the vessels were built, see our overview of the [longship](/en/blog/the-longship/).

## Who sailed? Crew, rank and responsibility

A ship in the North was not an anonymous carrier but a small community with clear roles. The Old Norse words survive: the **stýrimaðr** was the steersman and skipper, a **háseti** an ordinary crew member, and the **stafnbúi** kept watch in the bow. One everyday sailor's word still goes back to that age: the steering oar sat on the right-hand side at the stern, called *stýrisborði*, "steering board". It became our word starboard.

How many men sailed depended on the ship type. A warship needed many hands at the oars, a merchant ship managed with a few. The table sorts three well-studied vessels.

| Ship | Find | Length | Type | Crew |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gokstad | Burial mound near Sandefjord, Norway | about 23 m | large sailing and war vessel | 32 oars, at least as many rowers |
| Skuldelev 2 ("Havhingsten") | Roskilde Fjord, Denmark | about 30 m (reconstructed) | longship | about 65 people on the 2007 replica voyage |
| Skuldelev 1 | Roskilde Fjord, Denmark | about 16 m | merchant ship (knarr) | only a handful |

One caveat applies to all of this: crew sizes are reconstructions. No ship came down to us with a muster list. The number of oar holes sets a floor for rowers, nothing more. For Gokstad that floor is secure: 16 holes per side, each with a turning cover that kept water out under sail. More on that vessel is in our article on the [Gokstad ship](/en/blog/gokstad-ship/).

## Rowing or sailing? What really happened aboard

The cliché of the Viking pulling at his oar for hours does not survive the sources. At sea the sail did the work, and oars were used in harbours, along narrow coasts, in calms and in battle. The account of the seafarer **Wulfstan**, which King Alfred had included in his Old English Orosius, gives seven days and nights under sail for the trip from Hedeby to Truso. **Ohthere** of northern Norway likewise described his voyage around the North Cape in the same text.

The Gokstad replica "Viking", which Magnus Andersen sailed across the Atlantic to the Chicago World's Fair in 1893, is the classic practical proof: with about a dozen men it reached Newfoundland in just under four weeks. The Roskilde replica "Havhingsten fra Glendalough", sailed from Denmark to Ireland in 2007, showed again how much of such a voyage was sail handling and how little was pure rowing.

Does that make the rowing crew pointless? Not at all. Oars freed the longship from the wind. Anyone who had to leave a fjord against the breeze, or reach a harbour at night, needed rested arms.

## The daily routine: sail, steering, bailing

Judging from the find picture of a ship like Gokstad, the working day can be deduced sensibly. Much of this is inference, but tied closely to the vessel.

- **Sailing:** the mast stood in a massive step, the mast fish, and could be lowered when needed. Setting, trimming and taking in the sail was teamwork.
- **Steering:** the side rudder demanded strength and attention, so the helmsmen probably took turns.
- **Bailing:** every wooden ship takes on water, even a well-built one. Bailing was a standing job.
- **Lookout:** the man in the bow watched for shoals, coastal landmarks and strange sails.
- **Upkeep:** ropes, rigging and seams in the sailcloth needed constant checking.

A watch system in the modern sense of fixed shifts cannot be proven for the Viking Age. That crews slept in turns on long passages is logical; how exactly it was organised, we do not know.

## Food, drink and sleep

This is where the sources are thinnest, so the honest answer first: there are no surviving menus for shipboard meals. What we have are finds from everyday life of the period and reasonable conclusions. Durable food mattered most: **dried fish**, salted or smoked meat, bread, cheese and butter. Drink had to keep. Fresh water runs short at sea, which suits beer, but also sour whey, a store mentioned often in sagas from the North. Which of these were actually carried on a given voyage cannot be shown case by case. For the kitchen on land, see our article on [Viking food](/en/blog/viking-food-diet/).

As far as we know, people slept on deck or under a rigged tent. A ship like Gokstad had no cabins. Merchant ships of the knarr type had small decked areas at the ends with cargo in the middle. That crews on coastal runs went ashore in the evening to cook and sleep is the obvious solution, supported by landing places at known sites.

## Gear: shields on the rail and small boats

What was found in the Gokstad grave shows how a crew travelled. Along the rail hung 64 shields, 32 on each side, painted alternately yellow and black. That probably had a practical side effect: the shields could shelter rowers from spray and marked the vessel as a warship from far away. There were also three small boats, used for landings and ferrying in harbour. Whether the shields really hung outside on the open sea is unclear; many researchers assume they were mounted only in port or in times of danger.

The find also included oars, the steering oar and a gangplank. No sail survives, but replicas suggest that a woollen square sail took an enormous amount of working time. Each person's own kit was probably weapons, clothing, a knife and some provisions, little else would fit. That matches the tradition of the **leiðangr**, the levy, which was drawn from the farmers of a coastal district: each district supplied a ship with crew and provisions, and the men went back to farm and field after the season. The crew was often the same neighbourhood that worked together at home.

## Navigation and orientation

Without a compass, crews went by landmarks, the sun, bird flight, water colour and experience. They relied on the knowledge of a few men. The steersman had to judge latitude roughly if he wanted to hold a course for many days. How that worked, and what to make of the famous sunstone hypothesis, is covered in our article on [Viking navigation](/en/blog/viking-navigation-sunstone/).

## Myth versus fact: claims about the crew

| Claim | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Vikings rowed across the Atlantic | False. At sea the sail did the work; rowing was mostly for coasts and harbours. |
| Everyone aboard was a warrior | Unproven for merchant ships, which carried small specialised crews. |
| Women never sailed | Unclear. Families are attested on settlement voyages such as to Iceland or Greenland. |
| There were bunks like on modern ships | No evidence. Finds such as Gokstad have no cabins. |
| They sailed only in summer | Largely true. Ships were often drawn ashore in winter. |

## Frequently asked questions

### How many men sailed on a Viking ship?

It depended on the type. A merchant ship like Skuldelev 1 needed only a few people; a longship like Gokstad with 32 oars needed at least as many rowers, plus a steersman and lookout. On the large Skuldelev 2 the 2007 replica carried about 65.

### Did Vikings only row?

No. On open water they sailed. They rowed in calms, in harbours, on rivers and in battle. The surviving voyage accounts mention multi-day passages under sail.

### Where did Vikings sleep aboard?

On deck or under a tent. Known ship finds have no cabins. On coastal trips crews preferred to land for the night.

### What did Vikings eat at sea?

There is no direct evidence. Durable foods such as dried fish, salted meat, bread, cheese and butter are plausible, along with beer or sour whey.

## Our take: a ship was a shared house with no spare room

Lay the finds side by side and one thing stands out: life aboard was cramped, wet and without privacy. A ship like Gokstad measures about 23 metres, yet not a square metre is roofed or closed off. Thirty people and their gear shared an open deck for days and nights.

Our reading: that helps explain why loyalty, oaths and reputation mattered so much in this culture. On a ship you could not walk away from each other. Whoever sailed had to rely on the next man and get along with him. Daily life at sea was less adventure than a permanent test of cooperation. Does that correct the heroic image? At least it makes the seafarers more human.

Further sources are the accounts of Ohthere and Wulfstan in Alfred's Old English Orosius, the finds at the Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde and the Viking Ship Museum in Oslo.
