The **Hoen hoard** is the largest Viking Age treasure ever found in Norway: just over 2.5 kilograms of precious metal, nearly all of it gold, dug out of a bog at Nedre Hoen farm near Hokksund, Buskerud, in 1834. It holds two heavy neck rings, a Frankish trefoil brooch, arm rings and finger rings, dozens of pendants, well over a hundred beads and 20 coins from Rome, Byzantium, the Frankish empire, England and, above all, the Arab world. It was buried in the second half of the ninth century and now belongs to the Museum of Cultural History at the University of Oslo.

Weight alone isn't what makes it remarkable. Viking Age hoards in Scandinavia are almost always silver, with a little gold at most. Hoen flips that around, and its gold isn't bullion but finished, well-worn jewelry gathered from half of Europe and the Islamic world.

## How was the Hoen hoard found?

Norwegian broadcaster NRK puts the date at 12 August 1834. **Halvor Torstensen Kvernmoen**, a cotter (*husmann*) from Heddal in Telemark, had been hired to drain the bog on the big farm of Nedre Hoen when a gold ring suddenly appeared hanging from his spade. More pieces lay in the ditch, wrapped in what looked like a white film. The hoard had probably been bundled in cloth that slowly broke down in the wet ground. No box or container is recorded.

![An old wooden spade with an iron cutting edge against a dark background](/images/hoen-hoard-2.jpg)
*The spade used to dig in the bog at Nedre Hoen in 1834. According to NRK, it is still kept at the farm. Photo: Kulturhistorisk Museum, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

The landowner, **Borger Christophersen Hoen**, took the whole find to the Ministry of Finance in Christiania, today's Oslo. In 1836 the Storting voted to redeem it for 2,030 speciedaler and gave it to the university, according to the University of Oslo's coin cabinet (NRK gives 2,060). Hoen split the money evenly with the cotter, and for Halvor that was a fortune: by NRK's calculation, about 49 years' wages for a farm labourer.

Was everything recovered? Nobody knows. The Danish encyclopedia lex.dk says the pieces came out of the bog in several rounds over the nineteenth century, so the hoard may not be complete. A first publication, in Latin, appeared in 1835. Today an oversized replica of the spade stands on a roundabout in central Hokksund, and an information board marks the find spot.

## What is in the Hoen hoard?

How many pieces the Hoen hoard contains depends on who is counting. The standard monograph from 2006, edited by **Signe Horn Fuglesang** and **David M. Wilson**, lists 206 items: 54 objects of gold or silver-gilt, 20 coins and 132 beads of glass or semi-precious stone. Store norske leksikon, the Norwegian national encyclopedia, counts 20 coins, 50 pieces of jewelry and 125 beads. The metal comes to 2.548 kilograms according to lex.dk.

The key pieces at a glance:

- **Two gold neck rings.** The heavier one weighs roughly a kilogram; figures range from just under (NRK) to over 1.1 kilograms (University of Oslo coin cabinet).
- **Three arm rings and two finger rings of gold.** One sturdy arm ring weighs about 400 grams, according to NRK.
- **The trefoil brooch**, originally a Frankish strap distributor and the centrepiece of the hoard.
- **Around 25 gold and nine silver pendants** (lex.dk), including Scandinavian filigree work and a Byzantine medallion with a Greek inscription.
- **An English gold ring** in the ninth-century Anglo-Saxon Trewhiddle style.
- **Two small gold cases**, described by Store norske leksikon as reliquaries from the Mediterranean.
- **Beads of glass and semi-precious stone**, which Peter Steppuhn showed to be a mix of imported and locally made beads.
- **20 coins**, every one fitted with a loop, 15 of them gold.

Apart from the big rings and the strap distributor, every smaller piece and coin was turned into a pendant and strung with the beads into one spectacular necklace. Think of the hoard less as a pile of gold, more as somebody's jewelry box.

## Where did the Hoen hoard coins come from?

The 20 coins are the clearest evidence of how far Viking Age connections reached. The University of Oslo's coin cabinet lists them as follows:

| Origin | Number | Date struck |
|---|---|---|
| Arab dinars with Kufic inscriptions | 10 | 769/70 to 848/49 |
| Byzantine solidi | 2 | 751/75 to c. 843/56 |
| Merovingian imitation of a Byzantine solidus | 1 | 582/602 |
| Roman solidus | 1 | 364-367 |
| Frankish deniers | 2 | 814-840 |
| Gold imitation of a Frankish denier | 1 | 814-840 |
| Imitations of Frankish solidi | 2 | c. 816-840 |
| Anglo-Saxon penny | 1 | c. 810 |
| **Total** | **20** | 364 to 848/49 |

So half the coins are Arab gold dinars from the era of the Abbasid Caliphate, which corrects a common picture: Hoen is not mainly a Byzantine or Frankish hoard. Byzantium contributes two genuine solidi and a Merovingian copy of a Byzantine coin. The Frankish world supplies two genuine deniers and three imitations, all five tied to the reign of Louis the Pious (814-840). The oldest coin, a Roman solidus of 364-367, was already some 500 years old when the hoard was buried.

How Arab precious metal reached the North is the subject of our article on [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/). Mostly it was silver dirhams coming in via the Volga and the Baltic. Gold dinars like these are the exception.

The loops show how the coins got to Hoen. Numismatist **Mark Blackburn** identified six loop types and concluded that the 20 coins passed through the hands of gold- or silversmiths on at least eleven separate occasions. Loops on the Arab coins are mostly built differently from those on the Carolingian ones, pointing to different workshops: soldering seems to have been more common in Scandinavia, riveting in Francia. Runologist James Knirk also found scratched patterns on ten coins, nine of them Arab.

> Ten of the 20 coins are Arab dinars. Hoen is less a piece of Francia in Norway than a cross-section of the gold routes of the ninth century.

## The trefoil brooch: from Frankish sword belt to Viking jewelry

The showpiece of the hoard isn't a ring but a three-armed gold mount. It started life as a **strap distributor**, the fitting where the straps of a sword belt met, and Store norske leksikon counts such belts among the most important status symbols at the Carolingian court. It is decorated with acanthus foliage in gold and niello, and the encyclopedia calls it unique even by international standards. In the monograph, art historian Westermann-Angerhausen compared it with work from the time of Charles the Bald and concluded that it cannot be older than the latest coins in the hoard.

![The three-armed gold mount of the Hoen hoard, with coin pendants on white plinths behind it](/images/hoen-hoard-4.jpg)
*The Frankish strap distributor, later reworked into a trefoil brooch, with coin pendants in the VÍKINGR exhibition. Photo: Wolfmann, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons*

Later someone fitted a pin to the back and turned the mount into a brooch, and wear marks show it was worn for some time before burial. Katherine Elliott of the Museum of Cultural History told the science site forskning.no in 2019 that it is the first piece of its kind found in Norway and that it launched a whole new style: the shape was copied and became a popular woman's brooch worn at the neck. For how such a brooch sat over a shawl or cloak, see [What Did the Vikings Wear?](/en/blog/viking-clothing-jewelry/).

## How old is the Hoen hoard?

The youngest coin dates to 848/49, so the hoard cannot have been buried before then. How much later comes down to style and wear:

- **The Scandinavian jewelry** spans at least two, probably three phases of early Viking art, says Fuglesang. The oldest group is roughly contemporary with the wood carvings from the [Oseberg ship](/en/blog/oseberg-ship/) burial, made before 834.
- **The latest objects** date to the third quarter of the ninth century, by the research team's consensus.
- **The bead necklace** grew over one or at most two generations, according to Steppuhn.
- **Some pendants** are so worn that fine gold granules have partly rubbed away.

Store norske leksikon dates the burial to 875-900, while David Wilson and Ole Klindt-Jensen suggested around 860 back in 1966. Our reading: the last quarter of the ninth century is most plausible, after the necklace had been worn for a while. A precise year is beyond what the find can tell us.

## Why was the Hoen hoard buried?

There is no certain answer, but there are several serious readings:

- **A temple treasure:** an earlier idea with one big problem. There are no parallels for it anywhere in the Norse world.
- **A votive offering** to a deity: some researchers see the deposit in a bog as a religious act.
- **A hiding place:** the 2006 team assumes this was the accumulated wealth of one family, hidden in troubled times and never reclaimed. In her review (Collegium Medievale, 2008), archaeologist Bergljot Solberg criticised the team for ruling out other explanations too quickly.

Whether the gold arrived through raiding, trade or gift exchange cannot be decided, says Store norske leksikon. The setting is clearer: with the sea level higher in the Viking Age, the farm lay closer to the Drammensfjord, where traffic between inland eastern Norway and the coast could be controlled.

Who wore it? Hanne Lovise Aannestad, an archaeologist at the Museum of Cultural History, told NRK that the mix of jewelry points to a woman, and Steppuhn writes of a "lady of Hoen". Local tradition links the gold to a chieftain called Bødmod of Skot, but there is no evidence for that.

Unlike the hoards in our article on [Viking silver hoards](/en/blog/viking-silver-hoards/), Hoen isn't hacksilver and ingots for paying by weight but jewelry that was worn, which makes it more personal than almost any other hoard of the period.

## Where can you see the Hoen hoard today?

The hoard has belonged to the university in Christiania, now Oslo, since 1836, and from 1904 it was the centrepiece of the new Viking Age hall at the Historical Museum. According to the timeline of the digital exhibition "Viking Gold. Treasure politics since 1800", it was moved to a bank vault in rural Norway in early October 1939, weeks after the Second World War broke out and half a year before the German invasion of 9 April 1940.

After the war it travelled widely, including to London, New York and Minneapolis for the 1980 exhibition "The Vikings", and to Paris, Berlin and Copenhagen in 1992/93. Since 2019 it has been on show in the permanent exhibition **VÍKINGR** at the Historical Museum, part of the Museum of Cultural History in Oslo.

## FAQ

### Where is the Hoen hoard displayed?

At the Museum of Cultural History of the University of Oslo. Since 2019 it has been part of the VÍKINGR exhibition at the Historical Museum in central Oslo.

### How much does the Hoen hoard weigh?

Just over 2.5 kilograms of metal in total, 2.548 kilograms according to lex.dk, nearly all of it gold. The heavier neck ring alone weighs about a kilogram.

### Who found the Hoen hoard?

Halvor Torstensen Kvernmoen, a cotter hired to drain a bog at Nedre Hoen farm, found it in 1834. He split the Storting's payment of 1836 with the landowner, Borger Christophersen Hoen.

### How old is the Hoen hoard?

The youngest coin dates to 848/49 and the latest jewelry to the third quarter of the ninth century. Store norske leksikon dates the burial to between 875 and 900. The oldest coin is a Roman solidus from 364-367.

### Is the Hoen hoard the largest Viking gold hoard?

It is regarded as the largest Viking Age gold hoard in Scandinavia, both by the University of Oslo's coin cabinet and by the Danish encyclopedia lex.dk.

## Conclusion: a bog find that reaches the Caliphate

The Hoen hoard is more than Norway's heaviest Viking find. Arab dinars, Byzantine solidi and Scandinavian filigree on one necklace, plus an English gold ring and a Frankish sword-belt fitting turned brooch: that is how far the world of one wealthy family on the Drammenselva reached in the ninth century. A cotter from Heddal brought it back to light with his spade, and the farmer split the reward with him, which makes it one of archaeology's better find stories. Only the biggest question stays open: why did the woman who probably owned this jewelry never come back for it?
