**Idun** is the only Norse goddess whom the sources describe as the reason the gods do not grow old and die, yet nearly everything we know about her comes from just two texts: a skaldic poem from about 900 and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda from around 1220. The apples of youth are a myth with a surprisingly thin paper trail. This article sorts out what those texts really say, what later retellings added, and what is left once you separate the two.

## Who Is Idun? Keeper of the Gods' Youth

Iðunn (Old Norse, often spelled Idun or Idunn) is the wife of **Bragi**, god of poetry. In the *Gylfaginning* section of the Prose Edda, Snorri says she keeps in an ash-wood box (*eski*) the apples the gods must eat whenever they grow old. They then become young again, and according to Snorri this lasts "until Ragnarök". That is her whole function: Idun rules nothing, fights nothing and prophesies nothing. She looks after a supply. For that very reason the story cannot do without her, because without the apples the order of Asgard would collapse within days.

This makes her unusual. Most Norse gods stand for forces such as thunder, war or fertility. Idun stands for something the texts rarely discuss: that gods are vulnerable at all. They are not immortal by nature, only for as long as their supply holds. For the wider divine family, see our [Norse gods family tree](/en/blog/norse-gods-family-tree/).

## What Do the Sources Say? Two Texts Carry the Myth

The evidence is thin. At its core we have:

- **Haustlöng** by the Icelandic skald Þjóðólfr inn hvinverski, composed around 900. The poem describes pictures on a shield and tells the abduction of Idun along the way. Here the apples are called "ellilyf ása", the old-age remedy of the Æsir.
- **Skáldskaparmál** and **Gylfaginning** in Snorri's Prose Edda, around 1220, which retell the abduction at length and cite Þjóðólfr.
- **Lokasenna** in the Poetic Edda, where Loki accuses Idun of having laid her arms around her brother's killer. Idun rejects this and asks only for calm.

Notice what is missing: no cult site, no place name and no sacrificial find that can be tied to Idun with confidence. The idea of a widely worshipped fertility goddess is a modern projection. Whether the Vikings venerated her as a figure in her own right is an open question.

The word "apple" is also less clear than it sounds. Old Norse *epli* can mean fruit in general, including nuts. Wild crab apples grew in Scandinavia, while sweet cultivated apples probably became common only through trade and monasteries. ## The Abduction by Þjazi: The Story in Six Steps

Snorri tells the myth almost like a script.

1. **The journey:** Odin, Loki and Hœnir cross barren mountains and slaughter an ox, but the meat will not cook.
2. **The eagle:** An eagle in an oak claims it can make the fire work if it gets a share of the ox. It then takes both thighs and both shoulders.
3. **The catch:** Loki strikes it with a pole but sticks to the eagle and is dragged through the air. The eagle is the giant **Þjazi**, and he releases Loki only on the promise to lure Idun and her apples out of Asgard.
4. **The trick:** Loki tells Idun of even finer apples in a wood and asks her to bring hers for comparison. Þjazi swoops down as an eagle and carries her to Þrymheimr.
5. **The decay:** In Asgard the gods turn grey and old. Looking for the culprit, they threaten Loki with death.
6. **The rescue:** Loki borrows Freyja's falcon cloak, flies to Þrymheimr, turns Idun into a nut and carries her home. Þjazi follows as an eagle. The gods light a fire that burns his feathers, and he is killed.

How Loki got into such trouble, and why he so often brings rescue and ruin together, is explored in our article on [Loki the trickster](/en/blog/loki-trickster-god/).

## How Old Do the Gods Get Without Idun?

Snorri is drastic about the consequences: once Idun is gone, the Æsir turn grey and old. He does not say they die at once, but the gods immediately read the situation as a threat and turn on Loki. For a thirteenth-century audience this must have been a powerful image, since age, illness and early death were everywhere in daily life. The idea that even gods are not safe from them relativises their power and makes them more human.

## Skadi, Njörd and the Aftermath

The myth does not end with the giant's death. Þjazi's daughter **Skadi** arrives in full armour to take revenge. The gods offer compensation: she may choose a husband among them, but she sees only their feet. She picks the most beautiful pair and gets **Njörd** instead of the hoped-for Baldr. Loki must also make her laugh, which he does with a goat and a cord, details Snorri only hints at. Odin throws Þjazi's eyes into the sky, where they become stars.

What is striking is how little the abduction costs Idun herself. She is neither asked nor punished, and she then vanishes from the plot. That a woman becomes a bargaining chip between giants and gods is a recurring pattern in the myths.

## Idun in Lokasenna: The Disturbing Accusation

In *Lokasenna* Loki accuses every god of something. He hits Idun with the claim that she laid her arms around the killer of her brother. The text does not say who that brother is. Idun does not hit back; she asks for peace. That she has a brother in the Poetic Edda while Snorri says nothing of the kind shows how poorly the sources fit together.

My reading: the poet of *Lokasenna* knew myths about Idun that have not survived. The poem assumes knowledge we no longer have. It is worth remembering this when reading any catalogue of gods. Much of Norse mythology is only an echo.

## Myth Box: What Holds Up, What Was Added Later?

| Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Idun keeps apples that keep the gods young | Attested in Þjóðólfr and Snorri |
| Idun is Bragi's wife | Attested in Snorri and Lokasenna |
| Idun is a fertility goddess | Modern interpretation, not attested |
| The apples were golden | Snorri does not call them golden; a later addition |
| Idun was worshipped in a cult | No secure evidence |
| The apples make the gods immortal | Snorri: only "until Ragnarök", and they renew youth, not invulnerability |

"Golden apples" probably comes from confusion with the golden apples of the Hesperides in Greek myth. In our sources they are simply apples.

## What Does the Myth Mean? Our Editorial View

One can read Idun as a parable of the seasons: fruit ripens in autumn, winter follows, life returns in spring. Plausible, but unproven. I prefer a plainer reading. The story is about dependence. The most powerful beings of the North rely on a supply managed by one person, and the myth shows what happens when it is stolen: panic, blame, and a god who, afraid for his life, rescues the very situation he caused.

That fits Loki, who in the myths is so often both trigger and saviour. More on this pattern is in our article on [Fenrir and Ragnarök](/en/blog/fenrir-wolf-mythology/).

The setting also deserves a look. Asgard here is not a safe fortress but a place open to attack: a single giant in bird shape is enough to throw the gods into panic. The myth also says that order in the North is never a given and must be defended again and again, against giants, against cold and against one's own carelessness.

## FAQ

### Who is Idun in Norse mythology?
Idun is the goddess who guards the apples of youth. She is the wife of the god Bragi, attested mainly in Snorri Sturluson and in the skaldic poem Haustlöng.

### Were Idun's apples made of gold?
Not in the sources. Snorri does not mention golden apples. The image probably arose from mixing in Greek mythology.

### Do Idun's apples make the gods immortal?
Not quite. They keep the gods young when they age, and according to Snorri this lasts until Ragnarök. Immortality in the strict sense is not mentioned.

### Who kidnapped Idun?
The giant Þjazi in eagle form, after Loki promised to lure her out of Asgard. The gods later killed him when he pursued her.

### Did the Vikings worship Idun?
There is no secure evidence. Neither place names nor sacrificial finds can be tied to her with certainty.

## Conclusion

Idun is a goddess without a cult but with one of the most memorable stories of the Edda. The abduction by Þjazi and the role of the apples are secure; everything else, from "golden" fruit to a fertility goddess, is later addition. The charm of this myth lies in how little it reveals.
