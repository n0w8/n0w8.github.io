Across much of the Viking Age north, what counted in a payment was not the picture stamped on the metal but its weight: **hacksilver**, meaning chopped-up arm rings, ingots and coins, was weighed on small scales until the sum matched. Coins existed, but for a long time they were just one kind of silver among many, not money with a fixed face value. This article explains how paying in silver worked day to day, where the metal came from, and why merchants carried a knife, a balance and a touchstone.

## What Exactly Is Hacksilver?

The term describes silver that was cut up on purpose. It includes pieces of arm rings and neck rings, chopped ingots, broken jewellery and whole or halved coins, above all Arab dirhams from the caliphate. The shape of a piece hardly mattered. What mattered was its silver content, and the only way to measure that was to weigh it.

That is why so many hoards contain what looks like broken jewellery. It was not carelessness but design: an arm ring could be worn as a store of value, and a piece cut off whenever a payment was due. Wearing a whole ring showed your wealth on your arm. This is also why skaldic poetry calls a generous lord a "ring-giver" or "ring-breaker". How such finds look in the ground is covered in the overview of [Viking silver hoards](/en/blog/viking-silver-hoards/); here the question is how silver changed hands in daily life.

## Where Did the Silver Come From?

Scandinavia had few silver sources that were exploited in the ninth and tenth centuries. The metal had to be imported, and the sources shifted over time.

- **Arab silver (ninth to early tenth century):** Dirhams reached the north along the Russian rivers and through trading places such as Birka and Hedeby. The trade is well documented through coin finds; see [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/).
- **Plunder and tribute (ninth to eleventh century):** Raids and [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/) brought English and Frankish silver. The tribute payments to England are given in the sources for 991, 994 and 1012 as tens of thousands of pounds of silver.
- **Trade in furs, slaves and amber:** Goods were swapped for silver in the east, and the silver was swapped for goods again in the north.

The result was a money circuit without a central bank. Whoever held silver could melt it down, work it into jewellery or cut it up to pay. There was no single currency.

## The Silver Balance: A Merchant's Key Tool

A trader using hacksilver needed three things: a scale, weights and a way to test silver. At trading places from Kaupang to Birka, archaeologists have found folding balances with tiny pans that fitted into a case, together with small weights, often of lead and sometimes of bronze, a few of them decorated. Many of these weights appear to follow Arab weight systems, which fits the flow of silver from the east.

To test quality, buyers nicked or chopped the silver. Many dirhams and ring fragments therefore show small cuts known as test marks. They show that a buyer wanted to know whether there was silver under the surface or a core of base metal. Fraud was clearly a real risk, and nicking was the simple answer.

> The hacksilver system made every merchant his own inspector. Trust was good; a balance and a knife were better.

## Reckoning in Marks, Ounces and Ells

Weights needed names. In Scandinavia a reckoning in **marks** and **ores** took hold, with one mark equal to eight ores. The exact gram values varied by region and period, so conversions should be treated with caution, and the sources give orders of magnitude rather than fixed standards.

Iceland is a vivid case. The medieval law code Grágás states fines and prices in ounces of silver and in ells of **vaðmál**, a coarse woollen cloth. According to tradition, six ells of vaðmál equalled one ounce of silver. On an island with no silver mine and no mint, cloth was a practical substitute currency, because a household could produce it itself. Life on an Icelandic farm, described in [Viking Farm Life](/en/blog/viking-farm-life-year/), therefore also revolved around wool as a means of payment.

## Coins Existed, but Not as Everyday Money

The claim that "Vikings knew no coins" is wrong. They knew them well: they plundered them, traded them and used them as a source of silver. Hedeby and Ribe struck their own coins early, around 995 Olof Skotkonung had the first Swedish coins minted at Sigtuna, and in Denmark and Norway coinage took hold in the tenth and eleventh centuries.

The point is a different one, though: for a long time a coin was worth something not because a ruler vouched for it but because of its silver content. The shift to coinage as face value came step by step, with stronger kings who guaranteed weight and purity on their coins and wanted to standardise payment within their realms. Until then the balance had the last word.

## A Purchase in Practice

Picture a deal at Hedeby, reconstructed from finds and descriptions rather than from any surviving record. A trader wants to buy furs and offers half an arm ring. The seller puts the piece on the scale, sets lead weights against it and compares. If the sum is too high, a piece is cut off with a knife. If it is too low, the buyer adds a few coin fragments. In between, the seller makes a test nick to be sure the metal is silver all the way through.

The process is slow, but it has one advantage: it works anywhere there is silver and a scale, even across language barriers. A Frankish merchant, one from Dublin and one from the east could agree on the same reckoning without knowing the same coin. That is probably the real reason the system lasted so long. It matches the picture of traders and trading places in the article on [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/).

The price of that flexibility was mistrust. Counterfeiters were no secret, and the test marks are the visible proof. Whoever cheated at the scales probably risked his reputation, which in a society without paper money was valuable capital.

## Myth Box: Three Misconceptions About Viking Money

**Myth 1: "Vikings had no money."** They had money, just in the form of weight. Silver was an accepted means of payment whose value anyone could check.

**Myth 2: "Hacksilver is scrap."** No, it is a deliberately produced means of payment. Cutting it up made it possible to pay to the gram.

**Myth 3: "A hoard means poverty."** The opposite: whoever could bury silver had wealth. Why so many hoards were never recovered is unknown; death, flight or war are all possible.

## Our Assessment

The most interesting thing about hacksilver is not the silver but the trust such a system requires. Every trade was a small test procedure: balance, test nick, haggling. That this worked for centuries between merchants from Dublin, Hedeby and Kyiv speaks for a developed trading culture, which should not be buried under the raider cliche. The state coin was an answer to a problem that Vikings had already solved with scales and weights.

## FAQ

### What did Vikings pay with?

With silver by weight: hacksilver, whole and cut coins, rings and ingots. In some regions, such as Iceland, cloth like vaðmál and livestock also served as payment.

### Did Vikings have coins?

Yes. Their own coinage appeared at trading places such as Ribe and Hedeby and later under kings in Denmark, Norway and Sweden. Before that, foreign coins were just silver among other silver.

### How did Vikings test whether silver was real?

They nicked or chopped the piece and checked whether the inside was silver too. Such test marks are visible on many surviving dirhams and rings.

### Why was silver buried?

Possible reasons include safety in unsettled times, a lack of banks and religious offerings. That many hoards were never recovered suggests their owners could not return.

## Conclusion

Hacksilver was not a stopgap but a working money system built on weight, testing and trust. Anyone who wants to understand the Viking Age should take the balance and the test nick as seriously as the sword and the ship.
