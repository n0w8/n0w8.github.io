In medieval Iceland the year had 364 days, and nobody noticed the missing one until the summer started drifting into spring. The **Viking calendar** was not the Roman calendar, and it was certainly not the neat "wheel of the year" that fantasy websites like to draw. It was a working tool for farmers, sailors and lawspeakers. When does haymaking start? When must the cattle be indoors? When does the assembly meet? Answering those questions took observation, a habit of counting nights, and a few fixed landmarks in the year.

Almost everything we know about this calendar comes from Iceland, and almost all of it was written down after the Viking Age. That is the first awkward fact. The sources are twelfth and thirteenth century texts by Christian authors looking back at a vanished world. Still, a surprisingly clear picture emerges once you separate what is documented from what was added later.

## Two seasons, not four: vetr and sumar

Old Norse had two real seasons: **vetr** (winter) and **sumar** (summer). Spring and autumn existed as words, but as transitions rather than blocks of the year. In the *Gylfaginning*, Snorri Sturluson turns the two seasons into people: Sumar is the son of Svásuðr, Vetr the son of Vindsval. Winter comes from a harsh, cold family, summer from a gentle one. The myth echoes lived experience. In Scandinavia the year feels like a swing between dark and light more than a staircase of four equal steps.

Winter began once the harvest was in and the autumn slaughter done. Summer began when the herds went out and the sailing season opened. In Icelandic tradition the first day of winter is a Saturday between 21 and 27 October, and the first day of summer a Thursday between 19 and 25 April. That "first day of summer" (Sumardagurinn fyrsti) is still a public holiday in Iceland. Winter and summer thus each last roughly half the year, which suits farm life: half the year you produce, the other half you survive.

The split mattered in ritual too. In the *Ynglinga saga* Snorri describes three great sacrifices: one at the start of winter "for a good year," one in midwinter "for growth," and one at the start of summer, the "sacrifice for victory." The feasts sit exactly on the tipping points of the year. How historical that description is remains open, since Snorri wrote roughly three centuries after the Viking Age, but the logic matches what we can infer from daily life.

## Counting nights instead of days

Norse speakers counted time in **nights**, not days. This was not a northern quirk. The Roman writer Tacitus remarks in his *Germania* that the Germanic peoples reckoned by nights and that night came before day. Traces survive in English: *fortnight* simply means "fourteen nights" (Old English *fēowertīene niht*), and German still says "Weihnachten" in the plural.

The week, by contrast, was probably an import. The day names mix Norse gods with a Roman template: Þórsdagr (Thursday) and Frjádagr (Friday) recall Thor and Freyja or Frigg, while *laugardagr* simply means "washing day." The pattern is plainly the Roman seven-day rhythm dressed in Germanic names. When exactly the seven-day week became normal in the North is uncertain. For Iceland it is certainly present in the calendar system described below.

## How Iceland fixed a 364-day year

Our best source is a short passage in Ari Þorgilsson's *Íslendingabók*, written around 1130. Ari says the first Icelanders reckoned the year at 364 days, exactly 52 weeks. That has a neat advantage, since every date falls on the same weekday every year. But a solar year is a little over a day longer, so the calendar ran steadily behind the seasons. Eventually people noticed that summer was creeping into spring.

Ari tells how the problem was solved. A man named Þorsteinn surtr proposed adding a whole week every seventh summer, the *sumarauki* ("summer addition"). The Althing accepted it, traditionally dated to around 955 to 960. That is remarkable: a calendar corrected by **legislation**, not by astronomers. The assembly itself is described in our article on the [Viking thing and its law](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly/).

The arithmetic holds up. Fifty-two weeks plus one extra week every seven years averages exactly 365 days. That is slightly under the true solar year of about 365.24 days, so the error built up only slowly. For a farming society that needed the calendar mainly for assembly dates and contracts, it was good enough for a long time.

## The twelve months: a table

The calendar had twelve months of 30 days each, or 360 days. The missing four days were the *aukanætur* ("extra nights"), inserted between Sólmánuður and Heyannir. The table gives the months as the Icelandic tradition has them. Several name meanings are uncertain and debated by scholars, and the match to modern months is only approximate.

| Month | Roughly | Season | Possible meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gormánuður | Oct-Nov | Winter | "slaughter month" (gor = innards) |
| Ýlir | Nov-Dec | Winter | linked to *Jól*, Yule |
| Mörsugur | Dec-Jan | Winter | "tallow sucker," fat-stores season |
| Þorri | Jan-Feb | Winter | unclear, perhaps "frost time" |
| Góa | Feb-Mar | Winter | unclear, perhaps a personal name |
| Einmánuður | Mar-Apr | Winter | "single month," last of winter |
| Harpa | Apr-May | Summer | unclear, perhaps a woman's name |
| Skerpla | May-Jun | Summer | unclear |
| Sólmánuður | Jun-Jul | Summer | "sun month" |
| Heyannir | Jul-Aug | Summer | "hay work" |
| Tvímánuður | Aug-Sep | Summer | "double month" |
| Haustmánuður | Sep-Oct | Summer | "autumn month" |

Winter opened with Gormánuður, the slaughter month, and closed with Einmánuður. Summer ran from Harpa to Haustmánuður. In Icelandic custom, Þorri begins on a Friday between 19 and 25 January and Góa on a Sunday between 18 and 24 February. Those days are now called *bóndadagur* ("farmer's day") and *konudagur* ("woman's day"), and Icelanders still mark them.

## Yule, deadlines and the sun in daily life

The great winter feast was Yule. In the *Hákonar saga góða*, Snorri tells how King Haakon the Good moved Yule to Christmas in the tenth century. Before that, he says, Yule was held at "Hǫggunótt," midwinter night, for three nights. Caution applies again, but the text shows that the feast hung on the middle of winter, not a fixed calendar date. For the food side of the feast, see our [Yule bread recipe](/en/blog/viking-yule-bread-recipe/).

In daily practice the calendar mattered most for deadlines. The assembly met in summer, debts and lawsuits had legal time limits, and the rules on moving farm hands and tenants had set dates. The Icelandic law code *Grágás* contains such dates: people had to be in particular places in particular weeks. So the calendar was first a **legal instrument**, and only second a natural one.

For the time of day people used the sun. There were fixed marks for parts of the day, such as *dagmál* ("day meal," roughly mid-morning) or *miðr aptann*, and a unit called *eykt*, one eighth of a day and night. Scholars still argue about the exact values, but the principle is clear: time was read from the sky, the orientation of the house and habit. For the wider setting, read our overview of [everyday life in the Viking Age](/en/blog/viking-daily-life/).

## Myth or fact: was there a "wheel of the year"?

The internet loves the "Wheel of the Year" with eight festivals, supposedly an old Norse or Celtic tradition. That scheme comes from the twentieth-century Wicca movement, not from Viking sources. What the sources do support for the Viking Age is sacrifices at the start of winter, midwinter and the start of summer, plus the assembly dates. No more than that.

The same goes for the **primstav**, the Norwegian calendar staff with runes and symbols for saints' days. It is sometimes sold as a Viking inheritance. Surviving examples belong to the Middle Ages and later and carry Christian feast days. Whether wooden calendar sticks existed in the Viking Age we do not know, and cannot prove.

## Our assessment

The Viking calendar shows how much history hides in a small detail. A lawgiver in the 950s was willing to repair a calendar by vote, which means society saw time as something negotiable. That fits everything else we know about the assembly and legal life.

Still, be honest about the limits. The month names are Icelandic and come from sources of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. For Norway, Denmark or Sweden in the Viking Age they are not directly documented. Anyone hanging a "Viking calendar" on the wall is, strictly speaking, hanging a medieval Icelandic farmers' calendar. That is fine, as long as you know it.
