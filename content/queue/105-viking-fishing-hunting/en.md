**Viking fishing and hunting** supplied far less of the menu than films suggest: in the animal bones from Norse settlements, cattle, sheep, goats and pigs dominate, while wild game usually makes up only a small share. Fish, on the other hand, grew steadily in importance across the Viking Age and in Greenland became a staple. So how does that square with the image of the warrior who brings down a stag and roasts it on a spit?

In short, hunting was a supplement and a status marker, while the net and the line were everyday life. This article sorts out what archaeology, isotope analysis and written sources actually say about catching and hunting food, and where the sagas tell stories rather than report. For the wider picture, start with [Viking Food: What Did the Vikings Eat?](/en/blog/viking-food-diet/).

## Fishing: The Backbone of Coastal Farms

Anyone living on a Nordic coast had the sea at the door, and the finds show it was used. Settlement layers at Hedeby, Birka, Ribe and York yield bones and vertebrae of **herring**, **cod**, flatfish, eel and freshwater species. Tools turn up too: iron fish hooks, stone and lead sinkers, bone and wood netting needles, bark floats.

The methods were simple and effective:

- **Nets and traps:** In shallows and river mouths people set nets and woven fish traps. For eels and salmon they built fixed weirs of stakes and wattle.
- **Line fishing:** From boats, long lines were used for cod and haddock, with iron hooks baited with fish scraps or shellfish.
- **Spearing:** Salmon and trout in shallow rivers were speared in spring and autumn.
- **Herring shoals:** Shoals were encircled with nets. Herring was cheap, fatty and easy to preserve.

Fish mattered for more than taste. It was protein that stayed available when the fields failed. Practical ways to cook it are in [How to Smoke Fish Like a Viking](/en/blog/viking-smoked-fish-recipe/) and [Fried Herring, Viking Style](/en/blog/fried-herring-recipe-viking/).

## What a Catch Meant

Fishing was seasonal work, not adventure. In spring the herring came to the coast, summer meant cod and haddock, and in autumn salmon and sea trout climbed the rivers. A coastal farm combined agriculture and fishing: the boat lay near the barn and the nets hung beside the loom. The sources say little about who did which task, but mending, drying and salting happened between trips. Because fish spoils fast, what was done right after the catch decided its value: a fish hung, salted or smoked the same day could still feed a family in February.

## Dried Cod: From Catch to Trade Good

One of the biggest shifts of the era concerns **cod**. The archaeologist James Barrett and colleagues studied fish bones from sites across England and northern Europe and found a striking change: around the year 1000, the share of sea fish in towns rises sharply while freshwater fish declines. Barrett calls this the "Fish Event Horizon". Isotope measurements on cod bones from Hedeby also suggest that some fish came from cold northern waters, probably Norway. Dried cod, later known as **stockfish**, was therefore moved over long distances.

That fits what we know about commerce ([Viking Trade Routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/)). Someone fishing off the Lofoten coast could hang the catch on racks in cold air, with no salt and little effort, and the result kept for months. Fish was food and also a commodity.

## Hunting: More Fur and Ivory Than Roast

People hunted, of course. Red deer, roe deer, elk, wild boar, hare and wildfowl were available. But the value of hunting often lay elsewhere:

- **Furs:** Marten, squirrel, fox and beaver were trade goods that travelled as far as Byzantium and the Islamic world.
- **Antler and bone:** Red deer antler was the main raw material for combs, handles and gaming pieces.
- **Birds of prey:** Falcons from Norway and Iceland later became prized gifts in Europe.
- **Status:** Landowners often held hunting rights, and hunting became a pursuit of the elite, even if farmers had to guard their crops against wild animals.

That hunting rarely supplied most of the calories is not a contradiction. Game is seasonal, stocks are limited, and a farm with livestock does not urgently need the stag. In regions with little arable land the balance looked different.

## Ohthere's Account: Hunting in the Far North

One of the best written sources is the report of a man called **Ohthere** (Norse Óttarr), which King Alfred the Great had included in his Old English version of Orosius. Ohthere said he lived in the far north of Norway, in Hålogaland, and told Alfred:

> He was the northernmost of all Norwegians, owned a large herd of reindeer, and collected tribute from the Sami in furs, feathers, whale bone and ship ropes made of whale and seal hide.

He also hunted **walrus** for teeth and hide. The account is valuable because it shows hunting in the north as an industry, tangled up with tribute, trade and power. It dates to around the end of the ninth century and is one of the few texts in which a Norseman speaks for himself.

## Iceland, Greenland and the Hunt at Sea

In the North Atlantic islands hunting carried different weight because land and game were scarce. Iceland had no large land mammals apart from the arctic fox. Seabirds, eggs and seals helped instead. The Icelandic law code **Grágás** regulates in detail who owns a stranded whale: the landowner on whose beach it came ashore had a claim, while finders and neighbours received shares. That shows how valuable a whale was as a source of meat.

In Greenland the shift is especially measurable. Isotope analyses of skeletons from the Norse settlements show that the marine share of the diet rose markedly over the centuries. In the late phase a large part of the food came from the sea, above all from **seals**. The walrus was central too: its ivory was the settlers' most important export.

## Myth and Reality

The myth of the Viking who hunts daily and roasts game on the fire is stubborn. It leans on sagas and poems in which feast and hunt are literary set pieces. The boar **Sæhrímnir**, cooked every day in Valhalla and restored every night, is myth (see [Valhalla](/en/blog/valhalla-hall-fallen-warriors/)), not a kitchen report. Reality on the farm was humbler: porridge, bread, dairy, fish, meat from your own livestock, and game as the exception.

## Our Take

The hunter image survives partly because fish is unglamorous in stories. Mending nets does not make a heroic poem. Archaeologically the weighting is clear, though: to understand Viking life, think first of herring, cod and seal, and only then of the stag. In our view dried fish is the most underrated innovation of the period, because it linked coasts and markets. Plenty of bones also get lost: small fish bones survive poorly and are only found where layers are sieved, so older digs probably underestimated fish.

## FAQ

### What was the most important sea food for the Vikings?

Herring and cod. Both are common in settlement layers, and cod was traded widely as dried fish.

### Did Vikings hunt whales?

Stranded whales were used, and Iceland's Grágás regulates ownership. How far active whaling went is harder to prove.

### Did Vikings keep reindeer?

Ohthere's account mentions a large reindeer herd in northern Norway. It is a single testimony, but it fits what is known about the north.

### Did Vikings eat a lot of game?

In most settlements no. Bones show that livestock supplied the bulk of the meat and game stayed a small share.

### How did Vikings preserve fish?

By drying, smoking, salting and pickling in whey. Dried cod kept especially well.
