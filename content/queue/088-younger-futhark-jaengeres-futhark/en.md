The **Younger Futhark** has just 16 runes, eight fewer than the 24 of its predecessor, and from the early 800s onward almost every Viking Age rune inscription was written with this short row. It looks like a step backwards: the language had not lost any sounds, yet the alphabet had lost a third of its signs. Why would anyone do that, and how could anyone read the result? This article lays out the full row, explains its three forms, and separates what the evidence supports from what scholars still argue about.

## From 24 to 16: what changed around 800

The older rune row, the **Elder Futhark**, had 24 signs and was used from roughly the 2nd to the 8th century. How it is built is covered in our article on the [Elder Futhark](/en/blog/elder-futhark-runes/). Some time between the late 7th and the early 9th century, the row was cut sharply in Scandinavia. There is no precise year; the change seems to have spread over generations and not everywhere at once. By the Viking Age, traditionally dated from the Lindisfarne raid of 793, the short row was the standard script.

Why it happened is not settled. Three explanations are on the table:

- **Sound change:** Proto-Norse turned into Old Norse, endings wore away and some sounds merged. A few old runes became redundant.
- **Ease of use:** A smaller row is quicker to learn and to carve, which suits a script increasingly used for everyday notes and memorial stones.
- **Deliberate reform:** Some researchers suspect a planned reorganisation, perhaps pushed by elites who wanted a uniform script. This is not documented.

Our own reading: sound change alone cannot explain it, because the language still had many sounds that the new row wrote imprecisely. A mix of sound change and practical use is the most plausible story, while the reform idea stays a hypothesis rather than a fact.

## The 16 runes at a glance

The row is named, like the older one, after its first signs, here ᚠ ᚢ ᚦ ᚬ ᚱ ᚴ, so F-U-Þ-O-R-K. We know the rune names from medieval rune poems, a Norwegian one from the High Middle Ages and an Icelandic one preserved in later manuscripts. Both are centuries younger than the Viking Age, but the names themselves are probably old.

Two things stand out. First, every rune carries several values: **ᚢ** stands for u, o and v, **ᚴ** for k and g, **ᛏ** for t and d, **ᛒ** for b and p. Second, some signs were repurposed. ᛅ descends from *jera*, which used to be a j-sound, and ᛦ from *algiz*, which now wrote a sound that later drifted into r.

## Three hands: long-branch, short-twig and Hälsinge

The Younger Futhark did not come in a single shape. Researchers distinguish three main forms:

1. **Long-branch runes** (also called Danish runes): full staves and long branches. Common in Denmark, for instance on the [Jelling stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/), and also found in Norway and Sweden.
2. **Short-twig runes** (also Swedish-Norwegian runes): simplified signs with short branches. Mostly found in Sweden and Norway, and they look more like quick scribbles, probably because they were fast to write.
3. **Hälsinge runes** (staveless runes): the vertical stave is missing and only the branches remain. They come mainly from Hälsingland in Sweden and look like a secret code at first glance.

It is the same row in different handwriting. Whether the forms mirror regions or rather different writing traditions and situations is debated. As a rule of thumb, a Danish stone usually carries long-branch runes, while a Swedish inscription often shows short-twig forms.

## Reading with 16 signs: the ambiguity problem

Old Norse had well over 16 sounds, especially vowels. Anyone writing a word in the Younger Futhark had to let one rune stand in for several sounds. From today's point of view this makes the script imprecise.

A simple case: the sequence **ᚴᚢᚾ** can be read *kun* or *gun*, because ᚴ covers both k and g. Likewise ᛏ may be t or d. Whoever deciphers an inscription must know the language and work out from context which word is meant. It is like a crossword in which the letters are used several times over.

That does not mean the writers were sloppy. Their contemporaries knew the language and could fill the gaps. Later generations with only fragments of that knowledge struggled. To cut down the ambiguity, **dotted runes** appeared in the 11th century: an added dot or stroke separated, for example, a t from a d or a k from a g. Such extensions turn up in medieval inscriptions, including the wooden sticks from Bergen.

## Famous inscriptions in the Younger Futhark

A few finds show the range of the row:

- **The Jelling stones, Denmark:** The large stone of Harald states that he won all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian. It is usually dated to around 965 and is written in long-branch runes. The smaller stone, raised by Gorm for his wife Thyra, calls her "Denmark's ornament" or "Denmark's support", depending on how the word *bót* is read.
- **The Rök runestone, Sweden:** With about 750 characters it is regarded as the longest Viking Age rune inscription. It was probably made in the 9th century and mixes ordinary runes with **cipher runes**, in which signs are encoded by a fixed scheme. It shows how much room the short row left for play.
- **Uppland runestones, Sweden:** This region holds an especially large number of memorial stones, often for dead relatives or for men who died on expeditions. In total, several thousand rune inscriptions are known from Scandinavia, most of them in Sweden.

Runestones have also been found around the trading town of Hedeby in Schleswig, a sign that the script was known in trading circles. More about the place in our piece on [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/).

## What did the runes mean to people then?

Caution is needed here. Esoteric shops sell the 16 runes as an oracle alphabet with fixed "readings", much like tarot cards. The sources do not support that. The Younger Futhark was first of all a **script**: people carved names, kinship statements, ownership marks, trade notes and occasionally blessings. The rune names, such as *fé* for cattle or *nauðr* for need, are mnemonics used to memorise the row, the way we spell out "A for Alpha".

Some inscriptions may well be magical, such as protective formulas or curses against grave robbers, but those are the exception. Scholarly handbooks, for example by Düwel and by Barnes, make that distinction clear. For symbol fans, the [Vegvísir](/en/blog/vegvisir-viking-compass/) is worth a look, but it is a late Icelandic sign with only a loose link to the Futhark.

## Myth versus fact: four common mistakes

- **"The Vikings only knew 16 runes because they were primitive."** Wrong. The cut was an adaptation to language and use, not a sign of poor education. Skilled carvers produced texts as complex as the Rök stone.
- **"Every rune has a fixed magical meaning."** No. Rune names are memory aids, not oracle verses, and most inscriptions are plain memorial or ownership notes.
- **"The Younger Futhark replaced the older one overnight."** Also too neat. The change took time, and scholars still argue about how fast it went and where it began.
- **"Runes were only for stone."** Runes were carved on wood, bone and metal as well, but wood rots, which is why stones dominate what survives.

## Conclusion: a small script with a long reach

The Younger Futhark shows that fewer signs do not mean less impact. With 16 runes the Vikings wrote memorial stones, trade notes and greetings, in three different hands, from Denmark to Sweden and far beyond, on journeys as far as Iceland and Greenland. Its imprecision was the price of speed and simplicity. Today it is our problem; back then it was simply a feature of the system.
