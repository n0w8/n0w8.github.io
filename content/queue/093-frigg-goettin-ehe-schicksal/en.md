The **Norse goddess Frigg** knows every fate, according to the Lokasenna, yet never says a word of it, and that single line makes her the most puzzling figure among the gods. Odin pays an eye for wisdom and Thor settles problems with a hammer, while Frigg sits in her hall Fensalir and keeps quiet. Anyone who reduces her to "Odin's wife" and "Baldr's mother" misses how much of the plot in the sources actually hangs on her.

The evidence is thin, though. Frigg has no poem of her own and no myth cycle, and she tends to appear when someone else holds centre stage. This article sorts out what the **Eddas**, Snorri, Saxo and the continental sources really attest, and what later ages read into them.

## Who Is Frigg? Name, Origins and Rank

The name **Frigg** descends from a Germanic root meaning "beloved" or "love". In Old English she is Frig, in Old High German Frija. According to the Lokasenna she is the daughter of **Fjörgyn**, and Snorri calls her the highest of the goddesses in the Gylfaginning, next to Odin. The two share **Hliðskjálf**, the high seat from which all worlds can be seen. That Snorri has her as the Allfather's wife is beyond dispute; her family background he leaves out.

Her home is **Fensalir**, "marsh halls" or "sea halls". The Völuspá has her weeping there for Valhalla's grief after Baldr's death. The sources give no further scenery, which is striking for a goddess of her rank.

She comes with a retinue. Snorri lists several attendants, among them **Fulla**, who carries Frigg's casket and shoes, **Hlín**, who protects those Frigg wants to keep from danger, and **Gná**, who runs errands through air and sea on the horse Hófvarpnir. We would read this as a hint: Frigg's power is not violence but a network of information, care and mediation.

## What Frigg Really Knows About Fate

The famous line comes from a scene that has little to do with dignity. In the **Lokasenna**, the flyting at the feast of the sea giant Ægir, Loki hurls each god's faults at them in turn. When Frigg urges him to leave the past alone, he answers with an accusation that has consequences later, and adds that Frigg knows the fates of all, even if she does not speak them herself. It is mockery from Loki's mouth, but it hits a nerve: Frigg is someone who knows what is coming and keeps it to herself.

That makes her the counterpart to Odin. The Allfather chases knowledge, rides to Niflhel, questions the dead and sacrifices himself (see [Odin: Allfather and Seeker of Wisdom](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/)). Frigg already holds the knowledge and does not say it. Both roads end in catastrophe, by different routes. And then there are the Norns, the proper powers of fate, covered in [The Norns: Weavers of Fate](/en/blog/norns-weavers-of-fate/). The sources never settle how Frigg's knowledge relates to their decrees.

## The Wager over Geirröd: Frigg the Strategist

The best example of Frigg's agency is the prose frame of the **Grímnismál**. Odin and Frigg each have a foster son, **Agnar** and **Geirröd**. As boys the two brothers drifted out to sea and were separated, and Geirröd became a king. Odin claims his ward lives happily, Frigg replies that Geirröd is so miserly he tortures guests when too many arrive.

It is a real wager, and Frigg wins it not with magic but with espionage. She sends her maid **Fulla** to Geirröd to warn him that a sorcerer is coming, recognisable because even the fiercest dog will not attack him. Geirröd believes it and has the silent stranger, who calls himself "Grímnir", tortured between two fires. For eight nights Odin sits there, until young Agnar brings him a drink. Then Odin recites the stanzas of the Grímnismál, reveals himself, and Geirröd falls onto his own sword.

That Frigg outplays Odin here is hard to read any other way. She fights for her ward, deploys her servant and manipulates the laws of hospitality. This is the opposite of the patient wife.

## Frigg's Oaths and the Death of Baldr

The best-known story about Frigg comes from Snorri. Baldr dreams badly, and the gods resolve to protect him. Frigg takes an oath from all things not to harm him, skipping only the mistletoe, which seemed too young to her. Loki finds out by asking in Fensalir, disguised as a woman. The full sequence, including blind **Höðr**, is told in [Baldr: The Death of the Shining God](/en/blog/baldr-god-death-mythology/).

What matters for Frigg's image is the aftermath. She is the one who asks the gods who wants to earn her love and favour by riding to Hel and trying to ransom Baldr. **Hermóðr** volunteers, rides nine nights and bargains with Hel. The condition that all things must weep fails because of the giantess Þökk. And later the Völuspá says Odin's death in the fight against the wolf Fenrir is the second sorrow of **Hlín**, whom scholars usually read as another name for Frigg. Her first sorrow was Baldr.

One can also read the oaths more critically. Frigg secured the world but did not think about the relationship between protection and completeness. In our view the story is above all a lesson on precaution: whoever regulates everything fails at the one gap they judged unimportant.

## Scandal, Mockery and Saxo: The Broken Image

Frigg does not fare well in the sources. In the Lokasenna Loki accuses her of taking Odin's brothers **Vili** and **Vé** as lovers. The Ynglinga saga tells a similar episode: while Odin was away for a long time, his brothers divided his property and his wife between them. How much is myth and how much is mockery cannot be separated. What is certain is that the Icelanders believed their goddess capable of such a story, without embarrassment.

The Danish chronicler **Saxo Grammaticus** is even blunter around 1200. In book one of his *Gesta Danorum* he has "Frigga" seduce a servant to obtain the gold from a statue of Odin, whereupon Odin goes into exile out of shame. Saxo writes from a Christian standpoint, probably with deliberate contempt. He shows how early the storytelling tradition pulled its gods down to a human level.

## Frigg on the Continent: Friday, Frea and the Lombards

Worship of a goddess Frija is not only Nordic. The **Second Merseburg Charm**, recorded in Old High German in the 10th century, names Frija beside Volla, Sunna, Sinthgunt and Wodan while trying to heal a horse's sprained leg. That Volla matches the Norse Fulla is considered likely.

The Lombard writer **Paul the Deacon** tells in the 8th century how the Winnili invoked the gods before a battle against the Vandals. Frea (Frigg) advised that the women should tie their hair around their faces like beards and line up with the men at dawn. Godan saw them, asked who these long-beards were, and granted them victory, so the story goes. Hence the name Lombards. A historically dubious origin myth, but an early witness for a divine couple in which the wife plans more cleverly than the husband.

Then there is Friday. The word goes back to a translation of Latin *dies Veneris*: the Germanic peoples equated their goddess of love with the Roman Venus. Whether Frigg or Freyja was meant is disputed, one reason why the two goddesses are so stubbornly confused (see [Freyja: Goddess of Love and War](/en/blog/freyja-goddess/)). In Sweden a folk name for the three stars of Orion's Belt survived too: "Friggerock", Frigg's distaff.

## Our Assessment: Why Frigg Gets So Little Space

In our view the thin record says more about the sources than about the goddess. The Eddas came out of a Christian-shaped, male-written society that wanted to tell of battle, poetry and revenge. A goddess whose strength lies in restraint offers little material for that. That she still turns up in every decisive scene, the wager, her son's death, the quarrel over the gods' honour, suggests she once held a larger place than the surviving texts show. That is a suspicion, not proof, but it fits the continental evidence.

## FAQ

### Who is Frigg in Norse mythology?

Frigg is Odin's wife and Baldr's mother, according to Snorri the highest of the goddesses. She lives in Fensalir and is said to know fate without speaking of it.

### Are Frigg and Freyja the same goddess?

The sources distinguish them clearly: Freyja is a Vanir goddess, daughter of Njörd, Frigg is Odin's wife. On the continent the figures partly merge, which is why scholars argue over the origin of Friday.

### Why did Frigg not take an oath from the mistletoe?

According to Snorri she thought it too young to demand an oath from. The sources give no further reason.

### What does the name Frigg mean?

It is usually translated as "beloved" or "love" and goes back to a shared Germanic root.

### Are there places or finds linked to Frigg?

Archaeology cannot clearly attest Frigg. Place names and the weekday Friday are hard to assign to her rather than Freyja, so our picture stays confined to texts.
