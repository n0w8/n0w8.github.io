Vikings wove their clothes, their sails and even their money on one and the same machine: the **warp-weighted loom**, an upright frame leaned against a wall, with weights of clay or stone pulling the warp threads taut. No other tool shaped farm life as much as this plain-looking frame, and few are harder to study, because wood and wool rot away and usually only the weights survive. This article explains how the weaving worked, what archaeologists read from the leftovers, and where the sources fall silent.

## How does a warp-weighted loom work?

The design is simpler than it looks. Two upright posts lean at a slant against a wall, joined at the top by a horizontal cloth beam. From this beam the warp threads, the lengthwise threads of the future cloth, hang down in small bundles. Each bundle is tied at the bottom to a **loom weight** that keeps the thread under tension. Because gravity does the tensioning, the loom needs no fixed bottom frame like later looms.

Weaving proceeds from the top down. The weaver stands or sits in front of the frame, passes the weft through the warp and beats it upward against the finished cloth. The tool for this is the **weaving sword**, a flat, often tapering batten of wood or bone, also called a beater. A shed rod and a heddle rod split the warp into two sets, opening a gap for the weft to run through. As work advances, the finished cloth is rolled onto the cloth beam and the weaver works her way down.

A typical feature is the starting border. On this type of loom a tablet-woven band often forms the first edge. Such bands are documented in finds, which shows that tablet weaving and the warp-weighted loom worked hand in hand.

## Loom weights: What the finds leave behind

The frame rots. What archaeologists find are the weights, and these are among the most common finds on Viking Age farm sites. They are made of fired clay, stone or other materials, usually perforated, and weigh several hundred grams. Where they turn up in rows, for example in a burnt layer, they probably lay where the loom collapsed in a fire, and so they indicate the width of the loom.

Alongside them come **spindle whorls**, small round discs that give a hand spindle its momentum. They appear in great numbers in settlements such as [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/) and in graves. Whorls, weaving swords and loom weights also occur in women's graves, which fits what the sagas suggest about the division of labour: textile work lay mostly with women. Some caution is needed, though. A grave good shows what people wished to send along, not necessarily who sat at the loom every day.

Buildings give clues too. In several Viking Age settlements, small sunken huts known as sunken-featured buildings contained loom weights. It is often suggested that the damp, cool air near the ground helped the yarn, but the intention behind it is not firmly proven.

## From sheep to cloth: a long road

Anyone who says cloth means a chain of steps that could each cost days. The table sums them up.

| Step | What happened | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| Shearing or plucking | Gather wool, in some sheep breeds by plucking in spring | Shears, hands |
| Cleaning and combing | Remove grease, dirt and coarse hairs | Wool comb |
| Dyeing | Colour the yarn with plant dyes | Kettle, dye plants |
| Spinning | Twist fibre into thread | Hand spindle, spindle whorl |
| Weaving | Turn thread into cloth | Warp-weighted loom, weaving sword |
| Fulling and sewing | Condense the cloth, cut and sew | Needle, shears |

Spinning was the real bottleneck. A hand spindle produces thread slowly, and a single piece of cloth needs enormous quantities of it. Whoever held a piece of cloth in a settlement held several weeks of work in their hands. That explains why clothes were mended, handed down and re-sewn.

Dyeing used plants. Woad for blue and madder for red are proven in textile finds, and yellows from dye plants and lichens are possible too. Rich colour signalled wealth; more on appearance and ornament is in the article on [Viking clothing and jewelry](/en/blog/viking-clothing-jewelry/).

## Threads, patterns and twill

The surviving textile remains, for instance from Birka, Hedeby and York, show that wool was the main material. Linen was used as well, mainly for underclothing. Many woollen fabrics are woven in twill, where the weft passes over and under several warp threads in an offset order and forms a diagonal pattern. Twill is denser and more elastic than plain weave, which suited cloaks and weatherproof clothing.

It would be a mistake to imagine nothing but grey rags. Patterned bands, coloured stripes and elaborate weaves are attested, and fragments of tapestries from the Oseberg burial hint at how ambitious textile art could be. What most of these pieces looked like when complete remains reconstruction.

## Weaving as currency: Icelandic vaðmál

A cloth woven on this loom became money in Iceland: **vaðmál**, a coarse woollen twill. Iceland had few raw materials for coins but plenty of sheep. The medieval law code Grágás fixed prices in ells of vaðmál, and according to the tradition six ells equalled one ounce of silver, the eyrir. Vaðmál thus served as a measure of value and a means of payment, and it was exported as well.

That matters for economic history. In a world where silver was needed abroad but scarce at home, the women of a farm literally earned money at the loom. Part of the prosperity of Icelandic farms hung on this work, which the sources rarely honour. How silver moved in the wider picture is described in the article on [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/).

> Editorially speaking: anyone who wants to know who carried the Viking Age economically should look less at swords and more at loom weights. Cloth was clothing, capital and export good at once.

## Sails of wool: the biggest weaving project

The biggest textile project was the sail. By current understanding, Viking sails were mostly wool, though linen as an alternative is debated among researchers. The sail of a large ship meant a huge amount of woven cloth, and experimental archaeology, for instance in ship reconstructions around the Viking Ship Museum in Roskilde, reckons with a very large effort in raw material and time. Exact figures vary between reconstructions, so they are deliberately left out here.

The thought is impressive nonetheless: behind a [longship](/en/blog/the-longship/) stood not only carpentry but also the winter work of many weavers. Sailcloth had to be dense and sturdy, which suggests a deliberate choice of weave. Details of finishing are poorly documented.

## Weaving in myth: Norns and Valkyries

That weaving was more than a craft shows in mythology. The Norns weave the fates of humans, as the piece on the [Norns as weavers of fate](/en/blog/norns-weavers-of-fate/) explains. Even more graphic is the **Darraðarljóð**, a poem inside Njáls saga. Before the Battle of Clontarf in 1014, men see twelve women at a loom whose weights are men's heads, whose threads are intestines, whose sword serves as the beater and whose arrows are the heddle rods. They are Valkyries deciding the outcome of the fight.

The image only works because every listener knew the parts of a loom. It also shows how familiar the device was: its mechanics could be turned into a vision of war.

## Myth or fact: three misconceptions about weaving

- **Myth: Vikings wore only coarse, grey clothing.** Fact: woad, madder and patterned bands are documented, and fine cloth was a status symbol.
- **Myth: sails were made elsewhere by specialists.** Fact: current research suggests the farm itself carried much of the work, although the sources are thin.
- **Myth: the warp-weighted loom was primitive.** Fact: it is a mature device that could weave complex patterns such as twill, and in parts of Scandinavia and the Atlantic islands it stayed in use for a long time.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a warp-weighted loom?

An upright loom in which weights of clay or stone hold the warp threads taut. Cloth is woven from the top down and rolled onto the cloth beam.

### Who did the weaving in the Viking Age?

According to sagas and grave finds, mostly women. Weaving was part of household work, though exceptions are conceivable and the sources give no complete picture.

### What were Viking textiles made of?

Mainly wool, along with linen and occasionally silk arriving via trade routes. Wool was the everyday material.

### What was vaðmál?

A coarse Icelandic woollen cloth used as a unit for pricing. According to the law code Grágás, six ells of vaðmál equalled one ounce of silver.

## Conclusion

The warp-weighted loom was the quiet centre of the farm. It supplied clothing, the cloth for sails and, in Iceland, even the currency. What we see today are clay weights in museum cases, yet each one was part of work that filled many months of the year. To understand the Vikings, follow them not only onto the ships but also to the loom.
