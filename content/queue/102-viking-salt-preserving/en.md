Vikings preserved food mainly by **drying, smoking, souring and salting**, and their dried fish was so successful that cod from Arctic Norway reached Hedeby in northern Germany between the 8th and 11th centuries. That is the finding of a 2017 ancient DNA study of cod bones from Hedeby, led by Bastiaan Star. This article explains which preservation methods are well documented, which are educated guesses, and where the popular claims fall apart.

## Why preservation decided who ate in February

The north has long winters. A household that had not stored enough by October faced empty chests by February. There were no tins and no fridges, so the whole year was planned around what could be kept. In the Old Icelandic calendar, the month running from mid-October to mid-November is called **Gormánuður**, usually translated as "slaughter month". A month named after slaughter shows how central that moment was. For the wider picture of the farming year, see our overview of [Viking daily life](/en/blog/viking-daily-life/).

The core problem is simple. Fresh meat and fish spoil within days. Grain and legumes keep if they stay dry. Milk is undrinkable after a few days unless processed. Every method pulls the same lever: remove the **water**, make the food hostile to bacteria with acid, smoke or salt, or combine these.

## Stockfish: the north's export product

The best-documented trail leads to cod. Gutted fish is dried in cold wind without salt, a product still called **stockfish** in Norway. The Old Norse word for dried fish is *skreið*, related to Norwegian *skrei*, the migrating winter cod. Climate is the key: in spring the Lofoten Islands sit just above freezing, dry and windy, and flies are not yet active. The fish dries instead of rotting.

The 2017 study matters because it shows a trade that barely appears in written sources. Researchers analysed cod bones from Hedeby dating from the 8th to the 11th century. A large share did not come from the North or Baltic Sea but from the Arctic cod population, from the Barents Sea and northern Norwegian fishing grounds. Combined with bone patterns that point to already **processed** fish, this supports dried fish as a trade good travelling more than 1,500 kilometres as the crow flies.

How large the share was, and when regular trade began, is still debated. Earlier work on fish bones from English towns finds a clear shift around the year 1000, with sea fish becoming much more common, and preserved fish plausibly played a part. That is an interpretation, not proof for every town. For the trading places themselves, read about [Hedeby](/en/blog/hedeby-lost-trading-city/).

> Moving fish 1,500 kilometres needs no cold chain, only wind, cold and patience.

Our assessment: stockfish is the underrated counterpart of silver. Coins and hack-silver get most of the attention, while what merchants actually loaded into their ships gets far less. For a society living from the sea, preserved fish was probably worth at least as much as many a piece of jewellery.

## Smoke in the longhouse: the roof as smokehouse

The second big method happened almost by itself. A longhouse had a central hearth and no chimney in the modern sense; smoke drifted up through the roof. Whatever hung from the rafters was smoked over weeks. This is plausible and widely assumed in research on house interiors, though hanging hams naturally do not survive archaeologically. Meat, fish and sausages could be preserved this way at little extra cost.

Smoke works twice: it dries the surface and deposits compounds that slow bacteria and mould. For a practical version, see our [smoked fish recipe in Viking style](/en/blog/viking-smoked-fish-recipe/). The point for daily life: smoke was a by-product of warmth. Anyone who heated in winter preserved automatically.

## Salt: a scarce resource

Salt is the method people most want to talk about, and the one with the most question marks. The north has no salt lakes. In cool regions sea salt forms only slowly because the sun barely drives evaporation, so seawater must be boiled down using a lot of fuel. That was possible and is well attested for later centuries on northern coasts, but quantities for the Viking Age itself are unclear.

One well-known inland source is the Lüneburg saltworks, whose production is mentioned in a 956 charter of Otto I. That is a near-contemporary sign that large amounts of salt were produced close to the Viking trading world. Whether and how much went to Scandinavia is not firmly established. What we can say: salt was valuable, and sparing use is more likely than generous curing of every delivery of meat. For the scale of long-distance trade, see [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/).

A summary of the methods, with how strong the evidence is:

| Method | Preserves | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Air drying (stockfish) | Fish, especially cod | Strong: bones from Hedeby, 2017 DNA study |
| Smoking at the hearth | Meat, fish, sausages | Plausible: longhouse construction |
| Salting | Meat, fish | Weak to medium: salt was scarce |
| Souring in whey | Meat, dairy | Mostly later Icelandic tradition |
| Burial in bogs | Butter | Documented by bog butter finds |
| Drying grain and beans | Barley, oats, fava beans | Strong: finds in settlements |

## Souring and burying: whey and bog butter

Milk was the second preservation problem, and acid was the answer. Cream became butter, soured milk became skyr and cheese, and whey was left as sour whey. In **Icelandic tradition**, sour whey (*sýra*) is known well into modern times as a preserving liquid for meat. Whether it was used on that scale in the Viking Age cannot be said for certain. The principle, using acid to slow spoilage organisms, is as old as dairying itself, and our overview of [Viking food and diet](/en/blog/viking-food-diet/) touches on it too.

Then there is **bog butter**. In bogs in Ireland and Scotland, and also in Scandinavia, lumps of butter or tallow have been found, often in wooden kegs or bark containers. They date from many periods, many from the Middle Ages. The obvious explanation, though not settled, is that the cool, airless, acidic bog works as a natural cellar. Some may also have been buried to hide it or to mature it. Either way, people used the landscape as storage.

## Myth: "Vikings spiced rotten meat"

A perennial claim online says medieval cooks covered up spoiled meat with spices. For the Viking Age there is no evidence, and the logic argues against it. Pepper was rare and costly, and people with little had no money for it. Anyone who stored meat safely with smoke, drying or acid needed no cover-up. And safety was the goal of every method anyway: a sick farmhand was a lost worker in winter. We rate the claim as a modern anecdote. What is certain is that herbs, onions and wild plants improved flavour.

## What survives of Viking Age knowledge?

In Lofoten, stockfish still hangs on wooden racks every spring, and in Iceland sour whey is still served at festive meals. The methods survived because they work. If you want to try one, start with smoking fish, where practice most readily pays off. And for some atmosphere from the hearth:

## FAQ

### How did Vikings preserve meat?

Mainly by smoking at the hearth, air drying and, to some extent, salting. Sour whey appears in later Icelandic tradition. How widespread each method was in the Viking Age can only partly be shown.

### What is stockfish and how old is it?

Stockfish is unsalted cod dried in the open air. Bones from Hedeby show that such fish from the far north was already traded to Schleswig between the 8th and 11th centuries.

### Did Vikings have enough salt?

Probably not in abundance. Salt had to be produced laboriously or traded in, and quantities for the Viking Age are unclear. Drying and smoking therefore often took its place.

### What is bog butter?

Bog butter is butter or tallow found in peat bogs. It comes from many periods, including the Middle Ages. The cool, oxygen-poor bog acted as a natural cellar.

## Conclusion

Vikings preserved food with what the north had in plenty: cold, wind, smoke and acid. Salt was the exception, not the rule. The most surprising result remains Arctic cod in Hedeby, which shows that preserving was not only prudence but also business. That is our reading of the finds, not a consensus of all researchers.
