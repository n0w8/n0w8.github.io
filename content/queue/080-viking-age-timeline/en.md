The **Viking Age** lasted roughly 270 years by the usual count: it starts with the raid on Lindisfarne monastery on 8 June 793 and ends with the Battle of Stamford Bridge in 1066. Both dates are convention, not natural law. Archaeologists find Viking ships and long-distance trade before 793, and some scholars let the age run until Scandinavia's Christianisation in the 12th century. As a frame it still works, and this timeline sorts the main turning points.

## When Did the Viking Age Start? Lindisfarne 793 and What Came Before

The raid on **Lindisfarne** off England's north-east coast is the date textbooks give, because it appears in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and in a letter by the scholar Alcuin, who wrote that nothing like it had been seen in Britain. The full story is in our article on the [Lindisfarne raid](/en/blog/lindisfarne-raid/).

The departure was long in the making. Trading places such as Ribe in Denmark (founded around 710) and Birka in Sweden (mid-8th century) show that Scandinavians were already moving silver, furs and glass beads over long distances. How their ships looked is covered in [the longship](/en/blog/the-longship/).

Some years are firmly documented, others come from later sagas. Where sources give no fixed number we wrote "c.".

## The Early Phase, 793 to 850: Raids and First Bases

At first small groups struck fast and vanished. Monasteries were attractive because they held silver and shrines but little defence. After 830 the voyages grew larger. On the Continent trading centres such as Dorestad were hit, and in 845 an army stood before Paris. The payment of 7,000 pounds of silver was a forerunner of [Danegeld](/en/blog/danegeld-vikings-tribute/), and it shows a lesson the Vikings drew: violence paid.

In Ireland they founded fortified ship camps from the 840s, and cities such as Dublin grew out of them. So a second track emerged early next to the raids: settlement and trade.

What did that mean for ordinary people? Besides warriors and kings there were farmers, craftsmen, merchants and the unfree. Most of the population ploughed, fished and traded instead of raiding. How far the routes reached is shown in our piece on [Viking trade routes](/en/blog/viking-trade-routes/): they ran along rivers to the Black and Caspian Seas and brought silver, silk and glass north.

## Conquest and Settlement: 865 to 911

With the **Great Heathen Army** the character of the voyages changed. The army overwintered, took York in 867 and seized East Anglia, Northumbria and much of Mercia. Only Wessex held, under Alfred the Great, who won at Edington in 878. The course is described in the article on the [Great Heathen Army](/en/blog/great-heathen-army/); leaders such as [Ivar the Boneless](/en/blog/ivar-the-boneless-viking-history/) are known mainly from sagas. The result was the Danelaw, an area with its own law where place names ending in -by and -thorpe still recall the settlers.

In 911 the West Frankish king Charles the Simple granted the Viking leader Rollo land at the mouth of the Seine. That became Normandy. A century later its inhabitants, descendants of Vikings, spoke French, and in 1066 they would conquer England.

## The Atlantic Age: Iceland, Greenland, Vinland

In parallel, settlers moved west. According to the Landnámabók, Iceland was settled from about 874, and around 930 the Althing first met there, one of Europe's oldest assemblies. More in our piece on [the Viking thing assembly](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly-law/). Erik the Red founded settlements in Greenland in 986, and his son **Leif Eriksson** reached Vinland around the year 1000. The archaeological proof, L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, is presented in our article on [Vikings in America](/en/blog/lanse-aux-meadows-vikings-america/).

## Kingdoms and Christianisation: 950 to 1050

In Scandinavia itself, kingdoms emerged. Harald Bluetooth was baptised around 965 and celebrated it on the Jelling stones, as covered in [the Jelling stones](/en/blog/jelling-stones-denmark/). Around 980 the Danish ring fortresses were built. In 1013 Sweyn Forkbeard conquered England, and in 1016 his son Cnut the Great became English king, later also king of Denmark and Norway. Iceland accepted Christianity by assembly decision around the year 1000, and Norway followed under Olaf the Holy, who fell at Stiklestad in 1030.

In the east, Varangians served the Byzantine emperor; the guard existed from about 988. How that worked is told in the article on the [Varangian Guard](/en/blog/varangian-guard-byzantium/).

## The Timeline in Finds: What Archaeology Dates

Written sources cover only snippets; archaeology fills the gaps. The burial chamber of the Oseberg ship has been dated by tree rings to 834, in the early phase. The Gokstad ship belongs to about 900, and the Skuldelev ships to the 11th century, when Danish kings were long Christian. To compare ship types, see our articles on the [Oseberg ship](/en/blog/oseberg-ship/), the [Gokstad ship](/en/blog/gokstad-ship/) and the [Skuldelev ships](/en/blog/skuldelev-ships-roskilde/).

A second trace is the silver hoards. Arab dirhams that reached Scandinavia show how far trade extended east, and their dwindling in the late 10th century marks a shift in trade routes. We trust coin finds more than some sagas, because coins are hard to fake when it comes to dates.

What this means for the timeline: the picture of the "Viking Age" is a compression. Certain years stand out because someone wrote them down, a monk in England or a chronicler at the Frankish court. Contemporary texts from Scandinavia itself are almost absent, apart from rune stones and skaldic verse. Every timeline is therefore a construct seen through foreign eyes.

## 1066: Why the End Fits So Well

In September 1066 Harald Hardrada landed in northern England and fell on 25 September at Stamford Bridge against Harold Godwinson. Three weeks later, on 14 October, Harold lost at Hastings to William of Normandy, a descendant of Rollo. The age ends symbolically: the last great Norse attempt at conquest failed, and a descendant of Northmen ruled England, now as a Norman. The battle is covered in our article on [Stamford Bridge 1066](/en/blog/battle-of-stamford-bridge/).

## Our Take: How Useful Is the Timeline?

We see 793 and 1066 as a help, not as truth. They are England-centred, since both dates concern the British Isles. From a Scandinavian view the age ends rather with Christianisation and the building of stable kingdoms. To remember what really matters, hold three developments in mind: from raid to settlement, from chieftains to kings, from the old faith to Christianity.

## FAQ

### When was the Viking Age?
The usual span is 793 to 1066, about 270 years. In Scandinavia it runs into the 12th century, depending on definition.

### When exactly did the Viking Age begin?
By convention on 8 June 793, with the raid on Lindisfarne. Trade and seafaring existed earlier.

### Why does the Viking Age end in 1066?
Because Stamford Bridge is seen as the last great Viking attempt to conquer England. The Norman conquest followed soon after.

### Which Viking kings matter most?
Among others Harald Bluetooth, Cnut the Great and Harald Hardrada. From the early period, army leaders like Ivar the Boneless are handed down in sagas but only partly documented.

## Conclusion

The Viking Age was not one raid but a long change from voyages to kingdoms. To go deeper, start at Lindisfarne and end at Stamford Bridge; between lie conquest, trade and discovery.
