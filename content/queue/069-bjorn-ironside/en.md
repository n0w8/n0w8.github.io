Thanks to a well-known television series, almost everyone today recognizes the name **Bjorn Ironside**. What fewer people know is that the historical trail behind this figure is thinner, older, and stranger than any script. Between Icelandic sagas, a Norman chronicle, and genuine Frankish accounts of a bloody raid into the Mediterranean, a picture emerges that is neither pure legend nor settled history, but sits uncomfortably between the two. Understanding where Bjorn, son of Ragnar Lothbrok, ends and where a historical leader named Bjorn begins requires keeping the sources apart rather than blending them.

## The Son of Ragnar Lothbrok: What the Sagas Say

The main source for Bjorn's family background is the short Icelandic text **Ragnarssona thattr**, "The Tale of Ragnar's Sons," supplemented by details in Saxo Grammaticus's Latin **Gesta Danorum** from the early 13th century. Both credit Bjorn as one of Ragnar Lothbrok's sons, alongside better-known brothers such as Ivar the Boneless and Sigurd Snake-in-the-Eye, whom we covered in detail in our article on [Ragnar Lothbrok](/en/blog/ragnar-lothbrok-legend-truth).

The nickname **Jarnsida**, "Ironside," is never explained in the sagas themselves, which leaves room for speculation: tough armor, a reputation for being unwoundable in battle, or simply a nickname that outlived its original meaning. It's worth staying honest here: Ragnarssona thattr was written down in the 14th century, three to four hundred years after the events it claims to describe. As a strict historical source it is thin, but as a record of an oral heroic tradition it still carries real weight.

## Revenge for Ragnar: The Campaign Against King Aelle

According to the Ragnarssona thattr, Bjorn's own story really begins with an act of revenge. Ragnar Lothbrok is said to have died in a pit of snakes at the court of the Northumbrian king **Aelle**. His sons, Bjorn, Ivar, and Sigurd among them, supposedly then led an army to England together to avenge that death. This episode is usually linked to the historically well-documented **Great Heathen Army**, which invaded England starting in 865 and conquered several Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. While the existence of that army is beyond doubt, confirmed by the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Bjorn's specific role in it remains pure saga tradition with no outside confirmation. Several historians suspect that Bjorn, unlike Ivar, may not have held a leading role in the England campaign at all, and was instead already occupied with ventures of his own heading south.

## The Mediterranean Raid: Bjorn, Hastein, and the Year 859

This is where things get interesting, because here the saga tradition actually brushes up against contemporary, checkable history. The Norman monk and chronicler **Dudo of Saint-Quentin**, writing around the year 1000 in his history of the Normans, describes a leader called **Bier Costae Ferreae**, Latin for "Bjorn Ironside," who commanded a large fleet together with another leader named **Hastein**.

What that fleet did is actually documented in several independent Frankish sources, even if they don't always name the leaders directly. Starting around 859 AD, a Viking fleet worked its way down the Iberian coast, raided territory in Al-Andalus, passed through the Strait of Gibraltar, struck targets on the North African coast, and eventually pushed up the Rhone deep into what is now Italy. There, the fleet sacked the cities of **Pisa** and **Luna**, the latter according to a well-known, if clearly embellished, tradition because the raiders believed they had reached Rome itself.

The return was no triumphant homecoming. Contemporary sources such as the **Annales Bertiniani** (Annals of St-Bertin) suggest the fleet came back severely weakened by storms, disease, and combat losses along the way. Whether the "Bier" named by Dudo of Saint-Quentin is truly the same person as the saga's Bjorn cannot be proven, but the match in timing and geography is striking enough that scholars take the connection seriously.

We actually know considerably more about Bjorn's co-leader Hastein, which sheds additional light on how credible the whole episode is. Frankish sources such as the Annals of St-Bertin trace Hastein's activities for decades after the Mediterranean campaign, especially along the Loire, where he repeatedly raided monasteries and towns through the 860s and 870s and at times maintained a fortified camp of his own. That Hastein is so well documented as an individual indirectly supports the existence of his supposed co-leader Bjorn, even though Bjorn's own path after returning from the Mediterranean disappears from the Frankish record entirely.

## Munso: The Supposed Ancestor of Swedish Kings

A second trail leads to Bjorn, this time to Sweden. Medieval Swedish king-lists, including material in the **Hervarar saga**, name a Bjorn as the founder of a ruling line based on the island of **Munso** in Lake Malaren, not far from the later royal town of Sigtuna. This so-called **House of Munso** was treated by older scholarship as the earliest somewhat tangible Swedish royal line, though modern historians have grown increasingly skeptical about tying it to one single, concrete Bjorn.

Our assessment after weighing the sources: it is quite possible that two separate narrative traditions, a Danish Ragnarsson saga and a Swedish royal-line tradition, were later fused into a single figure simply because the name and title fit. That was common practice in medieval Iceland, where most of these texts were written down: scattered oral tradition got compressed into one coherent genealogy.

The Hervarar saga names Bjorn's successor on Munso as a son called **Erik**, from whom later, markedly more legendary kings are then derived. The further back these king-lists reach, the shakier the evidence becomes, a pattern found in nearly every early medieval royal genealogy in Northern Europe: the more recent, better-attested links in the chain count as relatively reliable, while the older links increasingly serve legitimation rather than historical record.

## Saga, Chronicle, and TV Show: Three Different Bjorns

It is worth keeping three levels clearly apart, because public perception constantly blurs them together.

- **The saga Bjorn**: son of Ragnar Lothbrok, brother of Ivar and Sigurd, part of an oral heroic tradition written down centuries later.
- **The historical Bjorn** (if identical with Dudo's "Bier"): co-leader of a documented raid into the Mediterranean from 859 to 862, attested in Frankish and Norman sources from his own era or shortly after.
- **The television Bjorn**: the lead character of the series "Vikings," a free dramatic invention that compresses and alters events, relationships, and timelines heavily to carry a character across multiple seasons.

Only the middle level can be anchored to genuine, contemporary documents. The first is tradition with a kernel of truth, the third is entertainment. For more on the wider historical backdrop of this Viking family, see our article on the [Great Heathen Army](/en/blog/great-heathen-army), which Bjorn's brothers led in force.

The television series in particular has shaped public perception so strongly that many viewers no longer recognize its dramatic liberties as liberties at all. Bjorn's relationship with his brothers, his bond with Ragnar, even the sequence of historical events were rearranged drastically for the show and concentrated onto a handful of lead characters who appear together across decades, even though the sagas themselves often suggest far more scattered, internally contradictory timeframes. That is not a knock against the production, drama runs on compression, but it does explain why checking the primary sources tends to come as a genuine surprise to most viewers.

## Why Bjorn Ironside Still Fascinates

The pull of this figure lies exactly in that ambiguity. Bjorn is not a cleanly documented king like later rulers attested by runestones and coins, but a figure who drifts between heroic saga, chronicle, and modern popular culture. That makes him an almost ideal example of how the Viking Age as a whole has come down to us: fragmentary, arriving from several directions at once, with real historical grains buried deep inside legendary material.

In the end, an honest answer is less satisfying than an invented certainty, but far more useful: a man named Bjorn, called Ironside, almost certainly existed, a raid under that name or something close to it almost certainly happened, but how much of Ragnar's son, Munso's founder, and the leader outside Pisa were actually one and the same person is something today's sources can no longer settle for good.

That very uncertainty is what makes Bjorn worth studying, not despite it but because of it. Anyone who spends time with the Viking Age learns something fundamental about the field itself from a figure like him: medieval history rarely consists of clean, settled facts, it consists of layers of tradition that support each other, contradict each other, and occasionally just invent things to round off a good story. Bjorn Ironside stands in for dozens of similar Viking Age figures whose names we know, whose deeds we can only guess at, and whose actual lives remain forever lodged in the gap between saga and stone.
