**Skadi** is the Norse goddess of hunting and winter, and according to Snorri Sturluson she marched into Asgard in full armor to avenge her father, then walked away with a husband of her own choosing and, to her own surprise, a laugh. That story, preserved in the thirteenth-century Prose Edda, is the longest text we have about her, and it is stranger than the "ski goddess" label suggests.

Skadi is not an Æsir goddess by birth. She is a giantess (Old Norse *jötunn*), and she still ends up among the gods. How that happens, and which parts come from Snorri's storytelling rather than older sources, is what this article sorts out.

## Who was Skadi? The sources

Skaði is named in several Eddic poems, but only one source tells her story at length.

| Source | Date | What it says about Skadi |
|---|---|---|
| Grímnismál (Poetic Edda) | manuscript 13th c. | Names Þrymheimr, her father Þjazi's home, which she later inhabits |
| Lokasenna (Poetic Edda) | manuscript 13th c. | Skadi appears in the gods' quarrel; Loki boasts of killing her father |
| Gylfaginning (Prose Edda) | c. 1220 | Her marriage to Njord and the split between homes |
| Skáldskaparmál (Prose Edda) | c. 1220 | Þjazi's death, her demand for compensation, her titles as ski and hunting goddess |
| Ynglinga saga | c. 1225 | Odin as father of Skadi's son Sæmingr |

Mind the gap in time. Every detailed text was written down by Christian authors long after the conversion of Iceland. How much is pre-Christian belief cannot be measured, and anyone who gives you a detailed "original Skadi religion" is inventing it.

## The avenger: why Skadi came to Asgard

The backstory is a revenge case. Her father Þjazi (Thiazi) was a giant who forced Loki to lure the goddess Idun and her apples of youth out of Asgard. The gods began to age until they compelled Loki to bring Idun back. Þjazi chased them in eagle shape, flew into the fire the Æsir had lit along the walls, burned his feathers, and was killed.

Skadi put on helmet and mail, took all her weapons, and went to Asgard to demand vengeance. Notice what Snorri does here: he does not describe a woman waiting for a male relative to act. She acts herself. The Æsir offered settlement and compensation: a husband of her choice among the gods, with one condition. She could only see the men's feet and had to choose by those. The gods also promised to make her laugh, which she thought impossible in her mood.

This is a legal procedure told as myth. Our article on [Viking law](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly-law/) shows how killings were settled by compensation and agreement. Skadi's negotiation follows the same pattern of claim, settlement and satisfaction.

## Choosing by feet: Njord instead of Baldr

Skadi picked the most beautiful pair of feet and assumed they belonged to radiant Baldr. They were Njord's, the god of sea and coast, whom we introduce in [Njord, the sea god](/en/blog/njord-norse-god-sea/). The joke is intended: the giantess who must judge by feet guesses wrong and gets the sea god instead of the shining one.

Loki made her laugh. He tied a cord to the beard of a goat and the other end to himself, and both shrieked and pulled until he fell into her lap. A dry irony: the god who got Skadi to laugh was the one who had brought about her father's death.

Afterwards, says Snorri, Odin took Þjazi's eyes and cast them into the sky as stars, one more act of amends that rounds off the story.

## Mountains or sea: the failed marriage

The couple could not agree where to live. Skadi wanted Þrymheimr, her father's mountain hall; Njord wanted Nóatún by the sea. The compromise was nine nights in the mountains, then three by the sea. Njord said he hated the mountains and the howling of wolves; he preferred the song of the swans. Skadi replied that she could not sleep by the sea because the gulls woke her every morning.

Snorri ends the scene with a separation. Skadi returned to Þrymheimr, went about on skis (*á skíðum*), hunted with her bow and killed animals. That is where her titles **öndurgoð** and **öndurdís**, "ski god" and "ski goddess", come from. At its core the quarrel contrasts two Nordic living spaces: the sailor's coast and the hunter's mountains.

The world of giants is a constant presence in the Eddas, as we describe in [the Jotnar, giants of Norse mythology](/en/blog/jotnar-giants-norse-mythology/).

## Skadi and Odin: Sæming and the earls of Hlaðir

After leaving Njord, Skadi is said in Ynglinga saga to have had many sons with Odin. One is named: Sæmingr. Skaldic poetry treats him as ancestor of the earls of Hlaðir (Lade) in Norway. The poem Háleygjatal, composed by Eyvindr skáldaspillir for Earl Hákon, traces his family back to Odin and Skadi.

That is revealing. Divine descent gave a northern ruler political weight, and descent from Skadi also signaled a claim to the mountain and fjord land of northwest Norway. The genealogy is political poetry, not religious doctrine. For the wider family tree see [the Norse gods and their family tree](/en/blog/norse-gods-family-tree/).

## Skadi in Lokasenna: she punishes Loki

In Lokasenna ("Loki's Quarrel") Loki insults everyone at the feast of the sea giant Ægir. When Skadi speaks, Loki retorts that he helped kill her father, and claims he shared her bed. The gods intervene. At the end Loki is bound, and the prose ending tells how Skadi hung a venomous snake above his face so the poison dripped on him. His wife Sigyn catches the venom in a bowl.

So the goddess of revenge finally gets her satisfaction. It makes a narrative arc: from Þjazi's death, which Loki caused, to Loki's punishment, which Skadi carries out. Whether the poets built the arc deliberately we cannot know, but it holds together.

## Traces in the land: place names

Place names suggest Skadi was more than a literary figure. In Sweden, Skedevi in Östergötland is read as "sanctuary of Skadi", and there are several Skade- names in Norway and Sweden. Such readings are common in name research but not undisputed, because place names often allow several explanations. A famous related claim: the word "Scandinavia" goes back, on one theory, to Proto-Germanic *Skaðinawjō, possibly "Skadi's island". The etymology is contested, and the link to the goddess is possible but unproven.

## Our assessment

Skadi unites two things the Edda rarely combines: outsider and member. As a giantess she comes from the world that threatens the gods, yet a legal settlement binds her in. That says a lot about how Norse people handled conflict: with compensation instead of the sword, when possible. We read the tale less as fairy tale than as a legal parable with humor, and that is what makes it worth reading.

## FAQ

### Is Skadi a goddess or a giantess?

Both. She is the daughter of the giant Þjazi, so a jötunn, but she is taken into the circle of the gods by her marriage to Njord and counted as a goddess (*ásynja*).

### What is Skadi the goddess of?

The sources call her a ski goddess and hunter. She lives in the mountains, hunts with a bow, and stands for winter. A tidy list of "domains" is a modern grid; the sources do not work that way.

### Who was Skadi married to?

Njord, god of the sea. The marriage failed over their different ways of life. Ynglinga saga later credits her with children by Odin, among them Sæmingr.

### What is Skadi's connection to Loki?

Loki took part in her father's death, later made her laugh, and was punished by her in Lokasenna when she hung a venomous snake over him.

### Was Skadi actually worshipped?

Place names such as Skedevi suggest it, but cult practice is not securely attested. The texts come from the thirteenth century.
