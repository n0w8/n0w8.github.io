**Mimir's Well** cost Odin an eye: he left it as a pledge for a single drink from the spring of wisdom, and according to the Völuspá it lies there still. That is the shortest version of one of Norse mythology's most famous sacrifices, and it comes from a poem written down by the thirteenth century at the latest. Everything else, from the talking head to the drinking horn, is scattered across several sources and sometimes contradicts itself.

Who is Mimir, where is the well, and what does the pledge mean? This article separates what the texts give us from what later readers added.

## The sources on Mimir at a glance

| Source | What it says about Mimir and the well |
|---|---|
| Völuspá, st. 27-28 | Odin's eye is hidden in the "famous well of Mimir"; Mimir drinks mead every morning from Odin's pledge |
| Völuspá, st. 46 | At the start of Ragnarök, Odin consults Mimir's head |
| Gylfaginning (Snorri) | The well lies under the second root of Yggdrasil; Odin gives his eye for a drink |
| Ynglinga saga (Snorri) | Mimir is sent as a hostage to the Vanir and beheaded; Odin keeps the head |
| Sigrdrífumál | Mentions Mimir's head in connection with rune knowledge |

The key point: there are two different stories at the core, and Snorri sets them side by side without reconciling them. One is about the well and the eye, the other about the head. Whether they originally belonged together is open.

## Where is Mimir's Well? The roots of Yggdrasil

In Snorri's Gylfaginning the world tree has three roots. One reaches the Æsir, one the frost giants, where Ginnungagap once was, and the third Niflheim. Under the root among the giants lies **Mímisbrunnr**, in which wisdom and understanding are hidden. Its owner Mímir is full of knowledge because he drinks from the well every day, using the Gjallarhorn.

For the tree itself, see our article on [Yggdrasil, the world tree](/en/blog/yggdrasil-world-tree/). In Fjölsvinnsmál the tree is even called Mímameiðr, "Mimir's tree", which underlines the close link.

A detail often overlooked: the well lies among the giants, not the gods. Wisdom belongs to a power outside Asgard, and Odin has to buy it. That fits a pattern in the Edda: knowledge comes from outside, and it is not inherited.

## The eye as a pledge: what Odin really paid

Snorri says Odin came to the well and asked for a drink. He did not get it for free: he had to put his eye down as a pledge. The Völuspá confirms it more tersely: Odin's eye is hidden in the well, and Mimir drinks mead every morning "from the pledge of the Father of the Slain".

Note the wording. A pledge is something you can get back. Odin has not lost his eye, he has deposited it. This is legal language, and it fits the role of contract, pledge and settlement in Norse law, which we describe in [Viking law and the Thing](/en/blog/viking-thing-assembly-law/).

A common mistake: that Odin gave the eye at the tree when he hanged himself on the windy tree to win the runes. That is another story, told in Hávamál (st. 138-141): nine nights he hangs wounded, pierced by a spear, and gains the runes. The eye is not mentioned there. Popular retellings often merge the two sacrifices. More on Odin's hunger for knowledge in [Odin, the Allfather](/en/blog/odin-allfather-wisdom/).

## Who was Mimir? The head that kept speaking

The second story is in Snorri's Ynglinga saga. In the war between the Æsir and the Vanir, both sides exchanged hostages. The Æsir sent Hœnir and Mímir, the Vanir sent Njord and Freyr. Hœnir was made a chieftain and looked the part, but whenever Mímir was not beside him he answered every question with: "Let others decide." The Vanir felt cheated, beheaded Mímir and sent the head to the Æsir.

Odin took the head, preserved it with herbs so that it would not rot, and sang charms over it. So it gained the power of speech and told him secrets. It is an eerily precise description of consulting the dead.

The Völuspá takes up the motif in stanza 46: when Ragnarök begins, Odin speaks with Mimir's head, seeking counsel before the last battle. More on the end of the gods in our article on [Ragnarök](/en/blog/ragnarok/).

## Myth or fact? Four common errors

Several claims about Mimir circulate that the sources do not support:

- **"Odin gave his eye at the world tree."** Wrongly merged. The eye is a pledge in the well; the runes came from hanging on the tree (Hávamál).
- **"Mimir was an Æsir god."** The sources never say so outright. In Ynglinga saga he is a wise man among the Æsir; his well lies among the giants.
- **"The well gave immortality."** Attested nowhere. It gives wisdom and understanding, not eternal life. Idun's apples handle the gods' youth.
- **"Mimir was Odin's uncle."** A possible reading of Hávamál 140, but the name is not given there.

The Gjallarhorn from which Mimir drinks according to Snorri is, incidentally, the same horn Heimdall blows at Ragnarök. The Völuspá has Heimdall's horn "hidden under the holy tree", which makes Snorri's picture of a drinking horn at the well plausible without proving it.

## Mimir in the Viking Age: what do we know about cult and belief?

An honest answer: very little. No secure finds are assigned to Mimir, no place names clearly carry him, and no inscription gives his name. We have literary texts from the thirteenth century that preserve older poetry. That does not rule out an older idea of a well of wisdom, but it forbids any detailed reconstruction of a cult. Anyone claiming Vikings "sacrificed to Mimir" at particular springs goes beyond the evidence.

What can be said: the motif of knowledge that must be paid for runs through Norse poetry. The skalds knew their art as mead from the gods' vessels, and in these texts Odin is always the god who searches, trades, and never has enough.

## Our assessment

It is striking that the god who wants to know everything never appears as the owner of knowledge, only as a customer. He pays with an eye, takes advice from a head, hangs on a tree. In these texts wisdom is not a trait but a price. Our reading: the Mimir stories say less about magic than about the value of experience and counsel, which is what keeps them timeless.

## FAQ

### What is Mimir's Well?

A spring of wisdom under a root of the world tree Yggdrasil, among the giants. Whoever drinks from it gains knowledge. Mimir drinks from it daily.

### Why did Odin sacrifice his eye?

As a pledge for a drink from the well. According to the Völuspá the eye still lies there. The sacrifice on the tree, hanging pierced by a spear (Hávamál), is a different story.

### Who is Mimir?

A wise being of the mythology; in Ynglinga saga an Æsir hostage among the Vanir, who beheaded him. Odin preserved the head, which then kept speaking.

### Is Mimir a god or a giant?

The sources do not settle it. He stands among both Æsir and giants, and his well is in giant territory. A clean label is a modern need.

### When does Odin consult Mimir's head?

According to the Völuspá at the beginning of Ragnarök, before the gods ride to the last battle.
